---
title: Bicipital tenosynovitis
summary: Inflammation of the tendon of origin of the biceps brachii and its synovial sheath in the intertubercular groove of the humerus. A common cause of chronic, intermittent thoracic limb lameness in middle-aged active dogs.
---

## What it is

Bicipital tenosynovitis is inflammation of the **tendon of origin of the biceps brachii** and of the synovial sheath that surrounds it. It usually results from **repetitive strain** or partial tears of the tendon, and the tendon may become mineralised. It may also be secondary to other shoulder diseases, such as osteochondral fragments from osteochondritis dissecans that lodge in the sheath, or to enlargement of the supraspinatus tendon nearby, which impinges on the sheath.

## Who it affects

Active, middle-aged to older dogs of large breeds.

## Anatomy involved

The **biceps brachii** (*m. biceps brachii*) arises from the **supraglenoid tubercle of the scapula**. Its tendon crosses the shoulder joint and runs distally in the **intertubercular groove** of the humerus, between the greater and lesser tubercles. The tendon of origin lies inside the shoulder joint, so diseases of the joint and of the tendon are closely linked. The muscle inserts on the radial and ulnar tuberosities; it flexes the elbow and extends the shoulder.

## Clinical signs

Chronic, intermittent thoracic limb lameness, often subtle, that worsens after exercise. The key finding is **pain on the biceps test**: flexing the shoulder while the elbow is extended and pressing directly on the tendon in the intertubercular groove. Muscle atrophy may develop in long-standing cases.

## Diagnosis

- Orthopaedic examination with the biceps test.
- **Radiographs** of the shoulder: osteophytes of the groove, mineralisation within the tendon or sheath.
- **Ultrasound**, arthrography, MRI or **arthroscopy** to assess the tendon and sheath directly; joint fluid analysis.

## Treatment

**Conservative treatment** first: rest and controlled exercise, NSAIDs and physiotherapy; an intra-articular or peritendinous injection of a corticosteroid is used in more severe cases. Dogs that do not respond, or that have a substantial tear, are treated surgically by **biceps tenotomy**, often arthroscopic, which relieves pain, or by **tenodesis**, which reattaches the tendon to the proximal humerus. Prognosis is good with either approach.
