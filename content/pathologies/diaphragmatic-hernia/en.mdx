title: Diaphragmatic hernia
summary: Displacement of abdominal organs into the thoracic cavity through a defect in the diaphragm, usually a tear caused by blunt trauma. It compromises breathing and may only be found long after the accident, as a chronic hernia.
---

## What it is

A diaphragmatic hernia is the passage of abdominal organs into the thorax through a defect in the diaphragm. In the dog most are **traumatic**: blunt trauma, typically a vehicle accident, tears the diaphragm. Abdominal organs such as the **stomach** or loops of **intestine** move into the thorax; the herniated organs, together with any pleural effusion, reduce lung expansion, and the more viscera herniate, the worse the dyspnoea.

The main congenital form is the **peritoneopericardial diaphragmatic hernia**, in which the abdominal cavity communicates with the pericardial sac; it is a different condition, often found incidentally, and the Weimaraner is overrepresented.

## Who it affects

Dogs of any age and breed after blunt trauma. Some are diagnosed only later, as chronic hernias, in which general signs such as weight loss may be more evident than respiratory signs.

## Anatomy involved

The **diaphragm** (*diaphragma*) is a dome-shaped musculotendinous sheet between the thoracic and abdominal cavities. Its centre is the **central tendon** (*centrum tendineum*), and its muscular periphery has a **lumbar part** (the right and left crura), a **costal part** and a **sternal part**. It has three openings: the aortic hiatus, the oesophageal hiatus and the foramen of the caudal vena cava. It is innervated by the **phrenic nerves**, from the cervical plexus. Its contraction expands the lungs, so a torn diaphragm impairs breathing.

## Clinical signs

Dyspnoea and tachypnoea, ranging from barely detectable to life-threatening, cyanotic mucous membranes in severe cases, muffled heart and lung sounds or absent lung sounds, and gut sounds heard over the thorax. In chronic hernias, general signs such as weight loss may be more evident than respiratory signs. A herniated stomach that fills with gas is a life-threatening emergency.

## Diagnosis

- **Thoracic radiographs**: loss of the diaphragmatic outline, gas-filled bowel or stomach in the thorax, displacement of viscera from the abdomen. Pleural effusion can make them hard to interpret.
- **Ultrasound** is often useful.
- Positive-contrast gastrointestinal study, **CT** or MRI in unclear cases; sometimes the diagnosis is only made at surgery.

## Treatment

**Surgical repair** is the definitive treatment: the organs are returned to the abdomen, the defect is sutured and residual air is removed from the pleural space (by thoracocentesis or a chest drain). The patient is stabilised first (oxygen, analgesia, intravenous fluids, thoracocentesis if there is effusion or pneumothorax), but surgery must not be unduly delayed; a herniated stomach that bloats can make the animal deteriorate very quickly. In chronic hernias anaesthesia needs special care, because re-expansion pulmonary oedema is more likely after repair.

## In the cat

**Traumatic** diaphragmatic hernias are common in cats after road traffic accidents, with the same consequences as in the dog; some are repaired as chronic hernias, long after the trauma. Dyspnoeic cats must be handled very carefully and stabilised with oxygen before any test.

In the cat, congenital hernias are more common than in the dog, and the most important is the **peritoneopericardial diaphragmatic hernia**. Long-haired cats, such as the **domestic longhair**, **Himalayan** and **Maine Coon**, are overrepresented. Many are found incidentally on radiographs taken for other reasons: an enlarged cardiac silhouette that overlaps the diaphragm, and sometimes intestine within the pericardial sac; ultrasound helps confirm the diagnosis. Cats with digestive or respiratory signs undergo surgery, while cats without signs can do well without it.

## Key studies in the cat

### Traumatic hernia

- **Schmiedt et al. (2003)**, 34 cats treated surgically. Mean age was 3.6 years and 50% had postoperative complications, mostly tachypnoea and dyspnoea. Mortality was associated with older age, concurrent injuries and, strikingly, lower respiratory rates on admission; it was not associated with how long the hernia had been present.
- **De Bastiani et al. (2023)**, 90 cats repaired with intraoperative transdiaphragmatic thoracocentesis and no chest drain. Intra- and postoperative complication rates were 3.3% and 12.4%, and survival was 93.3%. Comorbidities, emergency surgery and older age were associated with worse long-term survival. There is no comparison group.

### Peritoneopericardial hernia

- **Reimer et al. (2004)**, 67 cats. Domestic longhair and Himalayan cats were overrepresented, and in 27 cats the hernia was an incidental finding. Of 66 cats treated, 37 had surgery (postoperative mortality 14%, with mostly minor complications) and 29 were managed conservatively; in 2 of 22 conservatively managed cats, signs progressed to need surgery or caused death.
- **Banz and Gottfried (2010)**, 31 cats and 8 dogs treated surgically. Long-haired cats, particularly Maine Coons, appeared to be at higher risk, and respiratory signs predominated in cats. The liver, gallbladder and small intestine were the organs most often herniated, and perioperative mortality was low.
- **Burns et al. (2013)**, 30 cats and 28 dogs from two hospitals. Prevalence in cats was 0.062%, and half of the cats were incidental findings. Surgery resolved the signs in 85.3% of the animals operated on, and long-term survival did not differ between surgical and conservative treatment. Treatment was chosen according to signs, not at random.
