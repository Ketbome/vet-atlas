## What it is

A diaphragmatic hernia is the passage of abdominal organs into the thorax through a defect in the diaphragm.

In the dog most are **traumatic**. Blunt trauma, typically a vehicle accident, tears the diaphragm, and the stomach or loops of intestine move into the thorax.

The herniated organs, together with any pleural effusion, reduce lung expansion. The more viscera herniate, the worse the dyspnoea.

The main congenital form is the **peritoneopericardial diaphragmatic hernia**, in which the abdominal cavity communicates with the pericardial sac. It is a different condition, often an incidental finding, and the Weimaraner is overrepresented.

## Who it affects

Dogs of any age and breed after blunt trauma. Some are diagnosed only later, as chronic hernias.

## Anatomy involved

The **diaphragm** (*diaphragma*) is a dome-shaped musculotendinous sheet between the thoracic and abdominal cavities.

- Its centre is the **central tendon** (*centrum tendineum*).
- Its muscular periphery has a lumbar part, with the right and left crura, a costal part and a sternal part.
- It has three openings: the aortic hiatus, the oesophageal hiatus and the foramen of the caudal vena cava.
- It is innervated by the **phrenic nerves**, from the cervical plexus.

Its contraction expands the lungs, so a torn diaphragm impairs breathing.

## Clinical signs

- dyspnoea and tachypnoea, from barely detectable to life-threatening
- cyanotic mucous membranes in severe cases
- muffled heart and lung sounds, or absent lung sounds
- gut sounds heard over the thorax

In chronic hernias, general signs such as weight loss may be more evident than respiratory signs.

A **herniated stomach** that fills with gas is a life-threatening emergency.

## Diagnosis

- **Thoracic radiographs**: loss of the diaphragmatic outline, gas-filled bowel or stomach in the thorax and displacement of viscera from the abdomen. Pleural effusion can make them hard to interpret.
- Ultrasound is often useful.
- In unclear cases, a positive-contrast gastrointestinal study, CT or MRI.

Sometimes the diagnosis is only made at surgery.

## Treatment

**Surgical repair** is the definitive treatment. The organs are returned to the abdomen, the defect is sutured and residual air is removed from the pleural space, by thoracocentesis or a chest drain.

The patient is stabilised first with:

- oxygen
- analgesia
- intravenous fluids
- thoracocentesis, if there is effusion or pneumothorax

Surgery must not be unduly delayed. A herniated stomach that bloats can make the animal deteriorate very quickly.

In chronic hernias anaesthesia needs special care, because **re-expansion pulmonary oedema** is more likely after repair.

<summary>In the cat</summary>

### Traumatic hernia

Traumatic diaphragmatic hernias are common in cats after road traffic accidents, with the same consequences as in the dog. Some are repaired as chronic hernias, long after the trauma.

Dyspnoeic cats are handled very carefully and stabilised with oxygen before any test.

### Peritoneopericardial hernia

In the cat congenital hernias are more common than in the dog, and the most important is the **peritoneopericardial diaphragmatic hernia**. Long-haired cats, such as the domestic longhair, Himalayan and Maine Coon, are overrepresented.

Many are found incidentally on radiographs taken for other reasons. They show an enlarged cardiac silhouette that overlaps the diaphragm, and sometimes intestine within the pericardial sac. Ultrasound helps confirm the diagnosis.

Cats with digestive or respiratory signs undergo surgery. Cats without signs can do well without it.

<summary>Key studies in the cat</summary>

### Traumatic hernia

- **Schmiedt et al. (2003).** Study of 34 cats treated surgically, with a mean age of 3.6 years. Postoperative complications occurred in 50%, mostly tachypnoea and dyspnoea. Mortality was associated with older age, concurrent injuries and, strikingly, lower respiratory rates on admission; it was not associated with how long the hernia had been present.
- **De Bastiani et al. (2023).** Study of 90 cats repaired with intraoperative transdiaphragmatic thoracocentesis and no chest drain. Intraoperative complications were 3.3% and postoperative complications 12.4%, and survival was 93.3%. Comorbidities, emergency surgery and older age were associated with worse long-term survival. There is no comparison group.

### Peritoneopericardial hernia

- **Reimer et al. (2004).** Study of 67 cats. Domestic longhair and Himalayan cats were overrepresented, and in 27 cats the hernia was an incidental finding. Of 66 cats treated, 37 had surgery, with a postoperative mortality of 14% and mostly minor complications, and 29 were managed conservatively. In 2 of 22 conservatively managed cats, signs progressed to need surgery or caused death.
- **Banz and Gottfried (2010).** Study of 31 cats and 8 dogs treated surgically. Long-haired cats, particularly Maine Coons, appeared to be at higher risk, and respiratory signs predominated in cats. The liver, gallbladder and small intestine were the organs most often herniated. Perioperative mortality was low.
- **Burns et al. (2013).** Study of 30 cats and 28 dogs from two hospitals. Prevalence in cats was 0.062%, and half of the cats were incidental findings. Surgery resolved the signs in 85.3% of the animals operated on, and long-term survival did not differ between surgical and conservative treatment. Treatment was chosen according to signs, not at random.
