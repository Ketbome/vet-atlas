## What it is

A corneal ulcer is a defect of the corneal epithelium that exposes the underlying stroma.

Ulcers are classified by depth and behaviour:

- In a **superficial** ulcer only the epithelium is lost. Uncomplicated ulcers heal within about a week.
- In a **stromal** ulcer part of the stroma is lost. Deep ulcers threaten the integrity of the eye.
- In a **descemetocele** the whole stroma is lost and only Descemet's membrane remains. Perforation is imminent.
- In a **perforated** ulcer the full thickness of the cornea is breached.
- In a **melting ulcer** (keratomalacia), proteases from bacteria and from neutrophils digest the stroma, which softens. It can progress rapidly to perforation.
- The **spontaneous chronic corneal epithelial defect**, or "indolent" or "boxer" ulcer, is a superficial ulcer that fails to heal. The new epithelium does not adhere properly to the stroma because of a basement membrane defect, leaving a loose lip of epithelium at its edge.

Its causes are:

- trauma and foreign bodies
- eyelid and eyelash disorders: entropion, distichiasis, ectopic cilia
- keratoconjunctivitis sicca
- exposure from incomplete eyelid closure
- facial nerve paralysis
- infection

## Who it affects

Dogs of any breed.

**Brachycephalic breeds** (pug, shih tzu, French and English bulldog, Pekingese) are at high risk because of their prominent eyes, incomplete blinking, nasal folds and reduced corneal sensitivity.

Indolent ulcers are typical of middle-aged and older dogs, with the **boxer** as the classic breed.

## Anatomy involved

The canine **cornea** is the transparent front part of the outer fibrous tunic of the eye. From front to back it has four layers:

- the anterior epithelium
- the collagenous **stroma**, which makes up most of its thickness
- **Descemet's membrane**
- the endothelium

It has no blood vessels and is richly innervated, so ulcers are very painful.

Ulcers can also trigger a **reflex uveitis**, with miosis and ciliary spasm.

## Clinical signs

Blepharospasm, epiphora, rubbing of the eye, conjunctival hyperaemia, corneal oedema around the lesion and miosis.

Deep or infected ulcers may show a crater, a dense white infiltrate or a soft, melting stroma, and corneal vascularisation.

## Diagnosis

- On **fluorescein stain**, the hydrophilic stroma retains the dye. The intact epithelium and Descemet's membrane do not. A descemetocele shows a clear centre with stained walls.
- Depth is assessed with magnification or a slit lamp.
- Deep, melting or infected ulcers undergo **cytology and culture**.
- The cause is sought: eyelids, eyelashes, foreign bodies under the third eyelid and the Schirmer tear test.

## Treatment

### Superficial ulcers

- remove the cause
- topical broad-spectrum antibiotic
- topical atropine for the pain of reflex uveitis
- an Elizabethan collar
- systemic analgesics

They are reassessed within a few days.

### Indolent ulcers

The loose epithelium is debrided, often followed by grid keratotomy or diamond-burr debridement.

### Melting ulcers

Intensive antibiotic therapy based on cytology and culture, plus anti-collagenase agents such as autologous serum.

### Deep ulcers, descemetoceles and perforations

They need surgery: conjunctival pedicle grafts, corneoconjunctival transposition or other grafts.

**Topical corticosteroids are contraindicated** in any ulcer.

<details>
<summary>In the cat</summary>

In the cat the most important cause of corneal ulceration is **feline herpesvirus type 1**. It produces very characteristic **dendritic** ulcers, linear and branching, often recurrent.

Other causes are foreign bodies, eyelid defects such as entropion and the exposed cornea of brachycephalic breeds.

The feline cornea has peculiarities that change management.

### Corneal sequestrum

A chronic ulcer can progress to a plaque of necrotic stroma, brown to black. It is typical of the cat, especially the Persian and other brachycephalic breeds, and is associated with herpesvirus.

### Treatment

- **Grid and punctate keratotomy must not be performed** in cats, because they promote sequestrum formation. Non-healing superficial ulcers are treated with cotton-swab debridement or superficial keratectomy.
- **Antivirals** (oral famciclovir, topical cidofovir or ganciclovir) are added for ulcers suspected to be herpetic, and corticosteroids are avoided.
- Melting ulcers are treated as intensively as in the dog.

<details>
<summary>Key studies in the cat</summary>

- **La Croix et al. (2001).** Retrospective study of 29 cats with 36 non-healing ulcers. Adding grid keratotomy to debridement did not shorten healing (mean 42 versus 30 days). A sequestrum formed in 4 of 13 eyes with grid keratotomy compared with 2 of 21 after debridement alone. A small study that supports the advice against keratotomy in cats.
- **Goldreich et al. (2020).** 81 cats with culture-confirmed bacterial keratitis. *Staphylococcus* species made up 55% of the 102 isolates. Susceptibility was highest to ofloxacin (100%), ciprofloxacin (94%), chloramphenicol (93%) and doxycycline (92%), and very low to polymyxin B (2%) and bacitracin (15%).
- **Multari et al. (2021).** 172 cats (175 eyes) operated on for corneal sequestrum, 71.5% of them Persians and 12.2% Exotic Shorthairs. After keratectomy combined with various grafts or flaps, all eyes kept their vision, 86% had no or mild corneal opacity, and the sequestrum recurred in 11.4% within 2 years.
- **Mironovich et al. (2023).** Placebo-controlled study in 132 shelter cats with FHV-1 treated for 7 days. None of the corneal ulcers in the placebo group healed, whereas 77% healed in cats given an antiviral.
- **Frejlich and Payen (2026).** 392 referral cats with ulcerative keratitis or corneal sequestrum. Ulcerative keratitis was diagnosed in 36% of brachycephalic cats seen and 15% of non-brachycephalic cats. Brachycephalic cats had more central ulcers and more sequestra, but not more deep or perforated ulcers.
- **Reynolds et al. (2026).** Retrospective study of 71 spontaneous superficial ulcers in 68 cats. A sequestrum developed in 28%, and was more likely after cotton-tipped swab debridement (odds ratio 4.1). Oral lysine, topical NSAIDs and topical EDTA were associated with delayed healing. These are retrospective associations, not proof of cause.

</details>
