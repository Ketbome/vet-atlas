## What it is

In gastric dilatation-volvulus (GDV) the stomach fills with gas and fluid (dilatation) and rotates on its axis (volvulus). Neither the cardia nor the pylorus can empty it.

The distended stomach compresses the **caudal vena cava** and the **portal vein**. This reduces venous return and cardiac output, causing obstructive and hypovolaemic shock.

The gastric wall becomes ischaemic and may necrose and perforate.

The course is complicated by:

- reperfusion injury
- ventricular arrhythmias
- disseminated intravascular coagulation
- endotoxaemia

## Who it affects

Large and giant breeds with a deep, narrow thorax:

- Great Dane
- Saint Bernard
- Weimaraner
- Irish setter and Gordon setter
- standard poodle
- German shepherd
- basset hound

Risk increases with age, with a first-degree relative that had GDV, with a nervous temperament and with eating a single large meal a day quickly.

## Anatomy involved

### Stomach

Seen from the caudal aspect, it usually rotates **clockwise**, 90° to 360°, around the distal oesophagus. The **pylorus** moves ventrally and cranially and then to the left of the midline, trapping the duodenum between the oesophagus and the stomach.

### Spleen

It is attached to the greater curvature by the gastrosplenic ligament. It is dragged along and often trapped. It may enlarge or become ischaemic and thrombosed.

### Caudal vena cava and portal vein

They are compressed by the distended stomach.

### Oesophagus

A rotation of more than 180° occludes the distal oesophagus, so passing a stomach tube may be difficult.

## Clinical signs

Onset is acute, with:

- unproductive retching
- restlessness
- hypersalivation
- progressive cranial abdominal distension, with a tympanic sound on percussion

Signs of shock follow: tachycardia, weak pulses, pale mucous membranes, prolonged capillary refill time, tachypnoea and collapse.

**Ventricular arrhythmias** are common, sometimes in the first days after surgery.

## Diagnosis

- A large-breed dog with retching and abdominal distension has GDV until proven otherwise.
- On the **right lateral abdominal radiograph**, the gas-filled pylorus is displaced craniodorsally and separated from the fundus by a soft-tissue band. This is the "double bubble" or "Popeye arm" sign. Free gas suggests perforation.
- Lactate, electrolytes, acid–base and coagulation guide treatment and prognosis. A **lactate above 6 mmol/L** is associated with gastric necrosis.
- An electrocardiogram detects ventricular arrhythmias.

## Treatment

It is a surgical emergency.

### Stabilisation

First, shock is treated with intravenous fluids through cephalic veins. The stomach is decompressed by orogastric intubation or percutaneous trocarisation.

### Surgery

As soon as the patient is stable:

- the stomach is repositioned
- the viability of the gastric wall and spleen is assessed
- necrotic gastric wall is resected and a splenectomy is done if needed
- a **gastropexy**, which fixes the pyloric antrum to the right abdominal wall, is made to prevent recurrence

### Prevention

**Prophylactic gastropexy** is advised in high-risk breeds, often at the time of neutering.
