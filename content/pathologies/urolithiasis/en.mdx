## What it is

Uroliths form when urine becomes supersaturated with stone-forming minerals that precipitate as crystals and grow into stones.

The type depends on urine composition and pH, diet, genetics and infection.

### Types

- **Struvite** (magnesium ammonium phosphate) in dogs is usually **infection-induced**, by urease-producing bacteria (*Staphylococcus*, *Proteus*) that alkalinise the urine. It can be dissolved medically.
- **Calcium oxalate** occurs in middle-aged to older dogs, mainly of small breeds. It cannot be dissolved.
- **Urate** occurs in Dalmatians, some bulldogs and other breeds with a genetic defect in uric acid transport, and in dogs with portosystemic shunts.
- **Cystine** occurs in dogs with inherited cystinuria, mainly intact males of certain breeds.
- Calcium phosphate, silica and xanthine are less common, xanthine for example after allopurinol.

## Who it affects

- Struvite: dogs with urinary tract infection by urease-producing bacteria.
- Calcium oxalate: miniature schnauzer, Lhasa apso, Yorkshire terrier, bichon frise, shih tzu, Chihuahua and other small breeds.
- Urate: Dalmatian, English bulldog, Black Russian terrier, and dogs with portosystemic shunts.
- Cystine: English and French bulldog, Newfoundland, mastiffs and others.

## Anatomy involved

The **urinary bladder** is the most common site (cystolithiasis). Stones irritate the mucosa and predispose to infection.

Stones can lodge in the **urethra**, especially in males, and cause obstruction.

Nephroliths and ureteroliths are less common in dogs. They can obstruct the ureter and cause hydronephrosis and pyelonephritis.

## Clinical signs

- haematuria, pollakiuria, stranguria and dysuria
- urination in inappropriate places

Some stones are found incidentally.

**Urethral obstruction** is an emergency. There is unproductive straining and a large, painful bladder. After about 24 hours, post-renal azotaemia, hyperkalaemia, vomiting and depression follow.

## Diagnosis

- **Abdominal radiographs** must include the whole urethra. Struvite, calcium oxalate and calcium phosphate stones are radiopaque; urate and cystine stones are often **radiolucent**.
- Ultrasound detects radiolucent stones and evaluates the kidneys and ureters.
- Urinalysis and urine culture show pH, crystals and infection. Crystals suggest the stone type but do not confirm it.
- **Quantitative stone analysis** of a retrieved urolith is the only way to know its composition.

## Treatment

Urethral obstruction is relieved first, by retrograde urohydropropulsion of the stone back into the bladder or catheterisation, while correcting fluid and electrolyte disorders.

Following the ACVIM consensus, **medical dissolution** is attempted for:

- struvite, with a calculolytic diet plus antibiotics when infection is present
- urate
- cystine

Stones that cannot be dissolved are removed by the least invasive method available:

- voiding urohydropropulsion
- cystoscopic basket retrieval or laser lithotripsy
- percutaneous cystolithotomy
- cystotomy, if none of these is available

Prevention depends on stone type: diet, increased water intake, urine pH management and regular monitoring.

<summary>In the cat</summary>

In the cat the two main types are also **struvite** and **calcium oxalate**, but with important differences.

### Types

- Unlike in the dog, feline **struvite** is usually **sterile**, related to concentrated, alkaline urine rather than infection. It can be dissolved with a calculolytic diet, usually within a few weeks.
- **Calcium oxalate** makes up about half of feline uroliths. Longhaired cats are predisposed and hypercalcaemia is a risk factor. It cannot be dissolved. It makes up most nephroliths.
- Urate makes up about 6% of feline uroliths. It is occasionally linked to portovascular anomalies, but the cause is usually unknown.
- Solidified blood calculi are occasionally reported.

### Ureteral obstruction

Ureteral obstruction by **calcium oxalate ureteroliths** is an important problem in cats. Ureteral strictures are also found in more than 25% of obstructed cats. It can cause hydronephrosis and acute kidney injury.

It is diagnosed with ultrasound together with radiographs. It is treated with fluid diuresis for 24–72 hours and, if it does not resolve, with ureteral stents, a **subcutaneous ureteral bypass device** or surgery.

### Urethral obstruction and prevention

In male cats, small uroliths and urethral plugs cause urethral obstruction.

Prevention relies mainly on **increasing water intake**, with wet food and fountains, and on diets suited to the stone type.
