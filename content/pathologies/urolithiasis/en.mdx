---
title: Urolithiasis
summary: Formation of stones (uroliths) in the urinary tract, most often in the bladder. In dogs struvite and calcium oxalate predominate; stones cause cystitis signs and can obstruct the urethra.
---

## What it is

Uroliths form when urine becomes supersaturated with stone-forming minerals that precipitate as crystals and grow into stones. The type depends on urine composition and pH, diet, genetics and infection:

- **Struvite (magnesium ammonium phosphate)**: in dogs usually **infection-induced**, by urease-producing bacteria (*Staphylococcus*, *Proteus*) that alkalinise the urine. Can be dissolved medically.
- **Calcium oxalate**: in older dogs of small breeds, more often males; it cannot be dissolved.
- **Urate**: in Dalmatians (and some bulldogs and other breeds) with a genetic defect in uric acid transport, and in dogs with portosystemic shunts.
- **Cystine**: in dogs with inherited cystinuria, mainly intact males of certain breeds.
- Less common: calcium phosphate, silica and xanthine (for example after allopurinol).

## Who it affects

Struvite: more often females, because of their predisposition to urinary tract infection. Calcium oxalate: miniature schnauzer, Lhasa apso, Yorkshire terrier, bichon frise, shih tzu, miniature poodle. Urate: Dalmatian, English bulldog, and small breeds with shunts. Cystine: English and French bulldog, Newfoundland, dachshund, mastiffs and others.

## Anatomy involved

- **Urinary bladder**: the most common site (cystolithiasis); stones irritate the mucosa and predispose to infection.
- **Urethra**: stones lodge where the lumen narrows, typically just caudal to the os penis in males, causing obstruction.
- **Kidneys and ureters**: nephroliths and ureteroliths are less common in dogs; they can obstruct the ureter and cause hydronephrosis and pyelonephritis.

## Clinical signs

Haematuria, pollakiuria, stranguria and dysuria; urinary incontinence or inappropriate urination. Some stones are found incidentally. **Urethral obstruction** is an emergency: unproductive straining, a large painful bladder and, after 24–48 hours, post-renal azotaemia, hyperkalaemia, vomiting and depression.

## Diagnosis

- **Abdominal radiographs** including the whole urethra: struvite, calcium oxalate and calcium phosphate stones are radiopaque; urate and cystine stones are often radiolucent.
- **Ultrasound**: detects radiolucent stones and evaluates the kidneys and ureters.
- **Urinalysis and urine culture**: pH, crystals (suggestive, not definitive of stone type) and infection.
- **Quantitative stone analysis** of a retrieved urolith is the only way to know its composition.

## Treatment

Urethral obstruction is relieved first, by retrograde urohydropropulsion of the stone back into the bladder or catheterisation, while correcting fluid and electrolyte disorders. Following the ACVIM consensus, **medical dissolution** is attempted for struvite (calculolytic diet plus antibiotics when infection is present), urate and cystine stones. Stones that cannot be dissolved are removed by the least invasive method available: voiding urohydropropulsion, cystoscopic basket retrieval or laser lithotripsy, percutaneous cystolithotomy or, if not available, cystotomy. Prevention depends on stone type: diet, increased water intake, urine pH management and regular monitoring.
