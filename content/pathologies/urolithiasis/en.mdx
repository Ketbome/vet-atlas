---
title: Urolithiasis
summary: Formation of stones (uroliths) in the urinary tract, most often in the bladder. In dogs struvite and calcium oxalate predominate; stones cause cystitis signs and can obstruct the urethra. In the cat the same two types predominate, but struvite is usually sterile and calcium oxalate ureteroliths are common.
---

## What it is

Uroliths form when urine becomes supersaturated with stone-forming minerals that precipitate as crystals and grow into stones. The type depends on urine composition and pH, diet, genetics and infection:

- **Struvite (magnesium ammonium phosphate)**: in dogs usually **infection-induced**, by urease-producing bacteria (*Staphylococcus*, *Proteus*) that alkalinise the urine. Can be dissolved medically.
- **Calcium oxalate**: in middle-aged to older dogs, mainly of small breeds; it cannot be dissolved.
- **Urate**: in Dalmatians (and some bulldogs and other breeds) with a genetic defect in uric acid transport, and in dogs with portosystemic shunts.
- **Cystine**: in dogs with inherited cystinuria, mainly intact males of certain breeds.
- Less common: calcium phosphate, silica and xanthine (for example after allopurinol).

## Who it affects

Struvite: dogs with urinary tract infection by urease-producing bacteria. Calcium oxalate: miniature schnauzer, Lhasa apso, Yorkshire terrier, bichon frise, shih tzu, Chihuahua and other small breeds. Urate: Dalmatian, English bulldog, Black Russian terrier, and dogs with portosystemic shunts. Cystine: English and French bulldog, Newfoundland, mastiffs and others.

## Anatomy involved

- **Urinary bladder**: the most common site (cystolithiasis); stones irritate the mucosa and predispose to infection.
- **Urethra**: stones can lodge in it, especially in males, causing obstruction.
- **Kidneys and ureters**: nephroliths and ureteroliths are less common in dogs; they can obstruct the ureter and cause hydronephrosis and pyelonephritis.

## Clinical signs

Haematuria, pollakiuria, stranguria and dysuria; urination in inappropriate places. Some stones are found incidentally. **Urethral obstruction** is an emergency: unproductive straining, a large painful bladder and, after about 24 hours, post-renal azotaemia, hyperkalaemia, vomiting and depression.

## Diagnosis

- **Abdominal radiographs** including the whole urethra: struvite, calcium oxalate and calcium phosphate stones are radiopaque; urate and cystine stones are often radiolucent.
- **Ultrasound**: detects radiolucent stones and evaluates the kidneys and ureters.
- **Urinalysis and urine culture**: pH, crystals (suggestive, not definitive of stone type) and infection.
- **Quantitative stone analysis** of a retrieved urolith is the only way to know its composition.

## Treatment

Urethral obstruction is relieved first, by retrograde urohydropropulsion of the stone back into the bladder or catheterisation, while correcting fluid and electrolyte disorders. Following the ACVIM consensus, **medical dissolution** is attempted for struvite (calculolytic diet plus antibiotics when infection is present), urate and cystine stones. Stones that cannot be dissolved are removed by the least invasive method available: voiding urohydropropulsion, cystoscopic basket retrieval or laser lithotripsy, percutaneous cystolithotomy or, if not available, cystotomy. Prevention depends on stone type: diet, increased water intake, urine pH management and regular monitoring.

## In the cat

In the cat the two main types are also **struvite** and **calcium oxalate**, but with important differences:

- **Struvite**: unlike in the dog, in the cat it is usually **sterile**, related to concentrated, alkaline urine rather than infection. It can be **dissolved** with a calculolytic diet, usually within a few weeks.
- **Calcium oxalate**: about half of feline uroliths; longhaired cats are predisposed and hypercalcaemia is a risk factor; it cannot be dissolved. It makes up most **nephroliths** (upper urinary tract).
- **Urate**: about 6% of feline uroliths; occasionally linked to portovascular anomalies, but the cause is usually unknown. **Solidified blood calculi** are occasionally reported.

**Ureteral obstruction** by calcium oxalate ureteroliths is an important problem in cats, and ureteral strictures are also found in more than 25% of obstructed cats. It can cause hydronephrosis and acute kidney injury. It is diagnosed with ultrasound together with radiographs and treated with fluid diuresis for 24–72 hours and, if it does not resolve, with **ureteral stents**, a **subcutaneous ureteral bypass device** or surgery. In male cats, small uroliths and urethral plugs cause **urethral obstruction**. Prevention relies mainly on **increasing water intake** (wet food, fountains) and on diets suited to the stone type.

## Key studies

- **ACVIM consensus on uroliths in dogs and cats (Lulich et al., 2016)**: recommendations for treating and preventing uroliths by type. It covers medical dissolution of struvite and minimally invasive removal of stones that do not dissolve.
- **iCatCare consensus (Taylor et al., 2025)**: the current guideline for feline lower urinary tract diseases, uroliths included.
