---
title: Benign prostatic hyperplasia
summary: Androgen-dependent, age-related enlargement of the prostate in intact male dogs. It is the most common prostatic disorder; when it causes signs, these are mainly haematuria, haemospermia, bloody preputial discharge or straining to defecate.
---

## What it is

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a spontaneous increase in the number (hyperplasia) and size (hypertrophy) of prostatic epithelial cells, driven by dihydrotestosterone produced from testosterone by 5α-reductase within the gland. It begins as glandular hyperplasia and progresses to a cystic form, with small intraparenchymal cysts. The enlargement is symmetrical and the gland stays non-painful. BPH may also predispose to prostatitis.

## Who it affects

Intact male dogs. Glandular hyperplasia can begin as early as 2.5 years of age and cystic hyperplasia tends to develop after 4 years; it is found in almost all intact dogs over 6 years old, but many have no signs.

## Anatomy involved

- **Prostate (*prostata*)**: it lies within the pelvis, caudal to the bladder, and the proximal urethra runs through it. As it enlarges it moves cranially and can be found in the caudal abdomen.
- **Rectum**: lies dorsal to the prostate, which is why the gland is palpated per rectum; an enlarged gland can cause constipation and straining to defecate.
- **Urethra**: the prostatic urethra runs through the gland; in a dog with prostatic disease, urethral obstruction strongly suggests neoplasia rather than BPH.

## Clinical signs

Often none, or only haemospermia and/or infertility. When present: persistent or intermittent **haematuria**, haemospermia, **haemorrhagic preputial discharge**, and **tenesmus** (straining to defecate) or constipation. Systemic signs (fever, malaise, abdominal pain) suggest bacterial prostatitis, abscess or neoplasia rather than uncomplicated BPH.

## Diagnosis

- **Rectal palpation**: a symmetrically enlarged, smooth, non-painful prostate.
- **Ultrasound**: the best method to assess size and echotexture; diffuse, relatively symmetrical enlargement, sometimes with increased echogenicity and thin-walled intraparenchymal cysts. It allows guided fine-needle aspiration; definitive diagnosis requires cytology of an aspirate or histology of a biopsy.
- **Radiographs**: confirm prostatomegaly (the normal prostate is not usually visible); areas of mineralisation suggest chronic prostatitis or neoplasia instead.
- **Ejaculate cytology and culture** or prostatic wash: to look for concurrent infection.
- **Canine prostate-specific arginine esterase (CPSE)**: rises in BPH, but also in prostatitis and neoplasia, so it does not tell them apart.

## Treatment

**Castration** is the treatment of choice in dogs not intended for breeding: involution is usually evident within a few weeks and often complete in several months. When the dog is to be kept for breeding, medical treatment with osaterone acetate (an antiandrogen), finasteride (a 5α-reductase inhibitor) or a deslorelin implant reduces prostate size and signs; the effect lasts only as long as treatment does.
