## What it is

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a spontaneous hyperplasia and hypertrophy of the prostatic epithelial cells.

It is driven by **dihydrotestosterone**, produced from testosterone by 5α-reductase within the gland.

It begins as glandular hyperplasia and progresses to a cystic form, with small intraparenchymal cysts.

The enlargement is **symmetrical** and the gland stays non-painful.

BPH may also predispose to prostatitis.

## Who it affects

Intact male dogs.

Glandular hyperplasia can begin as early as 2.5 years of age, and cystic hyperplasia tends to develop after 4 years.

It is found in almost all intact dogs **over 6 years old**, but many have no signs.

## Anatomy involved

The **prostate** (*prostata*) lies within the pelvis, caudal to the bladder, and the proximal urethra runs through it. As it enlarges it moves cranially and can be found in the caudal abdomen.

The **rectum** lies dorsal to the prostate, which is why the gland is palpated per rectum. An enlarged gland can cause constipation and straining to defecate.

The prostatic urethra runs through the gland. In a dog with prostatic disease, **urethral obstruction** strongly suggests neoplasia rather than BPH.

## Clinical signs

Often none, or only haemospermia and/or infertility.

When present:

- persistent or intermittent **haematuria**
- haemospermia
- **haemorrhagic preputial discharge**
- **tenesmus** or constipation

Systemic signs, such as fever, malaise or abdominal pain, suggest bacterial prostatitis, abscess or neoplasia rather than uncomplicated BPH.

## Diagnosis

- On **rectal palpation** the prostate is symmetrically enlarged, smooth and non-painful.
- **Ultrasound** is the best method to assess size and echotexture. It shows diffuse, relatively symmetrical enlargement, sometimes with increased echogenicity and thin-walled intraparenchymal cysts. It allows guided fine-needle aspiration.
- Radiographs confirm prostatomegaly, since the normal prostate is not usually visible. Areas of mineralisation suggest chronic prostatitis or neoplasia instead.
- Cytology and culture of the ejaculate or of a prostatic wash look for concurrent infection.
- Canine prostate-specific arginine esterase (CPSE) rises in BPH, but also in prostatitis and neoplasia, so it does not tell them apart.

Definitive diagnosis requires cytology of an aspirate or histology of a biopsy.

## Treatment

**Castration** is the treatment of choice in dogs not intended for breeding. Involution is usually evident within a few weeks and often complete in several months.

When the dog is to be kept for breeding, medical treatment reduces prostate size and signs. The options are:

- osaterone acetate, an antiandrogen
- finasteride, a 5α-reductase inhibitor
- a deslorelin implant

The effect lasts only as long as treatment does.
