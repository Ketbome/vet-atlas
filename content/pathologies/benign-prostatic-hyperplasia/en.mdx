---
title: Benign prostatic hyperplasia
summary: Androgen-dependent, age-related enlargement of the prostate in intact male dogs. It is the most common prostatic disorder; when it causes signs, the enlarged gland compresses the rectum and causes tenesmus or haematuria.
---

## What it is

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a spontaneous increase in the number (hyperplasia) and size (hypertrophy) of prostatic epithelial cells, driven by dihydrotestosterone produced from testosterone by 5α-reductase within the gland. It begins as glandular hyperplasia and progresses to a cystic form, with small intraparenchymal cysts. The enlargement is symmetrical and the gland stays non-painful. BPH also predisposes to prostatitis and prostatic cysts. It does not occur in dogs castrated before puberty.

## Who it affects

Intact male dogs. Changes begin from early adulthood and are present in most intact males in middle and old age, but many have no signs.

## Anatomy involved

- **Prostate (*prostata*)**: in the dog it is the only accessory sex gland; it surrounds the proximal urethra at the neck of the bladder, at or just cranial to the pelvic brim. As it enlarges it can move cranially into the abdomen.
- **Rectum**: lies dorsal to the prostate; a symmetrical enlargement compresses it from below.
- **Urethra**: the prostatic urethra runs through the gland; unlike in men, urethral obstruction from BPH is uncommon in dogs.

## Clinical signs

Often none. When present: **tenesmus** and thin, ribbon-like faeces from rectal compression; **serosanguineous urethral discharge** or blood dripping from the prepuce independent of urination; **haematuria** and haemospermia. Systemic signs (fever, pain) suggest prostatitis or abscess rather than uncomplicated BPH.

## Diagnosis

- **Rectal palpation**: a symmetrically enlarged, smooth, non-painful prostate.
- **Ultrasound**: diffuse enlargement with uniform or slightly increased echogenicity and small cysts; it excludes abscess and neoplasia and allows guided sampling.
- **Radiographs**: prostatomegaly displacing the bladder cranially and the colon dorsally; mineralisation suggests neoplasia instead.
- **Ejaculate cytology and culture** or prostatic wash: to look for concurrent infection.
- **Canine prostate-specific arginine esterase (CPSE)**: rises with prostatic enlargement and supports the diagnosis.

## Treatment

Dogs without signs do not need treatment. **Castration** is curative: the prostate shrinks markedly within weeks. When the dog is to be kept for breeding, medical treatment with osaterone acetate (an antiandrogen), finasteride (a 5α-reductase inhibitor) or a deslorelin implant reduces prostate size and signs; the effect lasts only as long as treatment does. Prostatitis or cysts, if present, are treated at the same time.
