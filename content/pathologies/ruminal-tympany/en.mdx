## What it is

Ruminal fermentation continuously produces large volumes of gas (carbon dioxide and methane), which are removed by **eructation**.

To eructate, the gas must collect in the dorsal sac of the rumen and the **cardia** must be clear of fluid and foam. Bloat appears when this mechanism fails.

### Frothy (primary) bloat

The gas is trapped in small bubbles within a **stable foam** that fills the rumen and covers the cardia, so the eructation reflex is not triggered.

- On pasture it is caused by **legumes** in the vegetative stage, such as alfalfa (lucerne) and clovers, which are rich in soluble proteins that stabilise the foam.
- In the feedlot it is caused by diets rich in rapidly fermented grain. Bacteria produce a slime that increases the viscosity of the rumen fluid.

### Free-gas (secondary) bloat

The gas separates from the contents, but the animal cannot expel it because of a physical or functional failure of eructation. Causes are:

- **oesophageal obstruction** by foreign bodies, such as tubers or fruit
- external compression of the oesophagus by abscesses or enlarged mediastinal lymph nodes
- **vagal indigestion** from reticular adhesions or abscesses
- tetanus
- hypocalcaemia
- lateral recumbency

### Cause of death

The distended rumen pushes the diaphragm into the thorax and compresses the large abdominal vessels. Death is due to **respiratory failure** and reduced venous return.

## Who it affects

### Pasture frothy bloat

Cattle grazing alfalfa- or clover-dominant pastures, especially lush, rapidly growing pastures in the vegetative or early bud stage.

Bloat often starts on the second or third day on the pasture. Some animals are more susceptible than others.

### Feedlot frothy bloat

Feeder calves and finishing cattle on concentrate-rich, low-fibre rations.

### Free-gas bloat

Animals of any age and system with a cause that prevents eructation. It can be an isolated, recurrent case.

## Anatomy involved

Gas collects in the dorsal sac of the **rumen**, in contact with the left paralumbar fossa. This is why distension is seen first in the **left flank**. In severe cases the whole abdomen is distended.

The **oesophagus** is the exit route for the gas. Obstruction in the neck or thorax prevents eructation and is a common cause of free-gas bloat. It is also the route for passing the stomach tube for diagnosis and treatment.

The cardia opens at the junction of the **reticulum** and the atrium of the rumen. Reticuloruminal contractions, controlled by the **vagus nerve**, move the contents and clear the cardia for eructation. Adhesions and abscesses from traumatic reticuloperitonitis can damage the vagus and cause recurrent bloat.

## Clinical signs

- **distension of the left flank**, which can spread to the whole abdomen
- discomfort and frequent urination
- as it progresses, **dyspnoea**, open-mouth breathing, tongue protrusion and an extended head
- a tympanic sound on percussion of the left flank

In pasture frothy bloat animals may be found **dead** without signs having been seen.

In free-gas bloat distension is often moderate and recurrent.

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis is clinical, from left flank distension and the history: change of pasture, diet, several animals affected.

### Stomach tube

If gas escapes easily and the flank deflates, the bloat is free-gas. If little gas escapes and there is foam, it is frothy. The tube also locates an oesophageal obstruction.

### Underlying cause of free-gas bloat

The oesophagus, the reticulum (foreign body tests, ultrasonography) and the thorax are examined.

### Necropsy

In an animal that died of bloat the **bloat line** is seen in the oesophagus: the cervical portion is congested and the thoracic portion pale. It helps distinguish it from distension occurring after death.

## Treatment and prevention

### Frothy bloat

The animals are removed from the causative pasture or feed and given an oral **antifoaming agent**: poloxalene, vegetable or mineral oils, or detergents.

When death is imminent an **emergency rumenotomy** is performed. A trocar is of little use because the foam blocks it.

### Free-gas bloat

The gas is removed with a **stomach tube** or, if that is not possible, with a **trocar** in the left paralumbar fossa. The cause is then treated, for example by removing the foreign body from the oesophagus.

In recurrent cases a temporary rumen fistula (rumenostomy) can be created.

### Prevention on pasture

- offer hay before turnout
- use strip grazing to restrict intake
- keep grass dominant or use mixed grass-legume pastures
- use **non-bloating legumes**, such as sainfoin or birdsfoot trefoil
- give antifoaming agents daily during the risk season, or ionophores such as monensin where authorised

### Prevention in the feedlot

Enough roughage in the ration, appropriate grain processing and gradual adaptation to concentrate-rich diets.

Drug use in food animals is subject to each country's regulations and to **withdrawal periods** for milk and meat.

<summary>Key studies</summary>

The evidence on bloat is old and limited. It comes mainly from feeding trials with small groups of rumen-fistulated cattle. No controlled trials on the treatment of clinical bloat (stomach tube, trocar, antifoaming agents) were found, so those recommendations rest on clinical experience and reference texts.

- **Majak et al. (1995).** Synthesis of two decades (1973–1993) of trials at a Canadian research station, with rumen-fistulated cattle grazing or fed freshly cut alfalfa. **Every alfalfa cultivar** tested caused bloat, while sainfoin, birdsfoot trefoil and cicer milkvetch (*Astragalus cicer*) did not. Bloat was associated with the soluble protein content of alfalfa, which falls as the plant matures, and not with saponins. Cation supplementation did not prevent it and, of all the additives tested, only **poloxalene prevented it completely**. It is a review of the centre's own trials.
- **Gadberry et al. (2022).** Meta-analysis of the effects of monensin in growing cattle on forage. The bloat analysis included 12 experiments with 23 comparisons. On bloat-prone pastures, monensin reduced incidence by 20% and severity by 0.7 points on the bloat score. It reduces the problem but does not eliminate it.
- **Cheng et al. (1998).** Narrative review of feedlot bloat; it is the standard reference and there are no higher-level studies. It explains that excess rapidly fermented carbohydrate favours acid-tolerant bacteria (*Streptococcus bovis*, *Lactobacillus* spp.), that low pH reduces rumen motility and that bacterial slime stabilises the foam. The amount of roughage, grain processing and cereal type, dietary adaptation and additives such as ionophores influence its occurrence.
