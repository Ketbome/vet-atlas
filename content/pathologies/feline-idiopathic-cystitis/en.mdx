## What it is

Feline idiopathic cystitis (FIC) is a **diagnosis of exclusion**. The cat shows signs of feline lower urinary tract disease (FLUTD) with no uroliths, infection, neoplasia or other demonstrable cause.

It is the most common cause of these signs in cats under about 10 years of age. Bacterial urinary tract infection, by contrast, is uncommon in young cats and more typical of older cats with chronic kidney disease, diabetes or hyperthyroidism.

It is now considered a **systemic syndrome** rather than a disease of the bladder alone. It resembles human interstitial cystitis, or bladder pain syndrome.

Affected cats show:

- an altered stress response, with sympathetic nervous system activation without a proportionate adrenal response
- a defective **urothelium**, with a thinner glycosaminoglycan layer and increased permeability
- **neurogenic inflammation**: sensory fibres in the bladder release neuropeptides that cause oedema, submucosal haemorrhage and pain

Other digestive, skin or behavioural problems often coexist.

## Who it affects

Cats of roughly 2 to 10 years, with this profile:

- kept indoors, overweight and inactive
- eating mainly dry food and drinking little
- living in stressful environments: conflict with other cats, changes in the household, dirty or too few litter boxes

Most studies find a higher risk in males. In **males** FIC can be complicated by **urethral obstruction**.

## Anatomy involved

The bladder shows submucosal oedema, petechial submucosal haemorrhages, seen as **glomerulations** on cystoscopy, and loss of the protective glycosaminoglycan layer.

The male urethra is long and narrow, especially its penile part. Urethral plugs of protein matrix and crystals, or urethral spasm, can obstruct it.

## Clinical signs

- **pollakiuria**
- stranguria and dysuria
- **haematuria**
- urinating outside the litter box (periuria)
- licking of the genital area

Episodes usually resolve on their own within a few days to a week, but they frequently **recur**, often after stressful events.

A male cat straining without passing urine may be obstructed and is an **emergency**.

## Diagnosis

It is made by excluding other causes:

- Complete **urinalysis**. Many cases show haematuria without bacteria. Struvite crystalluria may be present and does not on its own indicate disease.
- **Urine culture** by cystocentesis.
- Abdominal radiographs including the whole urethra, and ultrasound, to rule out uroliths, plugs, polyps or neoplasia.
- In recurrent cases, contrast cystourethrography or cystoscopy.

In a male cat with stranguria the **bladder must always be palpated** to rule out obstruction.

## Treatment

### Acute episode

- **analgesia** with opioids such as buprenorphine; non-steroidal anti-inflammatories only in well-hydrated cats with normal kidney function
- stress reduction

**Antibiotics** are not indicated without proven infection.

### Preventing recurrence

It relies on **multimodal environmental modification (MEMO)**:

- enough clean litter boxes in quiet places
- separate resources in multi-cat households
- scratching posts and elevated areas
- play and predictable routines

And on **increasing water intake**, with wet food or water fountains.

Therapeutic urinary diets and synthetic feline facial pheromones have been used, with variable evidence. Amitriptyline has been used in severe, refractory cases.

The prognosis for each episode is good, but many cats relapse.

<summary>Key studies</summary>

- **iCatCare consensus (Taylor et al., 2025).** The current reference guideline for diagnosing and treating idiopathic cystitis and the other feline lower urinary tract diseases.
- **Systematic review of the management of recurrent idiopathic cystitis (Macleod et al., 2025).** It covers 22 studies. Only two measures are supported: multimodal environmental modification (MEMO) and therapeutic urinary diets, wet ones in particular. The authors found no evidence for non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, glycosaminoglycans, prazosin, short-term amitriptyline or intravesical lidocaine. They warn that primary studies are few and of low quality.
- **Kruger et al. (2015).** A randomised, masked trial of 31 cats fed a therapeutic diet or a control diet. The therapeutic diet led to fewer episodes with several clinical signs (0.7 versus 5.4 per 1000 cat-days). The proportion of cats with any recurrence did not differ (4 of 11 versus 9 of 14). It is a small trial.
