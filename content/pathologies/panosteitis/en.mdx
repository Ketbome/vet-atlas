---
title: Panosteitis
summary: Self-limiting disease of the medullary cavity of the long bones in young dogs of large breeds. It causes painful, shifting lameness that moves from one limb to another.
---

## What it is

Panosteitis is a self-limiting disease of unknown cause that affects the **bone marrow cavity** of the long bones. The fatty marrow undergoes necrosis and is replaced by fibrous tissue and new bone (osteoid) inside the medullary cavity; endosteal and periosteal bone reactions can also occur. Over weeks to months the new bone is remodelled and the marrow returns to normal. Genetic factors (in German shepherds) and rapid growth are suspected; diet, stress, infection and immune mechanisms have been proposed, but none is proven.

## Who it affects

Young, rapidly growing dogs of large and giant breeds, usually between 5 and 16 months of age, although it can occasionally appear up to about 2 years. The **German shepherd** is the classic breed; basset hound, Labrador and golden retriever, Great Dane, Saint Bernard and Dobermann are also predisposed. Males are affected about four times more often than females.

## Anatomy involved

The lesions start near the **nutrient foramen** of the diaphysis and spread through the medullary cavity. The **humerus** is the bone most often involved, followed by the **ulna**, **radius**, **femur** and **tibia**; the forelimbs are affected about four times more often than the hindlimbs. Several bones and limbs can be involved at the same time or in successive episodes.

## Clinical signs

Sudden lameness without a history of trauma that may shift from one limb to another (**shifting leg lameness**) and recur in episodes over months. The key sign is **pain on deep palpation of the diaphysis** of the affected bone, away from the joints. Some dogs show lethargy, inappetence or fever during episodes.

## Diagnosis

- **Radiographs** of the painful bone: patchy, ill-defined increases in **medullary opacity**, starting near the nutrient foramen, with irregular endosteal margins; endosteal and periosteal reactions may follow. Radiographic changes commonly lag 10–14 days behind the signs, so early radiographs can look normal.
- Rule out other causes of lameness in growing dogs: hip or elbow dysplasia, osteochondrosis, hypertrophic osteodystrophy, immune-mediated joint disease.

## Treatment

The disease resolves on its own, usually by the end of skeletal maturity. Treatment controls pain: NSAIDs or other analgesics during episodes and rest during flare-ups. Prognosis is excellent, but owners should know that new episodes in other bones are common until the dog stops growing.
