---
title: Tracheal collapse
summary: Progressive weakening of the tracheal cartilages in toy and small breeds. The rings flatten dorsoventrally and the dorsal membrane sags into the lumen, causing a typical "goose-honk" cough and airway obstruction.
---

## What it is

Tracheal collapse is a degenerative disease of the tracheal cartilage, which loses glycosaminoglycans, chondroitin and water and becomes soft. The C-shaped rings flatten and the **dorsal tracheal membrane** (trachealis muscle and its covering) becomes lax and protrudes into the lumen. Collapse is dynamic: the cervical trachea tends to collapse during inspiration and the intrathoracic trachea during expiration and coughing. It often coexists with bronchomalacia, chronic bronchitis, obesity and heart disease, which worsen the cough.

## Who it affects

Toy and small breeds, usually middle-aged: Yorkshire terrier, Pomeranian, Chihuahua, poodle, Maltese, pug.

## Anatomy involved

- **Trachea**: cervical, thoracic inlet and intrathoracic segments, alone or together; the carina and principal bronchi may also collapse.
- **Dorsal tracheal membrane**: the lax, redundant membrane sags into the lumen and narrows it.
- The disease is graded I to IV according to the loss of lumen (from about 25% reduction of the diameter to a completely flattened cartilage with the lumen essentially obliterated).

## Clinical signs

A dry, harsh, paroxysmal cough that sounds like a goose honk, triggered by excitement, pressure on the neck from a collar or tracheal palpation. Exercise intolerance, inspiratory or expiratory stridor, and in severe cases respiratory distress, cyanosis and syncope. Heat, stress and obesity aggravate it.

## Diagnosis

- **Physical examination**: gentle tracheal palpation readily elicits the cough.
- **Inspiratory and expiratory cervical and thoracic radiographs**: may show narrowing and are useful for screening, but their sensitivity is limited because collapse is dynamic, and they underestimate collapse at the carina.
- **Fluoroscopy**: shows the dynamic collapse during breathing and coughing.
- **Tracheobronchoscopy**: confirms and grades the collapse, and assesses the bronchi; it requires anaesthesia.

## Treatment

Most dogs are managed medically: weight loss, a harness instead of a collar, avoiding heat and smoke, antitussives (for example butorphanol or codeine), short courses of corticosteroids for airway inflammation, and treating concurrent disease. Dogs with severe collapse that does not respond can be treated with an **extraluminal tracheal prosthesis** or an **intraluminal self-expanding stent**; both have specific complications, such as laryngeal paralysis or recurrence of collapse.
