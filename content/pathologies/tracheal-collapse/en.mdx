## What it is

Tracheal collapse is a degenerative disease of the tracheal cartilage. The cartilage loses glycosaminoglycans, chondroitin and water, and becomes soft.

The C-shaped rings flatten. The **dorsal tracheal membrane**, made of the trachealis muscle and its covering, becomes lax and protrudes into the lumen.

Collapse is **dynamic**. The cervical trachea tends to collapse during inspiration, and the intrathoracic trachea during expiration and coughing.

It often coexists with bronchomalacia, chronic bronchitis, obesity and heart disease, which worsen the cough.

## Who it affects

Toy and small breeds, usually middle-aged: Yorkshire terrier, Pomeranian, Chihuahua, poodle, Maltese and pug.

## Anatomy involved

It can affect the cervical, thoracic inlet and intrathoracic segments of the trachea, alone or together. The carina and principal bronchi may also collapse.

The lax, redundant dorsal tracheal membrane sags into the lumen and narrows it.

The disease is graded **I to IV** according to the loss of lumen, from about 25% reduction of the diameter to a completely flattened cartilage with the lumen essentially obliterated.

## Clinical signs

A dry, harsh, paroxysmal cough that sounds like a **goose honk**. It is triggered by excitement, pressure on the neck from a collar or tracheal palpation.

There may also be:

- exercise intolerance
- inspiratory or expiratory stridor
- in severe cases, respiratory distress, cyanosis and syncope

Heat, stress and obesity aggravate it.

## Diagnosis

- On physical examination, gentle tracheal palpation readily elicits the cough.
- Inspiratory and expiratory cervical and thoracic radiographs may show narrowing and are useful for screening. Their sensitivity is limited because collapse is dynamic, and they underestimate collapse at the carina.
- Fluoroscopy shows the dynamic collapse during breathing and coughing.
- **Tracheobronchoscopy** confirms and grades the collapse, and assesses the bronchi. It requires anaesthesia.

## Treatment

Most dogs are managed medically:

- weight loss
- a **harness instead of a collar**
- avoiding heat and smoke
- antitussives, for example butorphanol or codeine
- short courses of corticosteroids for airway inflammation
- treating concurrent disease

Dogs with severe collapse that does not respond can be treated with an **extraluminal tracheal prosthesis** or an **intraluminal self-expanding stent**. Both have specific complications, such as laryngeal paralysis or recurrence of collapse.
