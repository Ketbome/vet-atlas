## What it is

Aspiration pneumonia develops when material from the oropharynx, oesophagus or stomach enters the lower airways.

Acidic gastric content causes a **chemical pneumonitis** that injures the airways and alveoli. Bacteria carried with the material, or arriving later, cause a secondary **bacterial bronchopneumonia**.

The protective reflexes, such as swallowing, laryngeal closure and cough, and mucociliary clearance normally prevent it. So it almost always has an underlying cause.

## Who it affects

Dogs with a predisposing condition:

- **megaesophagus** and other oesophageal disease
- persistent vomiting
- laryngeal paralysis, including after surgery for it
- brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome
- neuromuscular disease, such as myasthenia gravis or polyneuropathy
- reduced consciousness from seizures, sedation or anaesthesia
- force-feeding or feeding tubes

Puppies with cleft palate are also at risk.

## Anatomy involved

Gravity determines the distribution in the **lungs**. With the dog standing or in sternal recumbency, the **right middle lobe** and the cranial lobes are most often affected.

The aspirated material usually comes from the oesophagus and pharynx.

## Clinical signs

Acute or progressive tachypnoea, dyspnoea, moist cough, fever, lethargy and anorexia. They often follow an episode of vomiting, regurgitation or anaesthesia.

Auscultation reveals crackles or reduced sounds over the ventral lung fields.

Severe cases show hypoxaemia and cyanosis. Dogs with chronic regurgitation can have recurrent episodes.

## Diagnosis

- **Thoracic radiographs** in three views show an interstitial to **alveolar pattern** in the cranioventral lobes, most often the right middle lobe. Radiographic changes can lag behind clinical signs.
- Pulse oximetry or arterial blood gas assesses oxygenation and guides oxygen therapy.
- Blood work shows an inflammatory leucogram in many cases.
- Severe, recurrent or refractory cases need a tracheal wash or bronchoalveolar lavage with culture and sensitivity.
- The cause is sought with oesophageal imaging and neurological and laryngeal examination.

## Treatment

- oxygen supplementation when hypoxaemic
- intravenous fluids to keep the patient hydrated, which helps clear secretions
- nebulisation and coupage
- broad-spectrum antibiotics, adjusted to culture results when available

Mild cases can be treated at home.

Preventing recurrence means **managing the predisposing condition**. Examples are upright feeding in megaesophagus, or antiemetics and airway protection during anaesthesia.

<details>
<summary>In the cat</summary>

Aspiration pneumonia is recognised less often than in dogs, but the predisposing conditions are similar.

The most frequent causes are:

- **vomiting**
- anaesthesia and sedation
- force-feeding or tube feeding
- oesophageal and swallowing disorders
- oral **liquid paraffin** or other oily laxatives for constipation or hairballs, which can cause lipid pneumonia

Signs are similar to those in the dog: **tachypnoea**, dyspnoea, lethargy and anorexia. Owners can mistake the cough for retching or vomiting, and affected cats can be hyperthermic or hypothermic.

Diagnosis and treatment follow the same principles as in the dog: thoracic radiographs, assessment of oxygenation, airway lavage with culture in severe cases, oxygen, fluids and antibiotics.
