---
title: Aspiration pneumonia
summary: Inflammation and infection of the lungs after inhalation of gastric or oropharyngeal contents. It complicates regurgitation, vomiting, swallowing disorders and anaesthesia, and typically affects the dependent lung lobes.
---

## What it is

Aspiration pneumonia develops when material from the oropharynx, oesophagus or stomach enters the lower airways. Acidic gastric content causes a chemical **pneumonitis** that injures the airways and alveoli; bacteria carried with the material or arriving later cause a secondary **bacterial bronchopneumonia**. The protective reflexes (swallowing, laryngeal closure, cough) and mucociliary clearance normally prevent it, so it almost always has an underlying cause.

## Who it affects

Dogs with a predisposing condition: megaesophagus and other oesophageal disease, persistent vomiting, laryngeal paralysis (and after surgery for it), brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome, neuromuscular disease (myasthenia gravis, polyneuropathy), reduced consciousness (seizures, sedation, anaesthesia), and force-feeding or feeding tubes. Puppies with cleft palate are also at risk.

## Anatomy involved

- **Lungs**: gravity determines the distribution; with the dog standing or in sternal recumbency, the **right middle lobe** and the cranial lobes are most often affected.
- **Oesophagus and pharynx**: the usual source of the aspirated material.

## Clinical signs

Acute or progressive tachypnoea, dyspnoea, moist cough, fever, lethargy and anorexia, often after an episode of vomiting, regurgitation or anaesthesia. Auscultation reveals crackles or reduced sounds over the ventral lung fields. Severe cases show hypoxaemia and cyanosis. Dogs with chronic regurgitation can have recurrent episodes.

## Diagnosis

- **Thoracic radiographs (three views)**: interstitial to **alveolar pattern** in the cranioventral lobes, most often the right middle lobe; radiographic changes can lag behind clinical signs.
- **Pulse oximetry or arterial blood gas**: to assess oxygenation and guide oxygen therapy.
- **Blood work**: inflammatory leucogram in many cases.
- **Airway lavage (tracheal wash or bronchoalveolar lavage) with culture and sensitivity**: in severe, recurrent or refractory cases.
- **Search for the cause**: oesophageal imaging, neurological and laryngeal examination.

## Treatment

Oxygen supplementation when hypoxaemic, intravenous fluids to keep the patient hydrated (which helps clear secretions), nebulisation and coupage, and broad-spectrum antibiotics, adjusted to culture results when available. Mild cases can be treated at home. The predisposing condition must be managed to prevent recurrence, for example upright feeding in megaesophagus or antiemetics and airway protection during anaesthesia.

## In the cat

Aspiration pneumonia is recognised less often in cats than in dogs, but the predisposing conditions are similar. The most frequent causes are **vomiting**, **anaesthesia** and sedation, **force-feeding** or tube feeding, oesophageal and swallowing disorders and oral administration of **liquid paraffin** or other oily laxatives for constipation or hairballs, which can cause lipid pneumonia. Signs are similar to those in the dog (**tachypnoea**, dyspnoea, lethargy, anorexia), although owners can mistake the cough for retching or vomiting, and affected cats can be hyperthermic or hypothermic. Diagnosis and treatment follow the same principles as in the dog (thoracic radiographs, oxygenation, airway lavage with culture in severe cases, oxygen, fluids and antibiotics).
