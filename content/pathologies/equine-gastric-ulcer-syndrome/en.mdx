## What it is

**Equine gastric ulcer syndrome** (EGUS) groups the erosive and ulcerative lesions of the horse's stomach.

The 2015 consensus of the European College of Equine Internal Medicine (ECEIM) divides it into two diseases. They affect different mucosae and have different causes and treatments.

### Equine squamous gastric disease

**ESGD** affects the non-glandular part of the stomach, which is poorly protected against acid. Lesions occur when **acid** and volatile fatty acids in the gastric contents bathe that mucosa.

It can be **primary**, in otherwise healthy horses, or **secondary** to delayed gastric emptying, for example from pyloric obstruction.

It is favoured by:

- **long fasts**
- **starch-rich rations** low in forage, whose fermentation produces volatile fatty acids
- **exercise**, which raises abdominal pressure and pushes acid up into the squamous region

### Equine glandular gastric disease

**EGGD** affects the glandular mucosa, mainly the **pyloric antrum**. It is thought to result from a failure of mucosal protective mechanisms rather than from acid itself, but the cause is not clear.

## Who it affects

Horses of every type, but especially **performance horses**.

According to the ECEIM consensus, ESGD affects 37% of untrained Thoroughbreds. It reaches 80–100% within 2–3 months of race training. It is also common in Standardbreds and in endurance, sport and leisure horses.

Risk factors for ESGD are:

- intense exercise
- long intervals without forage
- high-starch rations
- straw as the only forage
- no water at pasture

Those for EGGD are poorly defined.

## Anatomy involved

### Stomach

The stomach (*Ventriculus*) is small for the size of the horse. Its mucosa has two parts separated by the **margo plicatus**:

- the **squamous** (non-glandular) **region**, dorsally
- the **glandular region**, ventrally, which includes the fundus, the body and the pyloric antrum

ESGD lesions concentrate along the margo plicatus, especially on the **lesser curvature**. EGGD lesions concentrate in the **pyloric antrum** and around the pylorus.

### Oesophagus

It enters the stomach at the **cardia**, and lesions of EGUS can extend to its terminal part. Gastroscopy needs a long endoscope, about 3 m, to pass along the oesophagus and reach the pylorus.

## Clinical signs

Many horses with ulcers show no signs. Those described are non-specific:

- **picky** or reduced appetite
- loss of body condition
- mild or recurrent **colic**
- **teeth grinding**
- behavioural changes, such as irritability or nervousness
- **poor performance**

None of them allows the disease to be diagnosed on its own.

## Diagnosis

**Gastroscopy** is the only reliable test. It is performed after withholding solid feed and should examine:

- the squamous region
- the margo plicatus
- the glandular fundus
- the pyloric antrum and the pylorus

ESGD is graded with the Equine Gastric Ulcer Council **0–4** scale, recommended by the consensus. There is no validated scale for EGGD, and describing the location, distribution and appearance of the lesions is recommended.

Lesion severity does not correlate well with clinical signs. The lesions found may also not be the cause of the horse's problem, so other causes of colic or poor performance must be ruled out.

No blood or faecal test has enough diagnostic value.

## Treatment and prevention

### ESGD

Oral **omeprazole**, a proton pump inhibitor, heals most squamous lesions in about 4 weeks. The consensus recommends 4 mg/kg daily of the buffered formulations. Enteric-coated granule formulations have worked well at lower doses.

### EGGD

It responds much less well to omeprazole alone. The 2015 consensus recommended adding **sucralfate**. Later studies have shown better results with **misoprostol**, a synthetic prostaglandin analogue.

In both cases gastroscopy is repeated to confirm healing before stopping treatment.

### Prevention and management

- forage available almost continuously, without gaps of more than 6 hours
- no more than 2 g of starch per kg of body weight per day or 1 g/kg per meal
- alfalfa hay for working horses
- turnout
- water always available

In risk periods, such as competitions, travel or changes of yard, low-dose preventive omeprazole can be used.

<summary>Key studies</summary>

- **ECEIM consensus (Sykes et al., 2015).** It separated ESGD and EGGD, recommended the 0–4 scale for ESGD and advised against grading EGGD until a validated scale exists. It reports ESGD prevalences of 80–100% in Thoroughbreds in training and healing rates of 70–77% with buffered omeprazole at 4 mg/kg for 28 days. For EGGD it recommended omeprazole plus sucralfate (12 mg/kg every 12 hours). It is expert opinion based on studies of variable quality.
- **Sykes et al. (2015).** Randomised blinded trial in 60 Thoroughbred racehorses with grade ≥ 2 ulcers, given 1, 2 or 4 mg/kg of enteric-coated omeprazole before exercise. The lower doses were non-inferior to 4 mg/kg for ESGD. At 28 days, 86% of squamous lesions had healed versus 14% of glandular lesions, and EGGD worsened in 36% of horses. Horses received the drug after a brief fast, which may favour its efficacy.
- **Mason et al. (2019).** Meta-analysis of 7 randomised trials with 566 horses in training. Preventive omeprazole reduced the occurrence of ulcers compared with sham (RR 0.28; 95% CI 0.18–0.43): 23.4% of treated horses developed them versus 77.2% of controls. Preventive doses varied between trials, and the data come from racehorses and mostly from squamous lesions.
- **Varley et al. (2019).** Prospective study in 63 sport horses with EGGD above grade 1. **Misoprostol** (5 µg/kg every 12 hours) healed 72% of cases versus 20% with omeprazole plus sucralfate, and healing was associated with resolution of signs (OR 11). Treatment was allocated by centre, not at random, and owners were not blinded.
- **Luthersson et al. (2009).** Cross-sectional study of 201 non-racing horses from 23 yards in Denmark. 53% had grade ≥ 2 ulcers. Straw as the only forage, more than 2 g/kg of starch per day or more than 1 g/kg per meal, and no water at pasture were associated with higher risk. For ESGD, so were more than 6 hours between forage meals. It was carried out only in winter, with horses stabled.
