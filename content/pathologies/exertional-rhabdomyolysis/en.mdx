---
title: Exertional rhabdomyolysis
summary: Necrosis of muscle fibres during or after exercise, causing stiffness, muscle pain and dark urine from myoglobinuria. It can be sporadic or recurrent; recurrent forms include that of Thoroughbreds and Standardbreds and the polysaccharide storage myopathies (PSSM types 1 and 2).
---

## What it is

Exertional rhabdomyolysis (**tying-up**, azoturia) is the breakdown of skeletal muscle fibres associated with exercise. As the fibres rupture they release **creatine kinase (CK)**, **aspartate aminotransferase (AST)** and **myoglobin** into the blood; myoglobin is excreted in the urine and can damage the kidneys. Two broad groups are distinguished:

- **Sporadic rhabdomyolysis**: an isolated episode in a horse with normal muscle, caused by exercise beyond its fitness, exhaustion in endurance rides, heat, electrolyte imbalances or, possibly, vitamin E and selenium deficiency.
- **Chronic or recurrent rhabdomyolysis**, due to an underlying myopathy:
  - **Recurrent exertional rhabdomyolysis (RER)** of Thoroughbreds, Standardbreds and Arabians: attributed to abnormal regulation of intracellular **calcium** in muscle. Episodes occur mainly in nervous horses on high-grain diets after days of rest.
  - **Type 1 polysaccharide storage myopathy (PSSM1)**: caused by a dominantly inherited gain-of-function mutation of the **GYS1** gene (glycogen synthase), which makes muscle accumulate glycogen and an abnormal polysaccharide.
  - **Type 2 PSSM (PSSM2)**: diagnosed by biopsy when there is abnormal glycogen accumulation without the GYS1 mutation. It probably groups several different disorders, including the myofibrillar myopathy of Arabians and warmbloods, and its cause is unknown.

## Who it affects

- The sporadic form, any horse, especially on **returning to work** after rest or in endurance rides.
- RER, **Thoroughbreds** and **Standardbreds**, more often **young females** and **nervous** horses, and Arabian endurance horses.
- PSSM1, **Quarter Horses** and related breeds, European and North American **draught** horses and, less often, other breeds.
- PSSM2, Quarter Horses and warmbloods, and myofibrillar myopathy, Arabians and warmbloods.

## Anatomy involved

- **Gluteus medius muscle**: the muscle mass of the croup; with the lumbar muscles, it is one of the muscles that become hardest and most painful during an episode, and in chronic myopathies it can atrophy.
- **Epaxial muscles**: the **longissimus** and the other muscles of the back and loins contract and become painful, giving the horse a tucked-up abdomen and a short, stiff stride.
- **Hindlimb muscles**: the muscles of the thigh are also involved, causing hindlimb stiffness; samples for **muscle biopsy** are taken from the affected muscle groups.
- The **kidneys** can be damaged by ischaemia and the combined nephrotoxic effects of myoglobin, dehydration and NSAIDs.

## Clinical signs

During or shortly after exercise the horse becomes **stiff**, shortens its stride, refuses to move and **sweats** excessively; the muscles of the croup and back are **hard and painful** on palpation. There may be tachycardia, tachypnoea, muscle tremors and colic-like signs. **Urine** is red or brown from myoglobin in moderate or severe cases. In the most severe cases the horse becomes recumbent and may develop **acute kidney injury**. In PSSM the signs may be subtler: exercise intolerance, lack of energy, muscle stiffness or atrophy, sometimes without clear increases in CK.

## Diagnosis

- **Muscle enzymes**: increased serum **CK**, **AST** and lactate dehydrogenase activity confirm the diagnosis; in PSSM1, CK stays elevated longer than in other forms. An **exercise test**, measuring CK before and 4 hours after light exercise, detects subclinical cases.
- **Kidney**: in horses with myoglobinuria, urea or creatinine are monitored to assess renal damage.
- **GYS1 genetic test** on blood or hair for PSSM1, the test of choice in affected breeds.
- **Muscle biopsy** for PSSM2, myofibrillar myopathy and other myopathies. Commercial genetic tests for PSSM2 are not validated (see Key studies).
- Differential diagnosis: colic, atypical myopathy (pasture myopathy, linked to *Acer* seeds) and other causes of stiffness or dark urine.

## Treatment and prevention

- **Acute episode**: **stop exercise at once** and move the horse to a well-bedded stall with fresh water. **Fluid therapy** if there is haemoconcentration or myoglobinuria, to restore fluid balance and protect the kidney. Tranquillisers with opioids relieve anxiety and stiffness; **non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs** only once the horse is well hydrated. Most horses are fairly pain-free within 18–24 hours.
- **Prevention in RER**: avoiding stall confinement with turnout or a hot walker, avoiding rest days before exercise, reducing stress and excitement, replacing part of the grain with **fat** and fibre and, in some horses, dantrolene before exercise.
- **Prevention in PSSM**: regular exercise (a few days of rest is a common trigger) and a forage-based diet **low in starch and sugar**, with fat as an energy source if needed.
- The evidence for diet and dantrolene comes from crossover studies with very few horses.
- The horse is a food-producing animal in many countries and drug use is subject to **national regulations** and **withdrawal periods**. In the EU, the equine identification document (passport) states whether the horse is excluded from slaughter for human consumption, and this decides which medicines may be given.

## Key studies

- **MacLeay et al. (1999)**, study of the records of 984 Thoroughbreds at a Midwestern US racetrack during one season, with a case-control study of management (59 cases and 47 controls). Exertional rhabdomyolysis affected **4.9%** of horses, mainly **2-year-old females**. **Nervous** horses were 5.4 times more likely to be affected and those with some lameness 4.2 times more likely. Episodes were associated with rest days before galloping and with more than 4.5 kg of grain a day. Management data come from retrospective questionnaires.
- **Isgren et al. (2010)**, case-control study in 683 Standardbred trotters from 22 yards. The annual incidence of exertional rhabdomyolysis was 6.4 per 100 horses. **Females** (OR 7.1; 95% CI 2.1–23.4) and **nervous** horses (OR 7.9; 95% CI 2.3–27.0) were at higher risk. Susceptible horses won more races than controls, and none of the 36 genotyped carried the GYS1 mutation. Confidence intervals are wide because of the small number of cases.
- **Norton et al. (2016)**, genomic study of 491 Thoroughbred and 196 Standardbred racehorses. The **heritability** of RER was estimated at about **0.34–0.46 in Thoroughbreds** and **0.39–0.49 in Standardbreds**, depending on the model. It indicates a moderate genetic basis, although it does not identify the causal mutation.
- **McCue et al. (2008)**, study of 831 horses of 36 breeds with PSSM diagnosed by biopsy. The **GYS1** mutation was found in 17 breeds, and was present in 87% of draught horses and 72% of Quarter Horses with PSSM, but in only 18% of warmbloods and 24% of other light breeds. This difference led to the definition of PSSM2. Cases come from a single reference laboratory.
- **Valberg et al. (2021)**, cross-sectional study of the variants tested by **commercial genetic tests** for PSSM2 and myofibrillar myopathy, in warmblood and Arabian horses with a biopsy diagnosis and breed-matched controls (182 horses) and in 205 public genomes. No variant was associated with the histological diagnosis, and the sensitivity of all of them was below 0.33; the variants were also present in ancient domestic horses. The authors advise against using them for diagnosis, pre-purchase examination or breeding selection.
