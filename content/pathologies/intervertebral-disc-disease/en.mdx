---
title: Intervertebral disc disease
summary: Degeneration of the intervertebral disc with extrusion or protrusion of disc material into the vertebral canal. A common cause of spinal cord compression in the dog, from neck or back pain to paraplegia.
---

## What it is

Intervertebral disc disease (IVDD) is the displacement of degenerated disc material into the vertebral canal, where it compresses and bruises the spinal cord or nerve roots. Two classic forms are recognised (Hansen classification):

- **Hansen type I (extrusion)**: early **chondroid degeneration** of the nucleus pulposus, which dehydrates and often mineralises. The degenerated nucleus ruptures through the dorsal anulus fibrosus and is pushed suddenly into the canal. Onset is usually acute.
- **Hansen type II (protrusion)**: slower **fibroid degeneration**; the anulus bulges dorsally without rupturing. Onset is gradual and the course chronic.

Acute non-compressive nucleus pulposus extrusion, a high-velocity extrusion of healthy nucleus that bruises the cord without staying in the canal, is a related form.

## Who it affects

Type I is typical of **chondrodystrophic breeds** (dachshund, French bulldog, beagle, Pekingese, shih tzu, Lhasa apso, cocker spaniel), with extrusions possible from 1–2 years of age, because their discs start degenerating within the first months of life. Type II affects mainly older, non-chondrodystrophic large breeds.

## Anatomy involved

Each intervertebral disc (*discus intervertebralis*) joins two vertebral bodies and has a gelatinous centre, the **nucleus pulposus**, surrounded by the fibrous rings of the **anulus fibrosus**. Disc material moves dorsally into the vertebral canal, beneath or through the dorsal longitudinal ligament, toward the spinal cord. Thoracolumbar extrusions are most frequent between **T11 and L3**. Cranial thoracic herniations are rare, which has been attributed to the **intercapital ligament** in that region.

## Clinical signs

- **Cervical**: marked neck pain, low head carriage, reluctance to move the head, root signature (lameness of a thoracic limb); neurological deficits are usually milder than with thoracolumbar disease.
- **Thoracolumbar**: back pain with kyphosis, then pelvic limb ataxia, paresis or paraplegia. In severe cases, loss of voluntary urination and of **deep pain perception**, which is the most important prognostic factor.

## Diagnosis

- **Neurological examination** to localise the lesion and grade its severity.
- **Survey radiographs** are only suggestive (narrowed disc space, mineralised discs in situ, small intervertebral foramen), not enough for a definitive diagnosis.
- **MRI** is the method of choice; **CT** and **myelography** are alternatives to locate the compression and its side before surgery.

## Treatment

Dogs with pain alone or mild paresis can be managed conservatively with strict confinement for several weeks, analgesia and gradual rehabilitation. Non-ambulatory dogs, those that worsen or do not respond, and those with recurrent pain are candidates for **surgical decompression**: hemilaminectomy or pediculectomy in the thoracolumbar region and **ventral slot** in the neck. Prognosis is good when deep pain perception is preserved and guarded when it has been lost; a small proportion of those dogs develop progressive myelomalacia, which is fatal.
