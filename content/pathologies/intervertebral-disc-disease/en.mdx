## What it is

Intervertebral disc disease (IVDD) is the displacement of degenerated disc material into the vertebral canal. There it compresses and bruises the spinal cord or nerve roots.

The Hansen classification recognises two classic forms.

### Hansen type I (extrusion)

There is early **chondroid degeneration** of the nucleus pulposus, which dehydrates and often mineralises. The degenerated nucleus ruptures through the dorsal anulus fibrosus and is pushed suddenly into the canal.

Onset is usually acute.

### Hansen type II (protrusion)

There is slower **fibroid degeneration**. The anulus bulges dorsally without rupturing.

Onset is gradual and the course chronic.

### Acute non-compressive extrusion

This is a related form. Healthy nucleus pulposus is extruded at high velocity, bruises the cord and does not stay in the canal.

## Who it affects

Type I is typical of **chondrodystrophic breeds**:

- dachshund
- French bulldog
- beagle
- Pekingese
- shih tzu
- Lhasa apso
- cocker spaniel

Their discs start degenerating within the first months of life, so extrusions are possible from 1–2 years of age.

Type II affects mainly older, non-chondrodystrophic large breeds.

## Anatomy involved

Each intervertebral disc (*discus intervertebralis*) joins two vertebral bodies. It has a gelatinous centre, the **nucleus pulposus**, surrounded by the rings of the **anulus fibrosus**.

Disc material moves dorsally into the vertebral canal, beneath or through the dorsal longitudinal ligament, toward the spinal cord.

Thoracolumbar extrusions are most frequent between **T11 and L3**. Cranial thoracic herniations are rare, which has been attributed to the **intercapital ligament** in that region.

## Clinical signs

### Cervical

- marked neck pain
- low head carriage and reluctance to move the head
- root signature, with lameness of a thoracic limb

Neurological deficits are usually milder than with thoracolumbar disease.

### Thoracolumbar

Back pain with kyphosis, then pelvic limb ataxia, paresis or paraplegia.

Severe cases lose voluntary urination and **deep pain perception**, which is the most important prognostic factor.

## Diagnosis

- The **neurological examination** localises the lesion and grades its severity.
- **Survey radiographs** are only suggestive: narrowed disc space, mineralised discs in situ, small intervertebral foramen. They are not enough for a definitive diagnosis.
- **MRI** is the method of choice. **CT** and **myelography** are alternatives to locate the compression and its side before surgery.

## Treatment

### Conservative

For dogs with pain alone or mild paresis:

- strict confinement for several weeks
- analgesia
- gradual rehabilitation

### Surgical

Non-ambulatory dogs, those that worsen or do not respond, and those with recurrent pain are candidates for **surgical decompression**.

- hemilaminectomy or pediculectomy in the thoracolumbar region
- **ventral slot** in the neck

### Prognosis

It is good when deep pain perception is preserved and guarded when it has been lost. A small proportion of those dogs develop progressive myelomalacia, which is fatal.
