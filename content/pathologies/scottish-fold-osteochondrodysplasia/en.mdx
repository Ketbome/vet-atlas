---
title: Scottish fold osteochondrodysplasia
summary: Inherited cartilage disorder caused by the same genetic variant that folds the Scottish fold's ears. It produces deformities of the distal limbs and tail and progressive, painful osteoarthritis; every fold-eared cat is affected to some degree.
---

## What it is

Scottish fold osteochondrodysplasia is a developmental disorder of cartilage and bone. It is caused by a dominant variant of the ***TRPV4*** gene, which encodes a calcium-permeable ion channel. The same variant makes the ear cartilage weak so that it folds forward, the trait that defines the breed: this is why **the folded ear and the disease are inseparable**. Articular and growth cartilage develop abnormally, and over time bony deformities, **exostoses** (bony proliferations) around the distal joints and progressive **osteoarthritis** appear.

Inheritance is **incompletely dominant**: **homozygous** cats, born from mating two fold-eared cats, have severe, early lesions; **heterozygous** cats, born from mating a Scottish fold with a straight-eared cat, also develop lesions, usually milder and later. There is no fold-eared Scottish fold free of the disease. On welfare grounds, some cat registries do not recognise the breed and breeding fold-eared cats is restricted in some countries.

## Who it affects

Fold-eared **Scottish fold** cats and crosses carrying the variant, such as some cats of related breeds. Radiographic changes can be seen in kittens only a few weeks old and lameness from 4–6 months; in heterozygous cats the age of onset and severity vary widely, from a few months to several years. Most straight-eared cats of the breed do not carry the variant, but some heterozygous kittens are born with folded ears that straighten as they grow, so only the genetic test rules it out.

## Anatomy involved

- **Distal pelvic limbs**: the **tarsus**, **metatarsus** and **phalanges** are most severely affected; the bones are short and misshapen and a large plantar exostosis forms caudal to the calcaneus and around the tarsometatarsal region in advanced cases.
- **Distal thoracic limbs**: similar lesions in the carpus, metacarpus and digits.
- **Tail**: short, wide, irregularly shaped **caudal vertebrae**; the tail is thick, short and stiff, and the cat cannot flex it normally.
- **Ears**: folded auricular cartilage.

## Clinical signs

**Lameness**, a stiff or stilted gait, reluctance to jump and move, and a short, thick, **inflexible tail**. In advanced cases hard swellings can be felt on the plantar aspect of the tarsi, and the distal limbs are short and misshapen. Cats hide pain well: chronic pain may show only as reduced activity and play, and an apparently calm temperament may actually reflect pain.

## Diagnosis

- **Breed and folded ears** with compatible signs.
- **Examination**: tail stiffness (assessed by flexing it gently, without forcing) and pain and thickening of the distal joints.
- **Radiographs** of the feet, tarsi, carpi and tail: short, misshapen bones, narrowed joint spaces, progressive new bone formation around the distal joints and, in advanced cases, a plantar exostosis caudal to the calcaneus.
- **Genetic test** for the *TRPV4* variant.

## Treatment

There is no cure, and no single treatment works for every cat; treatment is palliative and aims to control **chronic pain**: analgesics (a pain relief trial can show whether the cat becomes more active), and subcutaneous pentosan polysulfate or oral glycosaminoglycan preparations have helped some cats. In selected cases **palliative radiotherapy** of the exostoses, excision of the exostoses and pantarsal **arthrodesis** have been used. The environment is adapted with ramps, cat walks and cat trees so the cat can still reach high places. The most important measure is **prevention**: not breeding fold-eared cats.

## Key studies

- **Gandolfi et al. (2016)**, a genome-wide association study of 44 Scottish folds and 54 controls. It identified the *TRPV4* p.V342F (c.1024G>T) variant, associated with both the folded ear and the osteochondrodysplasia; it was absent from 648 unaffected cats. In cells, the mutant channel showed altered activity.
- **Rorden et al. (2021)**, a radiographic survey of 22 genotyped Scottish folds, assessed by four reviewers blinded to ear shape. All 10 fold-eared cats were heterozygous and none of the 12 straight-eared cats carried the variant. Fold-eared cats scored worse on average, but their changes were much milder than in earlier reports. The sample is small.
- **Velie et al. (2023)**, VetCompass records of 1131 Scottish Fold and 117 Scottish Shorthair cats in Australia (1992–2018). Only 12 Scottish folds had a clinical diagnosis of osteochondrodysplasia and another 69 cats were suspected cases; diagnosed cats were usually under 30 months old. It measures diagnoses recorded in first-opinion practice, not radiographic lesions.
- **Matsumoto et al. (2026)**, 114 cats genotyped and photographed as they grew. Seven heterozygous cats had folded ears as kittens that straightened as they matured (12.7% of heterozygotes); their pinnae were smaller than those of straight-eared cats without the variant. Ear shape alone is not enough to identify carriers.
- **Ukawa et al. (2026)**, genotyping of 8610 cats of 14 breeds in Japan. As genetic testing was adopted, homozygous Scottish folds fell from 14.2% in 2017 to 1.9% in 2024, while the proportion of heterozygotes held steady. The variant was also found in the American Curl, Norwegian Forest Cat, Munchkin and Minuet.
