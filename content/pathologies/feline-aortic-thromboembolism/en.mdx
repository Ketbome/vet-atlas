---
title: Feline aortic thromboembolism
summary: Sudden obstruction of the terminal aorta by a thrombus that almost always forms in the left atrium of a cat with cardiomyopathy. It causes acute, very painful paralysis of the pelvic limbs, with cold, pulseless extremities.
---

## What it is

Arterial thromboembolism is the occlusion of an artery by a thrombus that formed elsewhere and travelled in the blood. In the cat the great majority are **cardiogenic**: in the dilated left atrium of a cat with cardiomyopathy (above all hypertrophic cardiomyopathy) blood stagnates, and endothelial dysfunction and increased platelet activation probably contribute, so a thrombus forms. When it breaks free it usually lodges at the **aortic trifurcation** (the "saddle" thrombus), where the aorta divides into the iliac arteries. Less often the embolus obstructs a brachial artery (a single thoracic limb, usually the right), or a renal, mesenteric or cerebral artery. A minority of cases are associated with neoplasia, especially of the lung.

## Who it affects

Adult cats, more often males, with a cardiomyopathy that has often not been diagnosed: in many cats thromboembolism is the first sign of the heart disease. The risk rises with left atrial size, atrial dysfunction, spontaneous echo contrast or a thrombus on echocardiography, and previous episodes.

## Anatomy involved

- **Left atrium and auricle**: where the thrombus forms.
- **Terminal abdominal aorta**: the embolus lodges at the trifurcation and blocks flow to the external iliac arteries and, from them, to the femoral arteries, which lose their pulse.
- **Pelvic limb muscles and nerves**: the **gastrocnemius** muscles become very firm and painful. The limb nerves are also affected (lower motor neuron dysfunction), which explains the paralysis.

## Clinical signs

**Sudden** paresis or paralysis of one or both pelvic limbs, with **severe pain** and vocalisation. The "five Ps": paralysis, pain, pallor (pale or cyanotic pads and nail beds), pulselessness (no femoral pulse) and poikilothermia (cold limbs). The gastrocnemius muscles are firm. Tachypnoea is common from pain, stress or concurrent heart failure. Cats are often hypothermic, which worsens the prognosis.

## Diagnosis

- Diagnosis is mainly **clinical**: absent femoral pulses, cold limbs and pain in a cat with acute paralysis.
- **Glucose and lactate** in blood from the affected limb compared with a normal limb or the central circulation: glucose is lower and lactate higher in the ischaemic limb.
- **Doppler** or ultrasound of the terminal aorta to confirm absent flow.
- **Echocardiography** for the underlying cardiomyopathy, atrial size and residual thrombi; **thoracic radiographs** and NT-proBNP for heart failure.
- **Blood work**: hyperglycaemia, azotaemia and increased muscle enzymes are common; electrolytes, especially potassium, must be monitored.

## Treatment

**Analgesia** with opioids from the start, oxygen and treatment of heart failure if present. **Antithrombotic therapy** is started: clopidogrel together with an anticoagulant (unfractionated or low-molecular-weight heparin, or a factor Xa inhibitor such as rivaroxaban). Thrombolysis and surgical removal of the thrombus are little used because of reperfusion injury, in particular life-threatening **hyperkalaemia**. Recovery needs nursing care; motor function, when it returns, can take weeks or months, and distal necrosis may develop.

The prognosis is guarded. Many cats are euthanised at initial presentation. Survival is better when a single limb is affected, when some motor function is preserved and when the cat is not hypothermic; concurrent heart failure worsens it. Recurrence is common, so lifelong **clopidogrel prophylaxis** is maintained; in the FAT CAT trial clopidogrel was superior to aspirin in preventing further episodes.

## Key studies

### Presentation and prognosis

- **Smith et al. (2003)**, retrospective study of 127 cats at a referral hospital. Hypothermia (66%) and absent motor function (66%) were common, and 93% of the cats examined had an enlarged left atrium. Of 87 cats treated, 45% survived to discharge; rectal temperature, heart rate, phosphorus, motor function and the number of limbs affected differed between survivors and non-survivors, and a model based on rectal temperature predicted a 50% probability of survival at 37.2 °C. Median survival after discharge was 117 days, shorter in cats with heart failure (77 versus 223 days).
- **Borgeat et al. (2014)**, retrospective study of 250 cats in 3 general practices in the United Kingdom. 61.2% were euthanised at presentation. Hypothermia predicted death or euthanasia within 24 hours and, in cats that survived longer, so did not receiving aspirin, clopidogrel or both. Cats that survived 7 days had a median survival of 94 days, and 6 were alive a year later.

### Diagnosis

- **Klainbart et al. (2014)**, prospective controlled study of 22 cats with thromboembolism, 10 cats paralysed for other reasons and 10 ambulatory cats (and dogs in parallel). The difference between systemic glucose and glucose in the affected limb identified thromboembolism well: a difference of 30 mg/dL had 100% sensitivity and 90% specificity in cats.

### Treatment and prevention

- **Guillaumin et al. (2022, BLASTT)**, double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled trial of tissue plasminogen activator in 40 cats with thromboembolism of 2 or more limbs, treated within 6 hours. Only 20 cats survived to 48 hours. Limb scores, survival to discharge (45% with thrombolysis, 30% with placebo) and complications such as acute kidney injury or reperfusion injury did not differ significantly; the high mortality left the trial with little statistical power.
- **Hogan et al. (2015, FAT CAT)**, double-blind randomised trial in 75 cats that had survived a cardiogenic thromboembolism. Clopidogrel reduced recurrence compared with aspirin (median time to recurrence 443 versus 192 days) and also the combined outcome of recurrence or cardiac death. Both drugs were well tolerated.
- **Brainard et al. (2025, SUPERCAT)**, double-masked randomised trial in 45 cats that had recovered from cardiogenic thromboembolism. Recurrence was similar with clopidogrel (7 of 19 cats, 37%) and rivaroxaban (10 of 26, 39%), as was the time to death. The number of cats is small for a comparison of this kind.
