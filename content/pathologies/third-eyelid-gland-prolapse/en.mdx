## What it is

The gland of the third eyelid normally sits hidden at the base of the third eyelid.

When the connective tissue that anchors it is lax, the gland slips up and protrudes over the free margin of the third eyelid. There it becomes exposed, inflamed and swollen.

The condition is commonly known as **"cherry eye"**. The cause is thought to be an inherited laxity of the gland's attachments.

## Who it affects

Young dogs, usually under 2 years of age. The median age at diagnosis is about 7 months.

Predisposed breeds include:

- Neapolitan mastiff
- **English bulldog**
- cane corso
- Lhasa apso
- cocker spaniel
- beagle
- Boston terrier
- Pekingese
- brachycephalic breeds in general

It can be bilateral.

## Anatomy involved

The **third eyelid** (*plica semilunaris conjunctivae*) is a fold of conjunctiva at the medial angle of the eye, supported by a **T-shaped cartilage**.

The **gland of the third eyelid** (*glandula superficialis palpebrae tertiae*) surrounds the base of the stem of that cartilage.

Together with the lacrimal gland, it produces the **aqueous part of the tear film**. It produces about 30–60% of tears, which is why removing it predisposes to keratoconjunctivitis sicca.

## Clinical signs

A smooth, pink to red, oval mass protruding from behind the third eyelid at the **medial canthus**.

Mild ocular discharge and conjunctivitis are common. The swelling may recede for short periods, but the gland often remains prolapsed.

## Diagnosis

The diagnosis is clinical.

Deformity (eversion) of the **cartilage** of the third eyelid, which can accompany the prolapse, should be identified, because it changes the surgical technique.

A **Schirmer tear test** is useful before and after treatment.

## Treatment

**Surgical replacement** of the gland is the treatment of choice. The most widely used techniques are:

- the **pocket technique**, which creates a pocket in the conjunctiva on the bulbar surface of the third eyelid and buries the gland in it
- anchoring the gland with sutures to the orbital rim, the periorbital fascia or the cartilage of the third eyelid

Recurrence is possible, especially in giant breeds with a deformed cartilage, and may need a second procedure.

**Excision of the gland is contraindicated.** It increases the risk of keratoconjunctivitis sicca later in life, in 30–40% of dogs after complete excision.

Even after replacement some dogs develop it, so tear production is monitored.
