## What it is

Symmetric lupoid onychodystrophy, also called symmetrical onychomadesis, appears to be the most common immune-mediated claw disease of the dog.

An **immune-mediated** inflammation attacks the tissue that produces the claw, at the junction between the dermis and the epidermis of the claw base. The claws separate from the underlying dermis and fall off (**onychomadesis**). The new claws grow abnormal.

The trigger is unknown. A hereditary predisposition, linked to certain DLA class II haplotypes, has been shown in some breeds.

## Who it affects

Young adult and middle-aged dogs, usually between about 3 and 8 years of age.

Predisposed breeds include:

- **German shepherd**
- Gordon setter
- English setter
- giant schnauzer
- bearded collie
- Rhodesian ridgeback

It is also described in many other breeds.

## Anatomy involved

The **claw** (*unguicula*) is a horny capsule that covers the **ungual process of the distal phalanx**.

In this disease the inflammation forms a band beneath the epidermis of the claw bed, mainly along the dorsal aspect of the claw. The horn plate separates from the claw bed.

## Clinical signs

First one or more claws separate and slough. Within two to three months claws are lost on **all four feet**.

- The exposed claw bed is painful and may bleed.
- Dogs may be lame.
- Secondary bacterial infection is common.

The regrowing claws are brittle, crumbling and misshapen (onychodystrophy). They may slough again.

## Diagnosis

- Typical history and symmetrical involvement of multiple claws.
- Exclusion of other dermatological causes of onychomadesis. Bacterial culture and sensitivity testing are recommended.
- **Histopathology** of a claw biopsy. It shows a band-like (lichenoid) subepidermal infiltrate and supports the diagnosis.

## Treatment

In the acute phase, loose claws are removed under anaesthesia and secondary infections are treated.

Long-term therapy relies on **omega-3 fatty acids**, in the diet or as a supplement. They are used alone or with immunomodulating drugs:

- tetracycline with niacinamide
- pentoxifylline
- ciclosporin
- glucocorticoids

Because claws grow slowly, the response is judged over at least **3 to 4 months**.

Many dogs need lifelong treatment, but most regain functional, if imperfect, claws.
