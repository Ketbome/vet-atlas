---
title: Symmetric lupoid onychodystrophy
summary: Immune-mediated disease of the claw-forming tissue that makes the claws slough one after another on all four feet. The claws that regrow are brittle and misshapen.
---

## What it is

Symmetric lupoid onychodystrophy (also called symmetrical onychomadesis) appears to be the most common immune-mediated claw disease of the dog. An **immune-mediated** inflammation attacks the tissue that produces the claw, at the junction between the dermis and the epidermis of the claw base. The claws separate from the underlying dermis and fall off (**onychomadesis**), and the new claws grow abnormal. The trigger is unknown; a hereditary predisposition, linked to certain DLA class II haplotypes, has been shown in some breeds.

## Who it affects

Young adult and middle-aged dogs, usually between about 3 and 8 years of age. Predisposed breeds include the **German shepherd**, Gordon setter, English setter, giant schnauzer, bearded collie and Rhodesian ridgeback, but it is described in many other breeds.

## Anatomy involved

The **claw** (*unguicula*) is a horny capsule that covers the **ungual process of the distal phalanx**. In this disease the inflammation forms a band beneath the epidermis of the claw bed, mainly along the dorsal aspect of the claw, and the horn plate separates from the claw bed.

## Clinical signs

Separation and sloughing of one or more claws, followed within two to three months by loss of claws on **all four feet**. The exposed claw bed is painful and may bleed, and dogs may be lame. Secondary bacterial infection is common. The regrowing claws are brittle, crumbling and misshapen (onychodystrophy), and may slough again.

## Diagnosis

- Typical history and symmetrical involvement of multiple claws.
- Exclusion of other dermatological causes of onychomadesis; bacterial culture and sensitivity testing are recommended.
- **Histopathology** of a claw biopsy shows a band-like (lichenoid) subepidermal infiltrate and supports the diagnosis.

## Treatment

In the acute phase, loose claws are removed under anaesthesia and secondary infections treated. Long-term therapy relies on **omega-3 fatty acids** (diet or supplement), alone or with immunomodulating drugs such as tetracycline with niacinamide, pentoxifylline, ciclosporin or glucocorticoids. Because claws grow slowly, the response is judged over at least 3 to 4 months. Many dogs need lifelong treatment, but most regain functional, if imperfect, claws.
