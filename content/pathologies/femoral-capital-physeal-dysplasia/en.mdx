---
title: Femoral capital physeal dysplasia
summary: Spontaneous slipping of the femoral head epiphysis through a physis that has failed to close on time, without previous trauma. Typical of young, neutered, overweight male cats; it is often bilateral.
---

## What it is

Femoral capital physeal dysplasia, also called **slipped capital femoral epiphysis** or spontaneous capital physeal fracture, is a separation of the femoral head from the neck through the growth cartilage (**capital physis**) without significant trauma. In these cats the physis is **thickened and disorganised**, with irregular clusters of chondrocytes instead of the normal orderly columns, and it stays open longer than usual. **Early neutering**, which is associated with delayed physeal closure, and **excess body weight** are thought to play a role; over time the epiphysis slips and the femoral neck is resorbed.

## Who it affects

Young cats, usually **neutered males** that are **overweight**, from about 4–5 months to two years of age. Some breeds, such as the Maine Coon, British Shorthair, Bengal and Siamese, are at higher risk. It affects both hips in about a third of cats.

## Anatomy involved

- **Proximal femur**: the head epiphysis separates from the neck through the capital physis; the femoral neck undergoes **resorption** (osteolysis) and sclerosis, which increase the longer the signs last.
- **Hip joint**: secondary osteoarthritis develops.

## Clinical signs

Sudden, usually non-weight-bearing **lameness** of a pelvic limb without a history of trauma, or a chronic weight-bearing lameness; some cats only appear less active, hesitate to jump or show pain when touched around the hindquarters.

## Diagnosis

- **Orthopaedic examination**: hip pain.
- **Ventrodorsal and frog-leg radiographs** of both hips: displacement of the capital epiphysis, irregular widening of the physis and, in chronic cases, **resorption and sclerosis of the femoral neck**. Other physes should be checked too, as they may remain open at ages when they should be closed.
- **Histopathology** of the excised head confirms the physeal dysplasia.

## Treatment

- **Femoral head and neck excision arthroplasty**: the most common treatment; the prognosis is usually good, although the hip has a reduced range of motion and some mild lameness may remain.
- **Total hip replacement**: an alternative in centres experienced with cats.
- **Primary fixation** of the epiphysis with pins or screws: possible when there is little neck resorption; in chronic cases or with poor bone quality it may not be advisable or carries more complications.

Body weight is also controlled and the contralateral hip monitored, as its physis is often still open and it may become affected later. The functional prognosis is good.

## Key studies

- **McNicholas et al. (2002)**, a retrospective study of 26 cats older than one year with spontaneous capital physeal fractures. Mean age was 22.5 months, 25 of the 26 were neutered males, and they weighed significantly more than age- and sex-matched controls. Of the 16 cats with a known age at neutering, 14 had been neutered before 6 months. Nine had bilateral fractures, and other physes that should already have closed were still open (the distal femoral physis in 19 of 24 and the proximal tibial physis in 24 of 24). On histology, the growth cartilage had lost its normal columnar arrangement.
- **Borak et al. (2017)**, 17 Maine Coon cats from an Austrian clinic. Slipped capital femoral epiphysis was diagnosed in 8.2% of the Maine Coons seen (17 of 208) versus 0.67% of all cats (29 of 4348), about 12 times more. 16 of the 17 were male, with a mean weight of 7.5 kg; 7 had both hips affected and four were littermates. It is a single-centre series.
- **Butts et al. (2023)**, a radiographic measurement study of 28 affected cats and 5 controls, each read twice by five observers. Excluding fully displaced epiphyses, the S-sign on the frog-leg view was the most accurate (92.4%) and sensitive (93.9%) method, while Klein's line detected only 30.6% of cases. The control group is small.
- **Vink et al. (2022)**, 39 cats (46 fractures) stabilised with cortical positional screws. 45 of the 46 fractures showed signs of healing and 35 of the 39 cats walked normally at 6 weeks, although femoral neck osteolysis and osteoarthritis increased on radiographs and 7 of 25 owners reported a gait abnormality in the longer term. The authors considered it most successful in cats with mild preoperative neck osteolysis, although osteolysis was not associated with clinical outcome.
- **Rodiño Tilve et al. (2022)**, a multicentre registry of 44 cats with 56 total hip replacements. Slipped capital femoral epiphysis was the most common indication (34 of 56 hips). There were 11 complications in 56 hips, 9 of them major, and owners rated the outcome as very good in 30 of 33 cases after a median follow-up of 752 days.
