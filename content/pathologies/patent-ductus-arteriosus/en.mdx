## What it is

In the fetus the *ductus arteriosus* connects the pulmonary trunk to the descending aorta and carries blood past the non-ventilated lungs. It normally closes within the first days after birth and becomes the *ligamentum arteriosum*. In PDA it fails to close.

Aortic pressure exceeds pulmonary pressure, so blood flows continuously from the aorta into the pulmonary artery. This is a **left-to-right shunt**. The blood recirculates through the lungs and returns to the left atrium and ventricle, which dilate from volume overload.

A small minority develop severe pulmonary hypertension and the flow reverses. This is **right-to-left**, or "reversed", PDA.

## Who it affects

It is present from birth and is usually detected at the first puppy examinations. It is most common in small breeds and in **females**.

Predisposed breeds include:

- miniature and toy poodle
- Pomeranian
- German shepherd
- Border collie
- Shetland sheepdog
- Maltese
- bichon frise
- Yorkshire terrier
- cocker spaniel

## Anatomy involved

- **Ductus arteriosus**. Runs from the pulmonary trunk, near its bifurcation, to the descending aorta, on the left side of the base of the heart. The left vagus nerve passes over it. The left recurrent laryngeal nerve leaves the vagus just cranial to the ductus and loops around its caudal aspect. Both nerves are landmarks during ligation.
- **Aorta and pulmonary trunk**. The aorta is dilated up to the origin of the ductus, and the pulmonary trunk is dilated too.
- **Left atrium and ventricle**. They dilate from volume overload. Secondary mitral regurgitation may be present.
- **Lungs**. Pulmonary overcirculation and, when the heart fails, pulmonary oedema.

## Clinical signs

The typical sign is a **continuous murmur**, the "machinery" murmur, loudest at the left heart base. There is often a palpable thrill and hyperkinetic, bounding femoral pulses.

Many puppies show no signs at first. Without treatment, **left-sided congestive heart failure** develops in a large proportion of dogs, often within the first year.

Reversed PDA has no continuous murmur. It causes instead:

- **differential cyanosis**, with cyanotic caudal mucous membranes and pink cranial ones
- pelvic limb weakness on exercise
- polycythaemia

## Diagnosis

- **Auscultation**. A continuous left basal murmur in a young dog is highly suggestive.
- **Thoracic radiographs**. Left-sided cardiomegaly (left ventricle and atrium), a "ductal bump" of the aorta and pulmonary overcirculation, or pulmonary oedema if there is heart failure.
- **Echocardiography with Doppler**. Shows the ductus and continuous turbulent flow into the pulmonary artery. It measures the ductus for occluder sizing and looks for other congenital defects. A saline bubble study confirms reversal.
- **Angiography**. Performed during catheter-based closure.

## Treatment

### Left-to-right PDA

Closure is recommended as soon as possible, by one of two techniques:

- **surgical ligation**, through a left 4th intercostal thoracotomy
- minimally invasive **transarterial occlusion**, with a device (canine ductal occluder) or coils

After closure most dogs have a normal life expectancy.

### Reversed PDA

Reversed PDA must **not** be closed. Closure would worsen pulmonary hypertension and cause right-sided heart failure.

It is managed with periodic phlebotomy or hydroxyurea for polycythaemia, and with drugs for pulmonary hypertension.

<details>
<summary>In the cat</summary>

In the cat patent ductus arteriosus is less common than in the dog. Ventricular septal defect and atrioventricular valve dysplasias predominate among feline congenital heart defects. PDA can coexist with other defects.

### Signs

The picture is similar to the dog's but often less typical. Many cats have no signs when the defect is found.

The **murmur** at the left heart base is continuous in only about half of cats, and systolic in the rest. There may be bounding femoral pulses.

Without treatment, left-sided heart failure develops, which in the cat shows as:

- abnormal breathing
- lethargy or exercise intolerance
- stunted growth and poor weight gain

### Diagnosis and treatment

Doppler echocardiography confirms the diagnosis.

Closure is done mainly by **surgical ligation** through a left thoracotomy. The small size of feline vessels limits the use of occlusion devices designed for dogs. The prognosis after closure is good.

Pulmonary hypertension and other cardiac defects are common in cats with PDA. Reversed PDA is less frequent but not exceptional, and it must not be closed either.

<details>
<summary>Key studies in the cat</summary>

- **Schrope (2015).** Records of 57,025 mixed-breed cats. The prevalence of congenital heart disease was 0.14%. Ventricular septal defect was the most common, followed by aortic stenosis and obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.
- **Tidholm et al. (2015).** 162 cats with congenital heart defects at two referral centres in Sweden. Patent ductus arteriosus accounted for 3% of cats, far behind ventricular septal defect (50%).
- **Bascuñán et al. (2017).** Retrospective study of 50 cats from five referral hospitals, a large series for this rare defect. Median age at presentation was 6 months, with no sex difference, and 70.2% had no signs. The murmur was continuous in only 55% and systolic in the rest. 17.5% had right-to-left shunting, 54.5% had other cardiac anomalies and 45.7% had pulmonary hypertension. Closure was performed in 68%, with complications in 14.7% (one intraoperative death), and the long-term outcome after closure was favourable. Because it is retrospective and from referral centres, it may overrepresent complex cases.
