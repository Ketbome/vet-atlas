---
title: Nutritional myodegeneration
summary: Necrosis of skeletal muscle and myocardium due to deficiency of selenium, vitamin E or both, known as white muscle disease. It mainly affects young calves and can cause stiffness, recumbency, respiratory distress or sudden death.
---

## What it is

Nutritional myodegeneration (**white muscle disease**, nutritional muscular dystrophy) is a necrosis of muscle fibres caused by deficiency of **selenium**, **vitamin E** or both. Selenium is part of the enzyme **glutathione peroxidase** and vitamin E is a membrane antioxidant; together they protect cells from peroxide damage. When they are lacking, free radicals damage the membranes of muscle fibres, which necrose and mineralise, and the affected muscle looks **pale, with white streaks** and a chalky appearance.

Selenium deficiency occurs in areas with **selenium-poor soils**, whose forage is also poor in selenium; vitamin E deficiency, with poor-quality or long-stored conserved forage. Unaccustomed exercise (for example, turnout to pasture in spring), rapid growth and dietary **polyunsaturated fatty acids**, which increase oxidative stress, can trigger outbreaks.

## Who it affects

Mainly **calves** from birth to a few months of age, born to deficient cows; also young cattle housed over winter when turned out to pasture. There is a **congenital** form in stillborn or weak calves. It affects beef and dairy herds in regions with deficient soils if they are not supplemented.

## Anatomy involved

- **Muscles of the rump and thigh**: the large muscles of the pelvic limb, such as the **semimembranosus** and **quadriceps femoris**, are often affected, bilaterally and symmetrically; they are swollen and firm on palpation, with white streaks on cut section.
- **Heart**: in the **cardiac form** there are white plaques or streaks in the myocardium, preferentially in the **left ventricle** and papillary muscles; it causes acute heart failure and sudden death.
- **Diaphragm**: together with the intercostal muscles, its necrosis causes **respiratory distress**, which can be mistaken for pneumonia.
- The tongue (difficulty suckling), neck muscles and muscles of the shoulder can also be affected.

## Clinical signs

- **Cardiac form**: calves that die suddenly, sometimes after handling or exercise, or that show weakness, tachycardia, arrhythmias and dyspnoea.
- **Skeletal form**: stiffness, a stiff gait, tremors, weakness and **recumbency**, with the animal still alert and willing to eat. The muscles are firm and painful. Urine may be dark from **myoglobinuria**.
- **Respiratory form**: tachypnoea and abdominal breathing from involvement of the diaphragm and intercostal muscles.
- In the congenital form, weak calves at birth that cannot stand or suckle.

## Diagnosis

- **Muscle enzymes**: markedly raised **creatine kinase (CK)** indicates active muscle necrosis; aspartate aminotransferase (AST) stays raised for longer.
- **Selenium and vitamin E status**: whole-blood selenium or blood **glutathione peroxidase** activity, and serum α-tocopherol, in the affected animal and in other animals of the herd; liver selenium at necropsy.
- **Necropsy and histopathology**: pale muscles with white streaks, often symmetrical, and hyaline necrosis with mineralisation of the fibres.
- Differential diagnosis: pneumonia, trauma, neurological diseases and other causes of recumbency or sudden death in calves.

## Treatment and prevention

- **Parenteral selenium and vitamin E** for affected animals and the other animals at risk in the group; rest, minimal stress and supportive care. The skeletal form responds well if treated early; the cardiac form has a poor prognosis.
- **Prevention** in deficient areas: supplementation of cows during pregnancy (minerals in the ration or in blocks, ruminal boluses, organic selenium or injections), and of calves at birth when the risk is high; vitamin E in diets poor in it.
- Selenium has a **narrow safety margin** and is toxic in excess: several sources of supplementation should not be stacked without checking the herd's status.
- Drug and supplement use in food-producing animals is subject to **national regulations** (including limits on selenium in feed) and to **withdrawal periods** for milk and meat.

## Key studies

No controlled trials with clinical disease as the outcome were found; the evidence on prevention comes mainly from observational studies and from trials that measure selenium or vitamin E status.

- **Kennedy et al. (1987)**, controlled experimental study of 14 calves fed a diet low in vitamin E and selenium and 10 supplemented controls. Depleted calves showed falling α-tocopherol and glutathione peroxidase and rising CK, with mild myopathic lesions in the 6 that were killed, but no clinical signs. Adding **polyunsaturated fatty acids** to the diet of 8 depleted calves produced cardiopulmonary and locomotor signs and myoglobinuria within 6–11 days, with severe skeletal myodegeneration in all 8 and myocardial lesions in 5, mainly in the **left ventricle**. Controls had no lesions. It is a small experimental study.
- **Sanguinetti et al. (2023)**, systematic review of measures to reduce preweaning mortality in beef calves, with 11 studies (10 cross-sectional and 1 randomised trial). **Injecting selenium and vitamin E at birth** was one of the practices significantly associated with calf mortality; the abstract does not give the size of the effect and the evidence is mainly observational.
- **Ceballos et al. (2009)**, systematic review and meta-analysis of 42 studies of oral selenium supplementation in cattle. Supplementation raised milk selenium by an average of 0.16 µmol/L (95% CI, 0.117–0.207), with large heterogeneity between studies; organic selenium (yeast) raised it more than inorganic forms. It measures selenium status, not disease.
- **Swecker et al. (2008)**, three trials with 144 weaned steers given selenium, vitamin E or both subcutaneously. Selenium raised serum selenium, but **injected vitamin E did not raise serum vitamin E** in any of the trials, and no treatment improved weight gain.
