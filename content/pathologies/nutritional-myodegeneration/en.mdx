## What it is

Nutritional myodegeneration, also called **white muscle disease** or nutritional muscular dystrophy, is a necrosis of muscle fibres caused by deficiency of **selenium**, **vitamin E** or both.

Selenium is part of the enzyme **glutathione peroxidase**, and vitamin E is a membrane antioxidant. Together they protect cells from peroxide damage.

When they are lacking, free radicals damage the membranes of muscle fibres, which necrose and mineralise. The affected muscle looks **pale, with white streaks** and a chalky appearance.

### Causes

- Selenium deficiency occurs in areas with **selenium-poor soils**, whose forage is also poor in selenium.
- Vitamin E deficiency occurs with poor-quality or long-stored conserved forage.

Rapid growth and dietary **polyunsaturated fatty acids**, which increase oxidative stress, can precipitate the disease.

## Who it affects

Mainly **calves** from birth to a few months of age. Their dams usually ate selenium-deficient diets during gestation.

It has also been reported in yearling and adult cattle. It affects beef and dairy herds in regions with deficient soils if they are not supplemented.

## Anatomy involved

- **Locomotor muscles**. The large muscles of the limbs are affected bilaterally and symmetrically. They are firm and painful on palpation, with white streaks, calcification and oedema on cut section.
- **Heart**. In the cardiac form there are white plaques or streaks in the myocardium, in calves mainly in the **left ventricle** and the interventricular septum. It causes acute heart failure and sudden death.
- **Diaphragm**. Together with the intercostal muscles, its necrosis causes **respiratory distress**, which can be mistaken for pneumonia.
- **Tongue**. It can also be affected, causing dysphagia and difficulty suckling.

## Clinical signs

### Cardiac form

Calves found dead, or with respiratory distress and arrhythmias. Death often follows **within 24 hours** despite treatment.

### Skeletal form

- stiffness and weakness
- difficulty rising and **recumbency**; most animals can stand only for short periods
- firm, painful muscles
- dark urine from **myoglobinuria**

Mental status is normal.

### Respiratory form

Respiratory distress with increased abdominal effort, from involvement of the respiratory muscles.

## Diagnosis

- **Muscle enzymes**. Raised **creatine kinase (CK)**, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and lactate dehydrogenase support the diagnosis.
- **Selenium and vitamin E status**. Whole-blood selenium or blood glutathione peroxidase activity, and serum α-tocopherol, are measured in the affected animal and in other animals of the herd. At necropsy, liver selenium is measured.
- **Necropsy and histopathology**. Pale muscles with white streaks, often symmetrical. Coagulation necrosis with hypercontracted, fragmented and mineralised fibres.

**Low whole-blood and liver selenium** give the definitive diagnosis.

### Differential diagnosis

- pneumonia
- trauma
- neurological diseases
- other causes of recumbency or sudden death in calves

## Treatment and prevention

**Parenteral selenium and vitamin E** are given to affected animals and to the other animals at risk in the group, with rest, minimal stress and supportive care.

The skeletal form responds well if treated early. The cardiac form has a poor prognosis.

### Prevention

In deficient areas, cows are supplemented during pregnancy, with minerals in the ration or in blocks, ruminal boluses, organic selenium or injections. When the risk is high, calves are also supplemented at birth. Diets poor in vitamin E are supplemented with it.

Selenium has a **narrow safety margin** and is toxic in excess. Several sources of supplementation should not be stacked without checking the herd's status.

Drug and supplement use in food-producing animals is subject to national regulations, including limits on selenium in feed, and to withdrawal periods for milk and meat.

<summary>Key studies</summary>

No controlled trials with clinical disease as the outcome were found. The evidence on prevention comes mainly from observational studies and from trials that measure selenium or vitamin E status.

- **Kennedy et al. (1987).** Controlled experimental study of 14 calves fed a diet low in vitamin E and selenium and 10 supplemented controls. Depleted calves showed falling α-tocopherol and glutathione peroxidase and rising CK, with mild myopathic lesions in the 6 that were killed, but no clinical signs. Adding **polyunsaturated fatty acids** to the diet of 8 depleted calves produced cardiopulmonary and locomotor signs and myoglobinuria within 6–11 days, with severe skeletal myodegeneration in all 8 and myocardial lesions in 5, mainly in the **left ventricle**. Controls had no lesions. It is a small experimental study.
- **Sanguinetti et al. (2023).** Systematic review of measures to reduce preweaning mortality in beef calves, with 11 studies (10 cross-sectional and 1 randomised trial). **Injecting selenium and vitamin E at birth** was one of the practices significantly associated with calf mortality. The abstract does not give the size of the effect and the evidence is mainly observational.
- **Ceballos et al. (2009).** Systematic review and meta-analysis of 42 studies of oral selenium supplementation in cattle. Supplementation raised milk selenium by an average of 0.16 µmol/L (95% CI, 0.117–0.207), with large heterogeneity between studies; organic selenium (yeast) raised it more than inorganic forms. It measures selenium status, not disease.
- **Swecker et al. (2008).** Three trials with 144 weaned steers given selenium, vitamin E or both subcutaneously. Selenium raised serum selenium, but **injected vitamin E did not raise serum vitamin E** in any of the trials, and no treatment improved weight gain.

</details>
