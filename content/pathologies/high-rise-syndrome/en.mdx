## What it is

High-rise syndrome is the characteristic pattern of injuries in cats that fall from a height of at least two storeys.

During the fall, the cat uses its **righting reflex** to rotate its body and point its feet toward the ground. On impact the extended limbs absorb part of the energy. The rest is transmitted to the thorax and head, which strike the ground.

Hence the typical combination of injuries:

- **thoracic trauma**, the most common: pneumothorax and pulmonary contusion, sometimes rib fractures or diaphragmatic hernia
- **facial trauma**: epistaxis, midline **fracture of the hard palate**, **mandibular symphyseal separation**, dental fractures and facial abrasions
- **limb injuries**: fractures of the thoracic and pelvic limbs, luxations and injuries of the tarsus and digits

Some studies found that injury severity peaks at intermediate heights and falls at greater heights. This curvilinear pattern is debated: the largest series found that severity rose steadily with fall height.

## Who it affects

Young cats in urban settings with access to unprotected windows, balconies or terraces. There are more cases in warm months, when windows are open.

Falls usually happen while the cat chases insects or birds, or slips from a railing.

## Anatomy involved

### Lungs and pleural space

Pulmonary contusion and pneumothorax.

### Skull and mandible

The hard palate splits along the median suture and the **mandibular symphysis** separates in the midline. Fractures of the mandible and of the teeth also occur.

### Thoracic limbs

Fractures and luxations, less often than in the pelvic limbs.

### Pelvic limbs

Fractures of the tibia and femur, and **tarsal injuries**: fractures and luxations.

## Clinical signs

- dyspnoea and tachypnoea
- shock
- epistaxis and blood in the mouth
- malocclusion or inability to close the mouth
- lameness or limb deformity

## Diagnosis

- First, stabilisation and primary survey: airway, breathing and circulation. **Point-of-care thoracic ultrasound** detects pneumothorax.
- Thoracic radiographs once the cat tolerates them, to look for pneumothorax, contusion, rib fractures or diaphragmatic hernia.
- Oral examination, often under sedation, and CT of the head for palatal and mandibular fractures.
- Limb radiographs and abdominal ultrasound to rule out bladder and other organ injuries.

## Treatment

Stabilisation comes first: oxygen, **thoracocentesis** for pneumothorax, analgesia and treatment of shock.

Fractures are repaired once the cat is stable:

- mandibular symphyseal separation, with cerclage wire around the rostral mandible
- hard palate fracture, with suturing or flaps when needed, because untreated it can leave an **oronasal fistula**
- limb fractures, with internal or external fixation depending on the bone and fracture configuration

Most cats that reach the clinic alive survive with treatment.

Prevention is simple: sturdy **nets or screens** on windows and balconies.

<summary>Key studies</summary>

- **Whitney and Mehlhaff (1987).** 132 cats seen in New York over 5 months. 90% had some thoracic trauma; among them, 68% had pulmonary contusion and 63% pneumothorax. Facial trauma was present in 57%, limb fractures in 39% and hard palate fracture in 17%. 37% needed emergency treatment, and 90% of the treated cats survived.
- **Vnuk et al. (2004).** 119 cats in Zagreb. 59.6% were under one year old, the average fall was four storeys and 96.5% survived. 46.2% had limb fractures, more often of the pelvic limbs (61.5%), and the tibia was the bone most often fractured; thoracic trauma was found in 33.6%. Falls from the seventh storey or higher were associated with more severe injuries.
- **Bonner et al. (2012).** 84 cats in Philadelphia, with a mean fall of 2.65 storeys. 66% had some orofacial injury: epistaxis, hard palate fracture, mandibular fracture or symphyseal separation, and dental and tongue injuries. Survival was 94%; one untreated palatal fracture ended in an oronasal fistula.
- **Candela Andrade et al. (2025).** The largest series, published in two parts: 1125 cats treated in Berlin between 2004 and 2013. Mean age was 2.3 years; 77% of falls happened in summer, 62.1% at night and 74.2% onto hard surfaces. Thoracic trauma was found in 58.3%, orofacial injuries in 51.1%, limb fractures in 47.2% and shock in 48.6%, and 87% survived. Injury severity correlated with age, landing surface and fall height. It is retrospective and from a single centre.
