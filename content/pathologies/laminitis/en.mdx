## What it is

Laminitis is a lesion of the **laminae** of the hoof, the tissue that joins the horny wall to the **third phalanx** (distal phalanx).

The epidermal laminae on the inner face of the wall interdigitate with the dermal laminae covering the bone. This attachment carries the horse's weight and the pull of the deep digital flexor tendon.

When the laminae are damaged, the attachment weakens and the distal phalanx can:

- **rotate**: its tip moves away from the wall and down towards the sole
- **sink** inside the hoof

In severe cases the bone penetrates the sole.

### Forms by cause

- **Endocrinopathic**, the most common. It is associated with the **insulin dysregulation** of **equine metabolic syndrome** and with **pituitary pars intermedia dysfunction** (PPID, formerly equine "Cushing's disease"). Hyperinsulinaemia, often after access to sugar-rich pasture, triggers the laminar lesion.
- **Associated with sepsis or the systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS)**. It complicates colitis, enteritis, metritis, pleuropneumonia or grain overload.
- **Supporting limb laminitis**. It develops in the sound limb when the horse does not bear weight on the opposite limb for a long time because of a fracture or any other painful unilateral lameness. It is attributed to the loss of the normal cycles of loading and unloading that maintain laminar blood flow.

## Who it affects

Horses and ponies of any age. Risk is higher in:

- **ponies**, especially British native breeds
- **obese** animals or those with regional fat deposits, such as a cresty neck

The endocrinopathic form often follows weight gain or new access to grass.

Chronic laminitis is associated with increasing **age**. PPID testing is especially advised in horses over 10 years old.

The SIRS-associated form affects horses hospitalised with severe disease. The supporting limb form mainly affects heavy horses with prolonged unilateral lameness.

## Anatomy involved

### Hooves (*ungulae*)

The **wall** is attached to the distal phalanx by the epidermal laminae, which interlock with the dermal laminae of the bone. The **sole**, **frog** and **coronary band** complete the hoof capsule.

When the phalanx is displaced:

- the coronary band sinks
- the wall grows divergently, with rings wider apart at the heel than at the toe
- the **white line** widens
- the sole becomes flat or convex

### Phalanges of the thoracic and pelvic limbs

The **distal phalanx** (third phalanx) is the bone that rotates or sinks. The **deep digital flexor tendon** inserts on it, pulls it backwards and favours rotation.

The thoracic limbs are affected more often and more severely. Supporting limb laminitis can affect any limb.

## Clinical signs

**Lameness** of variable severity, usually in both thoracic limbs, although any combination of limbs is possible.

The stance is typical: the horse shifts its weight back, places the thoracic limbs forward to unload the toes and is reluctant to walk or turn. At the walk it takes short, stiff steps and has difficulty turning.

- bounding **digital pulses**
- a hoof that may be warm
- pain with hoof testers over the sole or the apex of the frog

Severity is graded with the **Obel** scale.

In chronic laminitis there are divergent rings on the wall, a depression at the coronary band and a flat sole. If the phalanx penetrates the sole, pus drains or the bone itself is exposed.

## Diagnosis

- **Clinical examination**: stance, digital pulses, response to hoof testers and Obel grade.
- **Radiographs**, lateromedial and dorsopalmar views of both feet. They measure rotation, sinking of the distal phalanx and sole thickness, and serve as a reference for shoeing and follow-up.
- **Endocrine testing** in every case without an obvious inflammatory cause: **basal insulin** or insulin after an oral sugar test for insulin dysregulation, and basal **ACTH** (or ACTH after TRH stimulation) for PPID.

If only the hindlimbs are affected, laminitis can be mistaken for a neurological problem.

## Treatment and prevention

### Acute phase

- rest on deep bedding
- **frog and sole support** to spread the load: pads, impression material, therapeutic shoes
- analgesia with **non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs** and other analgesics
- treatment of the cause

### Digital cryotherapy

Continuous cooling of the feet during the developmental and acute stages, especially in horses at risk of sepsis-associated laminitis, such as those with colitis.

It is the most effective measure to reduce laminar damage. The evidence comes from experimental models and retrospective studies.

### Endocrinopathic laminitis

- a **diet low in non-structural carbohydrates**: low-sugar hay, often soaked, with no cereals or sugary treats
- restricted or no grazing, for example with a grazing muzzle
- **weight loss** in obese animals
- exercise
- **pergolide** for PPID

The aim is to control insulin, because that is what predicts recurrence.

### Supporting limb laminitis

The risk rises with body weight and with the time the horse spends without loading the injured limb, for example longer periods in a cast. The supporting foot must be watched closely in these horses.

### Chronic cases

Corrective trimming and shoeing guided by radiographs, sometimes with deep digital flexor tenotomy. Euthanasia on welfare grounds is common when pain cannot be controlled.

### Regulations

The horse is a food-producing animal in many countries, and drug use is subject to **national regulations** and **withdrawal periods**.

In the EU, the equine identification document (passport) states whether the horse is excluded from slaughter for human consumption, and this decides which medicines may be given.

<summary>Key studies</summary>

- **Wylie et al. (2013a).** Prospective cohort study of veterinary-diagnosed laminitis in Great Britain, with 577 cases. The prevalence of active laminitis was 0.49% (95% CI 0.43–0.55%) of horses registered with practices and the incidence 0.5 cases per 100 horse-years. Both forelimbs were most often affected (53.5%), and the most common signs were increased digital pulses, difficulty turning and a short, stilted gait at the walk. The figures are much lower than earlier estimates, partly because of a different case definition.
- **Wylie et al. (2013b).** Case-control study with 191 cases of active laminitis and 819 controls from the same population. Risk was increased by **weight gain** in the previous 3 months, **new access to grass** in the previous 4 weeks, box rest in the previous week, a history of laminitis, lameness after trimming or shoeing and existing **endocrine disease** (PPID or equine metabolic syndrome); greater height was associated with lower risk. It is observational and based on owner questionnaires.
- **Knowles et al. (2023).** Prospective cohort of 374 non-laminitic ponies followed for up to 4 years (891 pony-years). Incidence was 4.8 cases per 100 pony-years. **Basal insulin** and insulin 60 minutes after an oral sugar test were the best predictors: with high basal insulin (above 45.2 µIU/ml), 10% of the population, the estimated 4-year incidence was 69%, compared with 6% with low insulin. ACTH was not independently associated. The cut-offs depend on the insulin assay.
- **de Laat et al. (2019).** Prospective cohort of 317 horses and ponies with acute laminitis, of which 276 endocrinopathic cases were followed for 2 years. Laminitis **recurred in 34.1%**. The risk of recurrence increased with basal fasted insulin, rising markedly once insulin exceeded the normal range, and with a previous diagnosis of laminitis; cases with a higher Obel grade recurred sooner. Cases were classified from questionnaires completed by the horses' veterinarians.
- **ECEIM consensus statement on equine metabolic syndrome (Durham et al., 2019).** It defines the syndrome as a collection of risk factors for endocrinopathic laminitis, of which **insulin dysregulation** is the most important. It recommends basal or dynamic insulin testing and control based on diet and exercise, with drugs only as an aid in some cases.
- **Kullmann et al. (2014).** Retrospective study of 130 horses with colitis and a systemic inflammatory response at 2 university hospitals. Laminitis developed in 7 of 69 (10%) treated with **digital cryotherapy** and 20 of 61 (33%) not treated (OR 0.11; 95% CI 0.03–0.44). Only 48% of horses with laminitis survived, compared with 98% of those that did not develop it. It is not a randomised trial: cryotherapy depended on the hospital and the clinician, which may bias the result.
