---
title: Chronic kidney disease
summary: Progressive, irreversible loss of functional nephrons over months to years. It is common in older dogs and causes polyuria and polydipsia, then uraemia, anaemia and hypertension. It is staged with the IRIS system.
---

## What it is

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is defined as structural or functional damage to one or both kidneys present for at least three months. Whatever the initial cause, nephrons are lost and replaced by interstitial fibrosis; the remaining nephrons hypertrophy and hyperfilter, which sustains function for a time but promotes further injury, so the disease tends to progress. Clinical signs appear only when most of the renal mass is lost. Causes in the dog include chronic glomerular disease (protein-losing nephropathies), chronic tubulointerstitial nephritis, pyelonephritis, nephrolithiasis, amyloidosis, familial renal dysplasias and the after-effects of acute kidney injury; in many older dogs the cause is no longer identifiable.

## Who it affects

Mainly older dogs. Familial nephropathies cause CKD in young dogs of certain breeds, for example juvenile renal dysplasia in the shih tzu and Lhasa apso, hereditary nephritis in the English cocker spaniel and Samoyed, and familial glomerulopathies in the soft-coated wheaten terrier and Bernese mountain dog.

## Anatomy involved

- **Kidneys**: usually small, irregular and firm, with loss of corticomedullary distinction; the cortex is thin. Glomerular sclerosis, tubular loss and interstitial fibrosis are seen histologically.
- **Extrarenal effects**: renal secondary hyperparathyroidism (with "rubber jaw" in severe juvenile cases), non-regenerative anaemia from reduced erythropoietin, systemic hypertension that damages the eyes, heart and brain, and uraemic gastritis and oral ulcers.

## Clinical signs

Early: **polyuria and polydipsia**, sometimes only noticed as nocturia. Later: weight loss, decreased appetite, vomiting, lethargy, poor coat, pale mucous membranes, uraemic breath and oral ulcers. Sudden blindness from hypertensive retinal detachment can be the presenting sign.

## Diagnosis

- **Blood work**: increased creatinine and urea and **SDMA** (which rises earlier than creatinine); hyperphosphataemia and non-regenerative anaemia in advanced stages.
- **Urinalysis**: inappropriately low urine specific gravity (isosthenuria); **urine protein:creatinine ratio** to detect proteinuria; urine culture.
- **Blood pressure** measurement.
- **Imaging**: ultrasound shows small, hyperechoic kidneys with poor corticomedullary distinction and detects stones, cysts or obstruction.
- **IRIS staging**: stages 1–4 based on fasting creatinine and SDMA in a stable, hydrated patient, sub-staged by proteinuria and blood pressure.

## Treatment

There is no cure; treatment aims to slow progression and control signs, and is adjusted to the IRIS stage. A **renal diet** (restricted phosphorus, moderate high-quality protein, omega-3 fatty acids) prolongs survival in dogs with azotaemia. Phosphate binders when phosphorus stays high, ACE inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers for proteinuria, amlodipine for hypertension, antiemetics and appetite stimulants, fluid therapy to correct dehydration, and erythropoiesis-stimulating agents for severe anaemia. The prognosis depends on the stage and on proteinuria.
