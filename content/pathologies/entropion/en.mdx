## What it is

Entropion is the inversion of all or part of the **eyelid margin** toward the eye. The hairs of the eyelid skin rub on the cornea and conjunctiva (trichiasis). This causes pain, chronic irritation and corneal damage.

Three forms are recognised:

- **Conformational** or developmental entropion is presumed to be inherited polygenically. It depends on the shape of the skull, the length of the palpebral fissure, the orbital anatomy and folds of facial skin. It is the most frequent inherited eyelid defect in many dog breeds.
- In **spastic** entropion, blepharospasm secondary to another painful eye disease pulls the lid inward. It can then perpetuate itself.
- **Cicatricial** entropion is caused by scarring of the eyelid.

## Who it affects

The conformational form appears mainly in young dogs. In a UK study, the median age at diagnosis was about 2 years.

The breeds with the highest prevalence are:

- **shar-pei**
- chow chow
- Neapolitan mastiff
- Clumber spaniel
- Saint Bernard
- English bulldog

Spaniels, brachycephalic breeds and purebred dogs in general are also at higher risk.

Breeds with very long eyelids may have combined entropion and ectropion ("diamond eye").

## Anatomy involved

Each **eyelid** (*palpebra*) is a fold of skin lined inside by palpebral conjunctiva. It contains the **orbicularis oculi muscle**, a thin, poorly developed fibrous tarsus and the tarsal (meibomian) glands, which open at the margin.

Entropion most often affects the **lower lid**: about three quarters of cases in a UK study. The upper lid and the canthi can also be involved, especially in breeds with heavy upper facial folds such as the shar-pei.

## Clinical signs

- epiphora
- **blepharospasm**
- conjunctivitis
- trichiasis

Chronic cases show corneal scarring and pigmentation, and **corneal ulcers** are common. The rolled-in lid margin is often visible, with the hairs touching the eye.

## Diagnosis

- Clinical examination of the position of the eyelid margins. In spastic entropion, the painful eye disease causing the blepharospasm must be found.
- **Fluorescein** to look for corneal ulcers.
- A search for other causes of irritation or ulceration: distichiasis, ectopic cilia, keratoconjunctivitis sicca.

## Treatment

### Puppies

Temporary **eyelid tacking** sutures or staples evert the lid for 2–3 weeks, and are replaced if needed. They are kept until the head reaches adult size, at about 5–6 months. At that age surgical correction gives the best result.

### Established entropion

It needs surgery, usually a **Hotz-Celsus procedure** or a wedge resection. In breeds with heavy upper facial folds, such as the shar-pei, the facial folds may also need to be tacked.

### Spastic entropion

The cause of pain is removed, with tacking sutures in the meantime. Once the keratitis resolves, surgery may no longer be needed.

Because the conformational form is presumed to be inherited, affected dogs should not be used for breeding.

<details>
<summary>In the cat</summary>

Several situations are recognised:

- In **brachycephalic breeds** (Persian, Exotic) it is linked to the flat face, often with epiphora.
- In young adult entire male **Maine Coons**, the in-turning is associated with excess facial ("jowl") tissue.
- Young cats can have **spastic** entropion, secondary to irritative ocular surface disease: conjunctivitis, corneal ulcers (often due to **feline herpesvirus**) or sequestrum. Continued blepharospasm keeps the lid rolled in.
- In older cats it is **involutional**, from lid laxity or **enophthalmos**, presumed to result from a reduction in orbital tissue.

The lid is corrected with a Hotz-Celsus procedure, ideally combined with lateral canthal closure. The underlying eye disease must also be treated.

Entropion often coexists with **corneal sequestrum**, especially in older cats.

<details>
<summary>Key studies in the cat</summary>

- **Williams and Kim (2009).** 50 cats with entropion at a referral clinic. Sixteen were young (mean 4.1 years) with prior irritative surface disease such as conjunctivitis, ulcers or sequestrum. 26 were older (mean 11.3 years) with involutional entropion, with or without enophthalmos. 5 were Persians and 3 young intact male Maine Coons. One Hotz-Celsus procedure cured most cats, although more eyelid tissue had to be removed than in the dog.
- **White et al. (2012).** 124 cats (200 eyes) operated on for lower eyelid entropion. A single procedure corrected 96% of eyes, and Hotz-Celsus combined with lateral canthal closure succeeded in 99.2%. In geriatric cats, 37% had a corneal sequestrum at the same time. 17% of cats with unilateral entropion developed it later in the other eye if that eye was not operated on prophylactically.
- **Anagrius et al. (2021).** Examination of 64 Persian and 12 Exotic Shorthair show cats, compared with 20 non-pedigree cats. Entropion was found in 32% of the Persians and Exotics, together with stenotic nares (86%) and epiphora (83%). These findings were not common in the non-pedigree cats.
- **Frejlich and Payen (2026).** 392 cats with ulcerative keratitis or corneal sequestrum. Entropion of the lateral lower lid accompanied these lesions more often in non-brachycephalic cats: 38 of 286 of their eyes needed entropion surgery, compared with 3 of 128 eyes of brachycephalic cats.

</details>
