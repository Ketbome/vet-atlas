---
title: Feline eosinophilic granuloma complex
summary: A group of eosinophil-rich skin and oral lesions of the cat, comprising indolent lip ulcer, eosinophilic plaque and eosinophilic granuloma. They are a reaction pattern, most often secondary to allergy, rather than a disease in themselves.
---

## What it is

The feline eosinophilic granuloma complex (EGC) groups three types of lesion that share infiltration by **eosinophils** and can appear alone or together in the same cat:

- **Indolent ulcer** (eosinophilic ulcer, "rodent ulcer"): a well-demarcated ulcer with raised edges on the **upper lip**, often next to the philtrum.
- **Eosinophilic plaque**: raised, red, moist or eroded and **intensely pruritic** plaques on the ventral abdomen and medial thighs, caused partly by licking.
- **Eosinophilic granuloma**: firm, reddish to orange-yellow nodular or linear lesions; the **linear** form runs along the caudal thigh, and they also appear on the **chin** ("fat chin") and in the oral cavity.

It is not a final diagnosis but a **cutaneous reaction pattern**. In most cats the underlying cause is **hypersensitivity**: to flea bites, to food or to environmental allergens (feline atopic skin syndrome). Mosquito bite hypersensitivity causes similar eosinophilic lesions, especially on the ears, although it is often regarded as a separate disease. A genetic predisposition has been suggested in some lines of related cats, and some lesions resolve spontaneously. Secondary bacterial infection is common in plaques and ulcers.

## Who it affects

Cats of any age, usually with an underlying allergic disease; no clear age or breed predisposition has been documented, although lesions have been described in groups of related cats, such as Norwegian Forest cats.

## Anatomy involved

- **Skin and mucosae**: dermis infiltrated by eosinophils, mast cells and other inflammatory cells; in the granuloma, granulomatous inflammation surrounds collagen fibres.
- **Upper lip and mucocutaneous junction**: site of the indolent ulcer.
- **Ventral abdomen and medial thighs**: typical site of plaques.
- **Caudal thighs, chin and oral cavity**: sites of the granuloma.

## Clinical signs

Depending on the lesion type: lip ulcers; moist, intensely pruritic plaques with excessive licking and alopecia; firm nodules or lines; chin swelling; or oral lesions. Other signs of allergy are often present: head and neck pruritus, self-induced alopecia or miliary dermatitis. Regional lymph nodes may be enlarged.

## Diagnosis

- **Clinical appearance** and location of the lesions.
- **Impression cytology**: eosinophils, often with bacteria and neutrophils if there is secondary infection.
- **Biopsy** of atypical or non-responding lesions to rule out **neoplasia**, viral disease and other infections.
- **Search for the underlying allergy**: strict flea control on all animals in the household, an **elimination diet** of 6–8 weeks or longer and, if both are ruled out, a diagnosis of feline atopic syndrome (with allergy testing only to choose immunotherapy).
- Haematology: peripheral eosinophilia may be present.

## Treatment

The key is to **control the cause**: continuous parasite control, a hypoallergenic diet when food allergy is present, and **allergen immunotherapy** or anti-allergic drugs in atopic syndrome. For the lesions: **antibiotics** when cytology shows infection (in a controlled trial, amoxicillin-clavulanate reduced plaques; in lip ulcers it reduced infection, but not their size significantly), **glucocorticoids** (oral prednisolone or methylprednisolone) and, as a steroid-sparing alternative, **ciclosporin**. Depot corticosteroids are best avoided, because they cannot be withdrawn if adverse effects such as diabetes appear. Lesions often recur if the underlying allergy is not controlled, and some are refractory to treatment.

## Key studies

- **Hobi et al. (2011)**, a multicentre study of 502 pruritic cats. Diagnoses were flea bite hypersensitivity (29%), food hypersensitivity (12%), non-flea, non-food hypersensitivity (20%) and other pruritic diseases (24%). None of the four typical lesion patterns, eosinophilic dermatitis among them, was specific to one cause, and food and non-food allergy could not be told apart clinically. Young adult, purebred and female cats were predisposed to the non-flea, non-food form.
- **ICADA diagnostic guidelines (Santoro et al., 2021)**: feline atopic skin syndrome is a clinical diagnosis that requires fleas and flea allergy, other parasites, infections and food allergy to be ruled out first.
- **Wildermuth et al. (2012)**, a **randomised**, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial with 16 cats completing the study. All lesions had bacterial infection on cytology. After 21 days of amoxicillin-clavulanate, plaque size fell by a mean of 96.2%, which did not happen with placebo. In lip ulcers the antibiotic reduced infection, but the reduction in size (42.6%) was not significant. There were very few cats per group.
- **ICADA systematic review of treatment (Mueller et al., 2021)**: it found good evidence of efficacy for systemic glucocorticoids and ciclosporin in feline atopic skin syndrome; limited evidence for topical glucocorticoids, oclacitinib and allergen immunotherapy; and low-to-moderate efficacy for antihistamines, fatty acids and palmitoylethanolamide.
- **King et al. (2012)**, a **randomised**, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of 100 cats with hypersensitivity dermatitis. At 6 weeks, lesions improved by more than 50% in 70% of cats given ciclosporin at 7 mg/kg daily, 47% of those given 2.5 mg/kg and 23% of those on placebo. The most common adverse effects were mild gastrointestinal signs.
