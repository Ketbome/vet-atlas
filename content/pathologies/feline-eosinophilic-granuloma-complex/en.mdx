---
title: Feline eosinophilic granuloma complex
summary: A group of eosinophil-rich skin and oral lesions of the cat, comprising indolent lip ulcer, eosinophilic plaque and eosinophilic granuloma. They are a reaction pattern, almost always secondary to allergy, rather than a disease in themselves.
---

## What it is

The feline eosinophilic granuloma complex (EGC) groups three types of lesion that share infiltration by **eosinophils** and can appear alone or together in the same cat:

- **Indolent ulcer** (eosinophilic ulcer, "rodent ulcer"): a well-demarcated ulcer with raised edges on the **upper lip**, next to the philtrum or opposite the lower canine; usually neither painful nor itchy.
- **Eosinophilic plaque**: raised, red, moist or eroded and **intensely pruritic** plaques on the ventral abdomen and medial thighs, caused partly by licking.
- **Eosinophilic granuloma**: firm, yellow-pink nodular or linear lesions; the **linear** form runs along the caudal thigh, and they also appear on the **chin** ("fat chin"), on the tongue and palate, and on the footpads.

It is not a final diagnosis but a **cutaneous reaction pattern**. In most cats the underlying cause is **hypersensitivity**: to flea bites, to food or to environmental allergens (feline atopic dermatitis, now called feline atopic syndrome). Mosquito bites cause a variant on the bridge of the nose and the ears. A form with genetic predisposition exists in some cat lines, and some lesions in young cats regress spontaneously. Secondary bacterial infection is common in plaques and ulcers.

## Who it affects

Cats of any age, more often young and young adult cats with allergic disease. The linear granuloma and the chin form are common in young cats.

## Anatomy involved

- **Skin and mucosae**: dermis infiltrated by eosinophils, mast cells and other inflammatory cells; in the granuloma, foci of collagen degeneration surrounded by giant cells and macrophages.
- **Upper lip and mucocutaneous junction**: site of the indolent ulcer.
- **Ventral abdomen and medial thighs**: typical site of plaques.
- **Caudal thighs, chin, oral cavity and footpads**: sites of the granuloma.

## Clinical signs

Depending on the lesion type: lip ulcers that may disfigure the lip; moist, intensely pruritic plaques with excessive licking and alopecia; firm nodules or lines; chin swelling; or oral lesions that cause dysphagia and drooling. Other signs of allergy are often present: head and neck pruritus, self-induced alopecia or miliary dermatitis. Regional lymph nodes may be enlarged.

## Diagnosis

- **Clinical appearance** and location of the lesions.
- **Impression cytology**: eosinophils, often with bacteria and neutrophils if there is secondary infection.
- **Biopsy** of atypical or non-responding lesions to rule out neoplasia (especially **squamous cell carcinoma** in lip ulcers and oral lesions) and other infections.
- **Search for the underlying allergy**: strict flea control on all animals in the household, an **elimination diet** of at least eight weeks and, if both are ruled out, a diagnosis of feline atopic syndrome (with allergy testing only to choose immunotherapy).
- Haematology: peripheral eosinophilia may be present.

## Treatment

The key is to **control the cause**: continuous parasite control, a hypoallergenic diet when food allergy is present, and **allergen immunotherapy** or anti-allergic drugs in atopic syndrome. For the lesions: **antibiotics** when cytology shows infection (in a controlled trial, amoxicillin-clavulanate reduced plaques; in lip ulcers it reduced infection, but not their size significantly), **glucocorticoids** (oral prednisolone or methylprednisolone) and, as a steroid-sparing alternative, **ciclosporin**. Depot corticosteroids are avoided because of their adverse effects, such as diabetes. Lesions respond well in most cats, but recur if the underlying allergy is not controlled.

## Key studies

- **Hobi et al. (2011)**, a multicentre study of 502 pruritic cats. Diagnoses were flea bite hypersensitivity (29%), food hypersensitivity (12%), non-flea, non-food hypersensitivity (20%) and other pruritic diseases (24%). None of the four typical lesion patterns, eosinophilic dermatitis among them, was specific to one cause, and food and non-food allergy could not be told apart clinically. Young adult, purebred and female cats were predisposed to the non-flea, non-food form.
- **ICADA diagnostic guidelines (Santoro et al., 2021)**: feline atopic skin syndrome is a clinical diagnosis that requires fleas and flea allergy, other parasites, infections and food allergy to be ruled out first.
- **Wildermuth et al. (2012)**, a **randomised**, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial with 16 cats completing the study. All lesions had bacterial infection on cytology. After 21 days of amoxicillin-clavulanate, plaque size fell by a mean of 96.2%, which did not happen with placebo. In lip ulcers the antibiotic reduced infection, but the reduction in size (42.6%) was not significant. There were very few cats per group.
- **ICADA systematic review of treatment (Mueller et al., 2021)**: it found good evidence of efficacy for systemic glucocorticoids and ciclosporin in feline atopic skin syndrome; limited evidence for topical glucocorticoids, oclacitinib and allergen immunotherapy; and low-to-moderate efficacy for antihistamines, fatty acids and palmitoylethanolamide.
- **King et al. (2012)**, a **randomised**, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of 100 cats with hypersensitivity dermatitis. At 6 weeks, lesions improved by more than 50% in 70% of cats given ciclosporin at 7 mg/kg daily, 47% of those given 2.5 mg/kg and 23% of those on placebo. The most common adverse effects were mild gastrointestinal signs.
