## What it is

Osteochondrosis is a failure of **endochondral ossification** in the growing articular-epiphyseal cartilage.

The cartilage stays abnormally thick. Its deep layers are poorly nourished and become necrotic, and a fissure separates a flap of cartilage from the subchondral bone.

Once a flap forms and the joint becomes inflamed, the condition is called **osteochondritis dissecans (OCD)**. The flap may stay attached, or detach and become a "joint mouse" that can migrate into the bicipital tendon sheath.

The cause is multifactorial:

- genetics
- rapid growth
- excess energy and calcium in the diet
- local mechanical stress

## Who it affects

Young dogs of **large and giant breeds**. Signs usually appear between 4 and 8 months of age.

It is more common in males and may be **bilateral**, even when lameness is seen in only one limb.

The shoulder is one of the joints most often affected. Osteochondrosis also occurs in the elbow, stifle and tarsus.

## Anatomy involved

The lesion sits on the **caudal or caudocentral part of the humeral head** (*caput humeri*), which articulates with the **glenoid cavity of the scapula** (*cavitas glenoidalis*).

Free fragments may attach to the joint capsule, be resorbed, or migrate into the sheath of the tendon of origin of the biceps brachii.

## Clinical signs

Thoracic limb lameness of gradual onset, mild to moderate. It improves with rest and **worsens after exercise**.

Pain and reduced range of motion of the shoulder are the key findings.

## Diagnosis

- Mediolateral **radiographs** of both shoulders. They show flattening or a concave defect of the caudal humeral head, with sclerosis of the subchondral bone; sometimes a mineralised flap or free body. Screen the opposite shoulder.
- Arthrography, CT or arthroscopy, to show a cartilage flap that is not mineralised.

## Treatment

Some dogs improve with rest and controlled exercise.

When lameness persists or a flap is present, the treatment of choice is **removal of the flap and curettage of its bed**, by arthroscopy or arthrotomy. Fibrocartilage then fills the defect.

Joint mice in the bicipital sheath should be removed.

Prognosis after surgery is good, much better than for OCD in the elbow, stifle or hock.

Avoiding overfeeding and excess calcium in growing large-breed puppies reduces the risk.
