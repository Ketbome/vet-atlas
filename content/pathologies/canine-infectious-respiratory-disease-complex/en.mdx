---
title: Canine infectious respiratory disease complex
summary: Contagious respiratory infection of dogs ("kennel cough") caused by one or more viruses and bacteria. Most dogs have a self-limiting tracheobronchitis; a minority develop pneumonia.
---

## What it is

Canine infectious respiratory disease complex (CIRDC), commonly called kennel cough or infectious tracheobronchitis, is an acute, highly contagious infection of the airways. It is multifactorial: several pathogens, often in combination, damage the respiratory epithelium and the mucociliary clearance. The main agents are ***Bordetella bronchiseptica***, **canine parainfluenza virus**, **canine adenovirus type 2**, **canine respiratory coronavirus**, **canine influenza virus** (H3N8, H3N2), **canine herpesvirus**, ***Mycoplasma*** spp. and ***Streptococcus equi* subsp. *zooepidemicus***; canine distemper virus can begin with similar signs. Transmission is by aerosols, direct contact and fomites, and is favoured where many dogs gather.

## Who it affects

Dogs of any age and breed, especially those in shelters, boarding kennels, day-care centres, shows and training classes. Puppies and debilitated or old dogs are more likely to develop severe disease or pneumonia.

## Anatomy involved

- **Trachea and bronchi**: the main site of disease; inflammation of the mucosa causes the cough.
- **Larynx and upper airways**: the cough is easily triggered from the larynx; nasal and ocular discharge and sneezing.
- **Lungs**: secondary bacterial **bronchopneumonia** in complicated cases.

## Clinical signs

After an incubation of a few days to about 10 days: a sudden, loud, harsh cough, often followed by gagging or retching, easily triggered by palpation of the larynx or trachea. Nasal and ocular discharge and sneezing. Most dogs stay bright and eat normally, and signs resolve in one to three weeks. Fever, lethargy, anorexia, tachypnoea and a productive cough suggest pneumonia.

## Diagnosis

- **Clinical**: compatible signs and a history of contact with other dogs are usually enough in uncomplicated cases.
- **Thoracic radiographs**: in dogs with systemic signs, to look for an alveolar pattern.
- **PCR panels** on nasopharyngeal or tracheal swabs or respiratory discharges: identify the agents involved, useful in outbreaks and shelters.
- **Airway lavage with culture and sensitivity**: in pneumonia or refractory cases.

## Treatment

Uncomplicated cases resolve on their own; isolation from other dogs is recommended. Antitussives can be used for a persistent cough when there is no pneumonia. Antibiotics active against *Bordetella* and *Mycoplasma* (doxycycline is a common first choice) are reserved for dogs with mucopurulent discharge together with fever, lethargy or inappetence, or with suspected pneumonia. Pneumonia may need hospitalisation, parenteral antibiotics and oxygen. Prevention relies on vaccination (parenteral and mucosal vaccines against *Bordetella*, parainfluenza, adenovirus and, where relevant, influenza), ventilation and hygiene in kennels.
