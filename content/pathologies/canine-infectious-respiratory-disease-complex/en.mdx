## What it is

Canine infectious respiratory disease complex (CIRDC), commonly called kennel cough or infectious tracheobronchitis, is an acute, highly contagious infection of the airways.

It is multifactorial. Several pathogens, often in combination, damage the respiratory epithelium and the mucociliary clearance. The main agents are:

- ***Bordetella bronchiseptica***
- **canine parainfluenza virus**
- **canine adenovirus type 2**
- **canine respiratory coronavirus**
- **canine influenza virus**, H3N8 and H3N2
- **canine herpesvirus**
- ***Mycoplasma*** spp.
- ***Streptococcus equi* subsp. *zooepidemicus***

Canine distemper virus can begin with similar signs.

Transmission is by aerosols, direct contact and fomites, and is favoured where many dogs gather.

## Who it affects

Dogs of any age and breed, especially those in shelters, boarding kennels, day-care centres, shows and training classes.

Puppies and debilitated or old dogs are more likely to develop severe disease or pneumonia.

## Anatomy involved

The trachea and bronchi are the main site of disease. Inflammation of their mucosa causes the cough.

The cough is easily triggered from the larynx. Involvement of the upper airways brings nasal and ocular discharge and sneezing.

In complicated cases, secondary bacterial **bronchopneumonia** develops in the lungs.

## Clinical signs

Incubation lasts from a few days to about 10 days.

The main sign is a sudden, loud, harsh cough, often followed by gagging or retching. It is easily triggered by **palpation of the larynx or trachea**. Nasal and ocular discharge and sneezing go with it.

Most dogs stay bright and eat normally, and signs resolve in one to three weeks.

Fever, lethargy, anorexia, tachypnoea and a productive cough suggest **pneumonia**.

## Diagnosis

- In uncomplicated cases, compatible signs and a history of contact with other dogs are usually enough.
- Dogs with systemic signs get **thoracic radiographs** to look for an alveolar pattern.
- **PCR panels** on nasopharyngeal or tracheal swabs or respiratory discharges identify the agents involved. They are useful in outbreaks and shelters.
- Pneumonia or refractory cases call for **airway lavage** with culture and sensitivity.

## Treatment

Uncomplicated cases resolve on their own. Isolation from other dogs is recommended.

Antitussives can be used for a persistent cough when there is no pneumonia.

Antibiotics active against *Bordetella* and *Mycoplasma* are reserved for dogs with mucopurulent discharge together with fever, lethargy or inappetence, or with suspected pneumonia. **Doxycycline** is a common first choice.

Pneumonia may need hospitalisation, parenteral antibiotics and oxygen.

Prevention relies on:

- **vaccination**, with parenteral and mucosal vaccines against *Bordetella*, parainfluenza, adenovirus and, where relevant, influenza
- ventilation in kennels
- hygiene in kennels
