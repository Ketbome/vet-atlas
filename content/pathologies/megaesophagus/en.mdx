---
title: Megaesophagus
summary: Generalised dilation and loss of motility of the oesophagus. Food and water pool in it and are regurgitated, and aspiration pneumonia is the main complication. It may be congenital or acquired.
---

## What it is

Megaesophagus is a diffuse dilation of the oesophagus with absent or ineffective peristalsis, so that swallowed food is not carried to the stomach. It is a syndrome rather than a single disease:

- **Congenital idiopathic**: appears at weaning, when puppies start eating solid food.
- **Acquired secondary**: caused by a neuromuscular or systemic disease, of which **myasthenia gravis** (focal or generalised) is an important one; others include hypoadrenocorticism, polymyopathies and polyneuropathies, and toxicoses.
- **Acquired idiopathic**: the most common form in adult dogs, with no cause found.

It must be distinguished from a **segmental** dilation cranial to an obstruction, such as a vascular ring anomaly (persistent right aortic arch) at the base of the heart, an oesophageal foreign body or a stricture.

## Who it affects

Congenital forms are reported more often in the Irish setter, German shepherd, Great Dane, Labrador retriever, Newfoundland, miniature schnauzer and wire-haired fox terrier. Acquired forms affect adult dogs.

## Anatomy involved

- **Oesophagus**: the canine oesophagus is striated muscle along its whole length, so diseases of skeletal muscle and the neuromuscular junction (myasthenia gravis) affect it. The dilation involves the cervical and thoracic segments down to the cardia.
- **Lungs**: regurgitated material is aspirated, typically into the cranioventral lung (for example the right middle and left cranial lobes).

## Clinical signs

**Regurgitation** — passive, effortless expulsion of undigested food or fluid, without the abdominal effort of vomiting — at variable times after eating. Weight loss or poor growth (affected puppies are unthrifty and smaller than their littermates), and sometimes a visible bulge at the thoracic inlet. Cough, fever and sometimes nasal discharge point to aspiration pneumonia; in some adults respiratory signs predominate. Generalised myasthenia adds fatigable, exercise-induced weakness.

## Diagnosis

- **Survey thoracic radiographs**: a gas-, fluid- or food-filled oesophagus, usually uniformly dilated, sometimes with a ventral deviation cranial to the heart; they also show aspiration pneumonia.
- **Contrast studies or fluoroscopy**: confirm the motility disorder and exclude segmental lesions; videofluoroscopy in an upright position can also show which food consistency clears best.
- **Search for a cause**: serum acetylcholine receptor antibodies (myasthenia gravis), baseline cortisol or ACTH stimulation test (hypoadrenocorticism), creatine kinase, thyroid tests and neurological examination.
- **Endoscopy**: for foreign bodies, strictures or oesophagitis.

## Treatment

The underlying cause is treated when found (for example pyridostigmine for myasthenia gravis). Management is otherwise supportive and lifelong: feeding in an upright position (for example in a Bailey chair) and keeping the dog upright for about 10–30 minutes after meals, adjusting food consistency to what the dog tolerates best (it varies between dogs), giving small frequent meals and treating aspiration pneumonia early. The prognosis depends on the cause and on the frequency of aspiration pneumonia, which is the main cause of death in idiopathic forms; some puppies with the congenital form improve with age, usually by 6 months.
