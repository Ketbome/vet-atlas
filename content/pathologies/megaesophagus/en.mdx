## What it is

Megaesophagus is a diffuse dilation of the oesophagus with absent or ineffective peristalsis, so that swallowed food is not carried to the stomach.

It is a syndrome rather than a single disease. It has three forms:

- **congenital idiopathic**, which appears at weaning, when puppies start eating solid food
- **acquired secondary**, caused by a neuromuscular or systemic disease; **myasthenia gravis**, focal or generalised, is an important one, and others include hypoadrenocorticism, polymyopathies and polyneuropathies, and toxicoses
- **acquired idiopathic**, with no cause found, the most common form in adult dogs

It must be distinguished from a **segmental** dilation cranial to an obstruction, such as a vascular ring anomaly (persistent right aortic arch) at the base of the heart, an oesophageal foreign body or a stricture.

## Who it affects

Congenital forms are reported more often in the Irish setter, German shepherd, Great Dane, Labrador retriever, Newfoundland, miniature schnauzer and wire-haired fox terrier.

Acquired forms affect adult dogs.

## Anatomy involved

The canine **oesophagus** is striated muscle along its whole length. So diseases of skeletal muscle and the neuromuscular junction, such as myasthenia gravis, affect it.

The dilation involves the cervical and thoracic segments down to the cardia.

In the **lungs**, regurgitated material is typically aspirated into the cranioventral region, for example the right middle and left cranial lobes.

## Clinical signs

The main sign is **regurgitation**: passive, effortless expulsion of undigested food or fluid, without the abdominal effort of vomiting. It happens at variable times after eating.

Also:

- weight loss or poor growth; affected puppies are unthrifty and smaller than their littermates
- sometimes a visible bulge at the thoracic inlet
- cough, fever and sometimes nasal discharge, which point to aspiration pneumonia

In some adults respiratory signs predominate. Generalised myasthenia adds fatigable, exercise-induced weakness.

## Diagnosis

- **Survey thoracic radiographs** show a gas-, fluid- or food-filled oesophagus, usually uniformly dilated, sometimes with a ventral deviation cranial to the heart. They also show aspiration pneumonia.
- **Contrast studies or fluoroscopy** confirm the motility disorder and exclude segmental lesions. Videofluoroscopy in an upright position can also show which food consistency clears best.
- Endoscopy is used for foreign bodies, strictures or oesophagitis.

### Search for a cause

- serum **acetylcholine receptor** antibodies, for myasthenia gravis
- baseline cortisol or ACTH stimulation test, for hypoadrenocorticism
- creatine kinase
- thyroid tests
- neurological examination

## Treatment

The underlying cause is treated when found, for example with pyridostigmine for myasthenia gravis.

Otherwise, management is supportive and lifelong:

- feeding in an **upright position**, for example in a Bailey chair, and keeping the dog upright for about 10–30 minutes after meals
- adjusting food consistency to what the dog tolerates best, which varies between dogs
- giving small frequent meals
- treating aspiration pneumonia early

The prognosis depends on the cause and on the frequency of aspiration pneumonia, which is the main cause of death in idiopathic forms.

Some puppies with the congenital form improve with age, usually by 6 months.
