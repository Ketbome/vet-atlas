## What it is

Mastitis is inflammation of one or more quarters of the udder. It is almost always caused by an **intramammary infection** with bacteria that pass through the **teat canal**.

The inflammatory response brings large numbers of leukocytes, mainly neutrophils, into the milk. These make up most of the **somatic cells**, whose count is the main measure of udder health.

It is the disease that accounts for most antimicrobial use in dairy herds.

### Forms

In **clinical mastitis** there are visible changes. It is graded as:

- **mild**, with abnormal milk only: clots, watery or serous appearance
- **moderate**, with also a swollen, hot or painful quarter
- **severe**, with also systemic signs

In **subclinical mastitis** the milk and the quarter look normal, but the **somatic cell count (SCC)** rises and yield falls. All dairy herds have cows with subclinical mastitis.

### Pathogens

**Contagious** pathogens live in the udder and spread from cow to cow during milking, through milkers' hands and liners. The main ones are ***Staphylococcus aureus***, ***Streptococcus agalactiae*** and ***Mycoplasma bovis***. They tend to cause persistent subclinical infections.

**Environmental** pathogens come from bedding, manure and the surroundings, and enter between milkings or during the dry period. The most important are coliforms (*Escherichia coli*, *Klebsiella*) and ***Streptococcus uberis***. They usually cause short clinical cases; some coliforms cause severe mastitis with endotoxaemia.

## Who it affects

Mainly dairy cows, although any female bovine can be affected.

The risk of new infections is highest at the **start of the dry period** and around **calving**. Clinical mastitis is most common in early lactation.

Risk is increased by:

- teat injuries and **severe teat-end hyperkeratosis**
- poor milking routines or milking machines
- dirty or wet bedding
- udder oedema around calving
- heat stress

## Anatomy involved

The **teat canal** is the entry point for almost all infections. The canal and the teat end form the main physical barrier. In high-yielding cows the canal can stay open for a long time after milking ceases at dry-off, so infection remains a risk.

Severe hyperkeratosis of the teat end weakens that barrier. Mild hyperkeratosis is a physiological response to milking.

Bacteria ascend from the teat cistern to the gland cistern and the lactiferous ducts.

The damage occurs in the **secretory tissue**, in the alveoli. Chronic infections, especially with *S. aureus*, form **microabscesses and fibrosis** that wall off the bacteria and hinder cure.

Each quarter has its own duct system. So several quarters can be infected subclinically, but clinical mastitis usually affects only one, and milk is tested per quarter.

## Clinical signs

- abnormal milk: change of colour, clots or flakes, serous secretion
- a swollen, hot, red and painful quarter

### Severe mastitis

Fever, anorexia, depression, reduced rumen motility, dehydration and endotoxic shock. **Coliforms** are the most common cause.

### Gangrenous mastitis

Mainly with *S. aureus*, when chronic infections become severe at times of immune dysfunction such as calving. The quarter may need debridement and is lost.

### Chronic mastitis

Repeated clinical episodes or a persistently high SCC, with fibrotic quarters and lower yield.

### Subclinical mastitis

No signs. It is detected only by SCC or culture.

## Diagnosis

- **Forestripping** examines the first streams of milk from each quarter before milking to detect abnormal milk.
- The **California Mastitis Test** is a cow-side test that estimates the somatic cells in each quarter.
- The monthly individual **SCC** from milk recording identifies cows with probable subclinical infection. The most widely used threshold is **200,000 cells/mL**. Bulk tank SCC reflects the prevalence of infection in the herd.
- **Bacteriological culture** of aseptically collected milk, in the laboratory or on the farm, is the only reliable way to identify the pathogen. It helps distinguish contagious from environmental problems and guides treatment. A single culture can miss the infection.

## Treatment and prevention

### Mild or moderate mastitis

It is treated with intramammary antimicrobials, chosen according to the pathogen when it is known.

Many cases caused by Gram-negative bacteria or with no bacterial growth cure without antibiotics. So **culture-guided treatment** reduces their use.

### Severe mastitis

It is an emergency. Treatment is fluid therapy, **non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs** and, depending on the case, systemic and intramammary antimicrobials.

### Chronic *S. aureus* infection

In cows that do not respond, the quarter is dried off or the cow is culled, because cure is unlikely and they are a source of infection.

### Prevention

- a good **milking routine**: teat preparation and drying, gloves and post-milking teat disinfection
- milking machine maintenance to protect the teat end
- clean, dry bedding
- **dry cow therapy**, with antimicrobials in all cows or only in infected ones (selective therapy), and **internal teat sealants**
- prompt treatment of clinical cases and culling of chronically infected cows
- monitoring of individual and bulk tank SCC, and segregation of cows infected with contagious pathogens

Drug use in food-producing animals is subject to each country's regulations and to withdrawal periods for milk and meat. Milk from treated cows cannot be used for human consumption during that period.

<summary>Key studies</summary>

- **Pantoja et al. (2020).** Systematic review of 18 studies (8 prospective cohorts, 9 cross-sectional and 1 case-control) on teat-end hyperkeratosis. **Severe** hyperkeratosis was a risk factor for clinical and subclinical mastitis, especially with *S. aureus*. Mild hyperkeratosis was not, and might even be protective. The most common limitation of the studies was failure to control for confounding.
- **Dohoo et al. (2011).** Evaluation of criteria for diagnosing intramammary infection from a single culture, using 25,886 sets of three samples and another 784 quarters sampled three times as the reference. Except for *S. aureus*, the sensitivity of every criterion was below 90%, and in many cases below 50%. Specificity was high for most species. A negative culture does not rule out infection.
- **Lago et al. (2011).** Randomised trial in 422 cows (449 quarters) with mild or moderate mastitis from 8 herds in the United States and Canada. With on-farm culture-guided treatment, 51% of cases received intramammary antibiotics, compared with 100% in the control group. There were no significant differences in days to clinical cure (3.2 vs 2.7), bacteriological cure (60% vs 71%), new infections (50% in both) or treatment failure (78% vs 81%).
- **Nobrega et al. (2020).** Systematic review and network meta-analysis of 30 studies on non-severe clinical mastitis. Antimicrobials of critical importance for human medicine were no more effective than the others. In cases caused by *E. coli* and *Klebsiella*, bacteriological cure was similar in treated and untreated cows.
- **Rabiee and Lean (2013).** Meta-analysis of internal teat sealants containing bismuth subnitrate. Used alone or with antimicrobials at dry-off, they reduced new intramammary infections after calving by 25% (RR 0.75), and by 73% compared with untreated cows (RR 0.27). They reduced clinical mastitis by 29% to 48% depending on the comparison. Results for infections were highly heterogeneous.
- **Winder et al. (2019).** Systematic review and meta-analysis of 9 trials comparing selective dry cow therapy with treatment of all cows. With selective therapy the risk of intramammary infection at calving was higher (RR 1.34), with substantial heterogeneity, but in the trials that used internal teat sealants there was no difference. The trials were few and did not allow clinical mastitis to be analysed.
