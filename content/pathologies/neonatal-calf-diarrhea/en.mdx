---
title: Neonatal calf diarrhoea
summary: Diarrhoea of calves in their first month of life caused by rotavirus, coronavirus, Cryptosporidium parvum and enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli, alone or combined. Dehydration and acidosis are what kill; colostrum feeding and hygiene are the basis of prevention.
---

## What it is

Neonatal diarrhoea is the most important cause of death in calves in their first weeks. The main agents are **enterotoxigenic *Escherichia coli*** (ETEC, with F5 or K99 fimbriae), **rotavirus**, **bovine coronavirus** and the protozoan ***Cryptosporidium parvum***; mixed infections are common. Other causes are *Salmonella*, *Clostridium perfringens* and errors in milk feeding.

There are two main mechanisms:

- **Secretory**: ETEC adheres to the small intestinal epithelium and produces an enterotoxin that causes secretion of water and electrolytes without damaging the villi. It mainly affects calves a few days old.
- **Malabsorptive**: rotavirus, coronavirus and *C. parvum* destroy the mature enterocytes of the villi, which shorten (**villous atrophy**). Absorptive surface and enzymes such as lactase are lost, and undigested milk reaching the colon ferments and draws water. They usually affect somewhat older calves, during the first weeks of life.

The consequences are **dehydration**, **metabolic acidosis**, electrolyte disturbances and hypoglycaemia. *C. parvum* is also a **zoonosis** for people who handle calves.

## Who it affects

Dairy and beef calves under one month of age. The most important risk factor is **inadequate transfer of passive immunity** from colostrum that is scarce, of poor quality or given late. Others are poor hygiene in calving and calf-rearing areas, inadequate protection from the weather, an insufficient or inappropriate diet, and mixing older calves with newborns.

## Anatomy involved

- **Small intestine**: the main site of infection. ETEC colonises the villi of the small intestine; rotavirus and coronavirus destroy the villous enterocytes; *C. parvum* lodges in the brush border of the enterocytes, mainly in the distal small intestine.
- **Large intestine**: coronavirus and *C. parvum* can also affect the caecum and colon, which worsens the diarrhoea. Poorly digested milk reaching the colon ferments and increases osmotic diarrhoea.
- **Abomasum**: in the suckling calf, milk passes through the reticular groove directly into the abomasum. Milk should **not be withheld** from the diarrhoeic calf: although it can increase faecal volume, it provides the energy the calf needs and can promote gut healing.

## Clinical signs

**Loose to watery faeces**, sometimes with mucus or blood. Depending on severity: **dehydration**, dullness, weakness, loss of the **suckle reflex**, inability to stand and recumbency. Severely dehydrated and **acidaemic** calves become comatose; body temperature is often normal or low, and in ETEC diarrhoea death from hypovolaemic shock can occur within 12–24 hours.

## Diagnosis

- **Clinical**: the calf's age points to the agent. Dehydration and acidaemia can be estimated with reasonable precision by physical examination.
- **Faecal tests**: on-farm rapid tests for the common pathogens and laboratory tests, on samples from several untreated calves early in the disease; enteropathogens are also found in healthy calves, which complicates interpretation.
- **Blood tests**: blood gases and electrolytes in severely affected calves.
- **Herd diagnosis**: necropsy of dead calves and monitoring of transfer of passive immunity by measuring serum total protein or Brix in calves 1 to 7 days old.

## Treatment and prevention

Treatment is based on correcting dehydration, acidosis and hypoglycaemia. A calf that still suckles receives **oral rehydration solutions** with alkalinising agents and glucose, while continuing to drink milk. A calf that cannot stand or has lost the suckle reflex needs **intravenous fluid therapy** with bicarbonate. Antimicrobials are not indicated routinely and are reserved for calves with systemic illness, because of the risk of bacteraemia. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs may improve welfare. Drug use in calves intended for consumption is subject to national regulations and withdrawal periods.

Prevention relies on:

- **Colostrum**: sufficient quantity, good quality and within the first hours of life, with routine monitoring of transfer of passive immunity.
- **Hygiene**: good hygiene in the calving and calf-rearing areas, separate maternity, calf-rearing and hospital pens, no mixing of older calves with newborns, and plain clean water from the first day.
- **Vaccination of dams** before calving against rotavirus, coronavirus and ETEC, to increase colostral antibodies.
- On farms with cryptosporidiosis, preventive treatment with **halofuginone** in the first days of life, where authorised.

## Key studies

- **Lombard et al. consensus (2020)**: a United States expert group that proposes four categories of passive immunity based on serum IgG: excellent (≥ 25 g/L), good (18–24.9), fair (10–17.9) and poor (less than 10). As a herd goal it proposes more than 40% of calves in the excellent category and less than 10% in the poor one, with equivalents in serum total protein and Brix for use on farm. It rests mainly on United States dairy heifer data and expert opinion.
- **Raboisson et al. (2016)**, meta-analysis of adjusted associations between failure of passive transfer and calf health. Failure multiplied the risk of death by 2.12, of respiratory disease by 1.75, of diarrhoea by 1.51 and of any disease by 1.91. The estimated mean cost was €60 per dairy calf and €80 per beef calf in European systems.
- **Bernal-Córdoba et al. (2022)**, systematic review of trials of antimicrobials for calf diarrhoea: of 2899 studies, only 11 met the criteria, with designs and outcomes so different that a meta-analysis was not possible, and with poor reporting of the trials. It concludes that there is **insufficient evidence** for the efficacy of antimicrobials in this disease, despite their widespread use.
- **Brainard et al. (2021)**, systematic review and meta-analysis of 25 experiments, mostly European randomised trials, on halofuginone for cryptosporidiosis. Started before 5 days of age, it significantly reduced oocyst shedding, diarrhoea and mortality. Publication bias is possible, especially for diarrhoea outcomes.
- **Higgs et al. (2025)**, systematic review and meta-analysis of dam vaccination against ETEC, with 22 publications. In field trials there was no evidence that vaccination reduced diarrhoea or death in calves under 14 days; in experimental challenge trials, the vaccine prevented death. Field trials were few and of limited quality, and very few reported randomisation, blinding or funding.
