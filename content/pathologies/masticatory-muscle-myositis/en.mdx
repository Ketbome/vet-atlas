## What it is

Masticatory muscle myositis (MMM) is a focal **immune-mediated myopathy**.

The muscles of mastication contain a special fibre type, **type 2M fibres**, with a myosin isoform not found in limb muscles.

In MMM the immune system produces **autoantibodies against 2M fibre myosin**. Inflammatory cells, mainly B lymphocytes and macrophages, infiltrate these muscles.

Limb muscles are not affected, which distinguishes MMM from polymyositis.

## Who it affects

Dogs of any breed, usually young adults. It is frequently reported in large breeds such as the **German shepherd**, Labrador, golden retriever and Dobermann.

An atypical form with eye involvement has been described in Cavalier King Charles spaniels.

## Anatomy involved

The muscles of mastication are:

- the **temporalis**
- the **masseter**
- the medial and lateral pterygoid muscles
- the rostral belly of the digastricus

All are innervated by the **mandibular nerve**, a branch of the trigeminal nerve. The digastricus usually lacks 2M fibres and is spared.

In the acute phase the swollen muscles can be accompanied by exophthalmos.

## Clinical signs

### Acute phase

- swelling of the temporalis and masseter
- pain when opening the mouth or handling the head
- **trismus**
- exophthalmos, fever and lymphadenopathy

Not every dog shows a clear acute phase.

### Chronic phase

- **marked atrophy of the temporalis and masseter**, usually bilateral, giving a "skull-like" head
- restricted jaw opening from fibrosis

Some dogs present with slowly progressive atrophy without a previous acute phase.

## Diagnosis

- The **serum 2M antibody test** is highly specific. The sample should be taken before immunosuppressive treatment, which can cause false negatives.
- **Biopsy of the temporalis or masseter** shows an inflammatory infiltrate targeting 2M fibres, necrosis and, in chronic cases, fibrosis.
- Creatine kinase may be raised in the acute phase.
- Electromyography shows abnormalities restricted to the masticatory muscles.

### Differential diagnosis

- trigeminal neuropathy, in which the dog cannot close the mouth, rather than cannot open it
- temporomandibular joint disease
- retrobulbar disease
- polymyositis
- infectious myositis

## Treatment

The mainstay is **immunosuppressive doses of corticosteroids**. They are tapered slowly over weeks to months to the lowest dose that keeps signs from recurring. Other immunosuppressants such as azathioprine are added when needed.

Forcing the jaw open under anaesthesia is not recommended.

Soft food or a feeding tube supports nutrition while the dog cannot eat.

Prognosis is good when treatment starts early. Relapses are common if treatment is stopped too soon, and fibrosis that has already formed does not reverse.
