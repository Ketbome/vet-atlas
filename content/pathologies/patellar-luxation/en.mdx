## What it is

Patellar luxation is the slipping of the patella out of the trochlear groove of the femur.

It can be caused by trauma, but in the great majority of cases it is **congenital or developmental**. The puppy is born with a faulty alignment of the extensor mechanism (quadriceps, patella, patellar ligament and tibial tuberosity), which pulls the patella out of the trochlea.

It is part of a wider malalignment of the limb. In growing animals the deformities can worsen with time.

## Who it affects

**Medial luxation** is by far the most common. It mainly affects small and toy breeds:

- Yorkshire terrier
- Chihuahua
- Pomeranian
- miniature poodle
- Boston terrier

It is also seen in large breeds, where **lateral luxation**, associated with genu valgum, is proportionally more frequent than in small dogs.

It is bilateral in a substantial share of cases. It is usually diagnosed in young animals, although low grades may go unnoticed until adulthood.

## Anatomy involved

- **Femoral trochlea**. Shallow or even flat. In medial luxation the medial femoral condyle is hypoplastic.
- **Femur**. In varus in medial luxation and in valgus in lateral luxation. Femoral torsion may be present.
- **Tibial tuberosity**. Displaced medially, or laterally, which deviates the line of pull of the patellar ligament. The tibia is internally rotated.
- **Soft tissues**. The retinaculum and capsule are contracted on the side of the luxation and stretched on the opposite side.

## Grades

- **Grade I**. The patella can be luxated manually but returns to the trochlea on its own when released. Lameness is rare.
- **Grade II**. It luxates spontaneously with stifle flexion or manual pressure, and stays out until reduced by extension or manipulation. Intermittent lameness is typical: the dog "skips a step" with the limb flexed and then bears weight normally.
- **Grade III**. Luxated most of the time, but can be reduced manually; it luxates again easily. Lameness is consistent and bony deformities are evident.
- **Grade IV**. Permanently luxated and cannot be reduced. Lameness and limb deformity are severe, and the animal may walk with flexed stifles and crouched hindlimbs.

## Diagnosis

- Palpation of the patella with the stifle in extension and in flexion assigns the grade.
- Lateral and craniocaudal radiographs show the position of the patella, femoral varus or valgus and osteoarthritis.
- The "skyline" view assesses the depth of the trochlea.
- Computed tomography is used to plan corrective osteotomies.

The **cranial cruciate ligament** must always be examined, since patellar luxation predisposes to cranial cruciate ligament disease.

## Treatment

Grade I without lameness is managed without surgery. Surgery is indicated for grades II to IV with clinical signs and combines techniques according to the deformities present:

- **trochleoplasty**, to deepen the groove
- **tibial tuberosity transposition**, to realign the patellar ligament
- release of the contracted retinaculum
- imbrication of the stretched side
- corrective osteotomy of the distal femur, in marked femoral varus

The prognosis is good in low grades and worsens with deformity and established osteoarthritis.

<summary>In the cat</summary>

Patellar luxation is less common in the cat than in the dog, but not rare. It is almost always **medial** and often bilateral.

The Devon Rex and Abyssinian are predisposed, and an association with hip dysplasia has been described.

### Signs

Many cats with low grades show no signs, or only difficulty jumping, and the condition is found on examination. Cats with signs show hindlimb lameness or reluctance to jump.

### Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by palpation and radiographs, as in the dog.

Surgery is indicated in cats with clinical signs, and most improve afterwards. It includes trochleoplasty, imbrication of the stretched side, release of the contracted side and, if needed, tibial tuberosity transposition.

<details>
<summary>Key studies in the cat</summary>

- **Smith et al. (1999).** Cross-sectional study of 78 cats. 58% had medial patellar luxation, mostly grade 1 and bilateral in 71%, and 32% had hip dysplasia; 24% had both. The association between the two was weak: cats were 3 times more likely to have both than either alone. Only 11 of the 78 had pelvic limb signs.
- **Loughin et al. (2006).** 42 cats. 34 had bilateral luxation, 95% of affected joints were medial and only 17% of cats had a history of trauma. Low-grade luxations could cause lameness as severe as high-grade ones. The outcome was excellent in 23 of 35 joints treated surgically and in 8 of 17 treated without surgery.
- **Rutherford et al. (2015).** 85 surgeries in 71 cats from four referral centres. Complications occurred in 26%, major in 20% and reluxation in 5%. The risk was higher with a previous femoral fracture on the same side, grade 4 luxation, tibial tuberosity transposition and previous stifle surgery.
- **Mills et al. (2024).** 44 stifles in 39 cats treated with polyethylene prostheses that raise the trochlear ridges. Minor complications occurred in 20% and major ones in 7%. At 2 years or more, 74% had osteoarthritis with mineralisation, yet function was good or very good in 93%. One of the authors was involved in the commercial development of the implant.

</details>
