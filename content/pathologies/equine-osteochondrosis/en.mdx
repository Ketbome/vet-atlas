## What it is

Osteochondrosis is a failure of **endochondral ossification**. In an area of the growth cartilage beneath the articular cartilage, cartilage is not converted into bone.

In the horse the initial lesion is a focal necrosis of the growth cartilage. It is caused by failure of the blood supply through the cartilage canals, which supply the cartilage at that stage.

The cartilage that does not ossify thickens and weakens, cracks under load and can detach as an osteochondral fragment. This is **osteochondritis dissecans** (OCD). At other sites the lesion forms a **subchondral cyst**.

Lesions appear very early, in the first months of life, and many **regress** on their own. In a study of warmblood foals, hock lesions were permanent by 5 months and stifle lesions by 8 months.

The disease is multifactorial. Contributing factors are:

- genetics
- rapid growth and large size
- diet, with excess energy or mineral imbalances such as copper deficiency
- exercise
- trauma to immature cartilage

## Who it affects

Foals and young horses, more often of large, fast-growing sport breeds: warmbloods, Standardbreds and Thoroughbreds. It is less common in ponies and small breeds.

Many lesions are found in horses aged 1 to 3 years on sale or selection radiographs, sometimes without clinical signs.

## Anatomy involved

### Hock

In the tarsocrural joint, the most common site is the **distal intermediate ridge of the tibia**. The medial malleolus of the tibia and the lateral trochlear ridge of the talus, especially its distal end, are also affected.

### Stifle

In the femoropatellar joint, the typical site is the **lateral trochlear ridge of the femur**. The medial trochlear ridge and the patella are affected less often.

In the femorotibial joint, the **medial femoral condyle** is the classic site of subchondral cysts.

### Fetlock

The sagittal ridge of the distal end of the third metacarpal and third metatarsal bones is affected. Fragments from the dorsoproximal aspect of the first phalanx are often included in this group too.

### Other sites

Less often, the shoulder joint and the cervical vertebrae are affected. In the neck, osteochondrosis of the articular processes can narrow the vertebral canal and cause ataxia.

## Clinical signs

The most typical sign is painless **joint effusion**, often bilateral:

- in the hock, a soft distension of the tarsocrural joint (bog spavin)
- in the stifle, a distension of the femoropatellar joint

Lameness is usually mild or absent, and appears or worsens when training starts.

Large stifle lesions in foals can cause more obvious lameness and stiffness, and affected foals spend more time lying down.

Many lesions are radiographic findings without clinical signs.

## Diagnosis

- **Radiographs** of the typical joints, often of both sides because lesions are frequently bilateral. They show flattening or defects of the subchondral outline, fragments and cysts.
- Ultrasonography of the stifle, to assess the cartilage and fragments that have not yet ossified.
- Arthroscopy, which confirms the lesion, assesses the cartilage and allows treatment in the same procedure.

In young foals, serial radiographs show whether a lesion regresses or progresses before treatment is decided.

## Treatment and prevention

### Treatment

In horses with effusion or lameness, the usual treatment is **arthroscopic surgery**: fragments are removed and the affected cartilage and bone are debrided.

In young foals with small stifle lesions, which can heal, conservative treatment with rest and controlled exercise is an option. Large lesions or loose fragments need surgery.

### Prevention

- avoid excess dietary energy and overly rapid growth
- balance minerals
- provide regular exercise in a paddock of moderate size
- take osteochondrosis into account when selecting breeding stock

Because heritability is low to moderate, selection alone does not eliminate the disease.

### Regulations

The horse is a food-producing animal in many countries, and drug use is subject to national regulations and withdrawal periods.

<summary>Key studies</summary>

- **Lepeule et al. (2009).** Cohort study on 21 farms in Normandy (France) with 378 foals of 3 breeds, followed from gestation to weaning. At 6 months, 47% had developmental orthopaedic lesions and 36% had osteochondrosis. Warmblood foals were at higher risk than Thoroughbreds (OR 2.9; 95% CI 1.1–7.5 for osteochondrosis), as were foals with greater wither height at one month. Some exercise conditions, such as large pastures or irregular exercise, also increased the risk. No association with feeding was found, possibly because of little variation between farms. The farms were a convenience sample.
- **Denoix et al. (2013).** Radiographic study of 392 six-month-old French Trotter, Selle Français (warmblood) and Thoroughbred foals from the same population. The most affected site was the hind fetlock (28.3% of foals), followed by the fore fetlock in Thoroughbreds and Selle Français and by the carpus and proximal tarsus in Trotters. Selle Français foals were the most affected. It is a descriptive study at a single age, and many lesions at this age may still regress.
- **Russell et al. (2017).** Retrospective cohort with radiographs of 1,962 Thoroughbred yearlings in Australia. The prevalence of osteochondrosis was 23%: 10% of stifles, 6% of hocks and 8% of fetlocks. Heritabilities were low, from 0 to 0.21, highest for the lateral trochlear ridge of the femur. A meta-analysis with earlier studies gave values of 0.10 to 0.17 depending on the joint (0.20 for the joints combined). The estimates have wide standard errors.
- **Grøndahl and Engeland (1995).** Cohort of 753 Standardbred trotters radiographed as yearlings and followed through their racing careers. Horses with hock osteochondrosis, palmar or plantar fetlock fragments or other fetlock changes, including osteochondrosis of the sagittal ridge, had fewer starts and somewhat lower earnings than horses without radiographic changes. It is a single breed and the abstract does not give the effect size.
- **Laws et al. (1993).** Retrospective study of 114 Standardbreds with osteochondrosis of the distal intermediate ridge of the tibia, 58 treated arthroscopically and 56 conservatively, compared with 456 controls matched by year of birth, sex and sire. Overall, affected horses had fewer starts than controls but similar earnings and no difference in record time; females did perform worse. It is retrospective and treatment was not randomised.
