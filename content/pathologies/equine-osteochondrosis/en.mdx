---
title: Equine osteochondrosis
summary: Developmental disorder in which ossification of the articular growth cartilage fails, leaving defects or fragments of cartilage and bone (osteochondritis dissecans). In the horse it mainly affects the hock, stifle and fetlock of foals and young horses.
---

## What it is

Osteochondrosis is a failure of **endochondral ossification**: in an area of the growth cartilage beneath the articular cartilage, cartilage is not converted into bone. In the horse the initial lesion is a **focal necrosis** of the growth cartilage caused by failure of the blood supply through the cartilage canals, which supply the cartilage at that stage. The cartilage that does not ossify thickens and weakens, cracks under load and can detach as an **osteochondral fragment**: this is **osteochondritis dissecans** (OCD). At other sites the lesion forms a **subchondral cyst**.

Lesions appear very early, in the first months of life, and many **regress** on their own: in a study of warmblood foals, hock lesions were permanent by 5 months and stifle lesions by 8 months. The disease is **multifactorial**: genetics, **rapid growth** and large size, diet (excess energy, mineral imbalances such as copper deficiency), exercise and trauma to immature cartilage all play a part.

## Who it affects

Foals and young horses, more often of large, fast-growing **sport breeds**: warmbloods, Standardbreds and Thoroughbreds; it is less common in ponies and small breeds. Many lesions are found in horses aged 1 to 3 years on sale or selection radiographs, sometimes without clinical signs.

## Anatomy involved

- **Tibia**: the most common site in the hock is the **distal intermediate ridge of the tibia**, in the tarsocrural joint; also the **medial malleolus**.
- **Tarsus**: the **lateral trochlear ridge of the talus**, especially its distal end, in the same tarsocrural joint.
- **Femur**: in the stifle, the **lateral trochlear ridge of the femur** is the typical site in the femoropatellar joint; the medial trochlear ridge and the patella are affected less often. In the femorotibial joint, the **medial femoral condyle** is the classic site of subchondral cysts.
- **Metacarpus and metatarsus**: in the fetlock, the **sagittal ridge** of the distal end of the third metacarpal and third metatarsal bones. Fragments from the dorsoproximal aspect of the first phalanx are often included in this group too.
- Less often, the shoulder joint and the cervical vertebrae are affected, where osteochondrosis of the articular processes can narrow the vertebral canal and cause ataxia.

## Clinical signs

The most typical sign is painless **joint effusion**, often **bilateral**: in the hock it appears as a soft distension of the tarsocrural joint (bog spavin), and in the stifle as a distension of the femoropatellar joint. **Lameness** is usually mild or absent and appears or worsens when training starts. Large stifle lesions in foals can cause more obvious lameness and stiffness, and affected foals spend more time lying down. Many lesions are radiographic findings without clinical signs.

## Diagnosis

- **Radiographs** of the typical joints, often of both sides because lesions are frequently bilateral: flattening or defects of the subchondral outline, fragments and cysts.
- **Ultrasonography** of the stifle to assess the cartilage and fragments that have not yet ossified.
- **Arthroscopy**: confirms the lesion, assesses the cartilage and allows treatment in the same procedure.
- In young foals, serial radiographs show whether a lesion regresses or progresses before treatment is decided.

## Treatment and prevention

- **Arthroscopic surgery**: removal of fragments and debridement of the affected cartilage and bone; it is the usual treatment in horses with effusion or lameness.
- **Conservative treatment** with rest and controlled exercise in young foals with small stifle lesions, which can heal; large lesions or loose fragments need surgery.
- **Prevention**: avoiding excess dietary energy and overly rapid growth, balancing minerals, providing **regular exercise** in a paddock of moderate size and taking osteochondrosis into account when selecting breeding stock. Because heritability is low to moderate, selection alone does not eliminate the disease.
- The horse is a food-producing animal in many countries and drug use is subject to **national regulations** and **withdrawal periods**.

## Key studies

- **Lepeule et al. (2009)**, cohort study on 21 farms in Normandy (France) with 378 foals of 3 breeds followed from gestation to weaning. At 6 months, 47% had developmental orthopaedic lesions and **36% had osteochondrosis**. Warmblood foals were at higher risk than Thoroughbreds (OR 2.9; 95% CI 1.1–7.5 for osteochondrosis), as were foals with greater wither height at one month. Some exercise conditions, such as large pastures or irregular exercise, also increased the risk, but no association with feeding was found, possibly because of little variation between farms. The farms were a convenience sample.
- **Denoix et al. (2013)**, radiographic study of 392 six-month-old French Trotter, Selle Français (warmblood) and Thoroughbred foals from the same population. The most affected site was the **hind fetlock** (28.3% of foals), followed by the fore fetlock in Thoroughbreds and Selle Français and by the carpus and proximal tarsus in Trotters; Selle Français foals were the most affected. It is a descriptive study at a single age, and many lesions at this age may still regress.
- **Russell et al. (2017)**, retrospective cohort with radiographs of 1,962 Thoroughbred yearlings in Australia. The prevalence of osteochondrosis was 23%: 10% of stifles, 6% of hocks and 8% of fetlocks. Heritabilities were low, from 0 to 0.21, highest for the lateral trochlear ridge of the femur, and a meta-analysis with earlier studies gave values of 0.10 to 0.17 depending on the joint (0.20 for the joints combined). The estimates have wide standard errors.
- **Grøndahl and Engeland (1995)**, cohort of 753 Standardbred trotters radiographed as yearlings and followed through their racing careers. Horses with hock osteochondrosis, palmar or plantar fetlock fragments or other fetlock changes (including osteochondrosis of the sagittal ridge) had **fewer starts** and somewhat lower earnings than horses without radiographic changes. It is a single breed and the abstract does not give the effect size.
- **Laws et al. (1993)**, retrospective study of 114 Standardbreds with osteochondrosis of the distal intermediate ridge of the tibia, 58 treated arthroscopically and 56 conservatively, compared with 456 controls matched by year of birth, sex and sire. Overall, affected horses had fewer starts than controls but similar earnings and no difference in record time; females did perform worse. It is retrospective and treatment was not randomised.
