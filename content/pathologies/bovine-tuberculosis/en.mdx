## What it is

Bovine tuberculosis is a chronic disease caused by ***Mycobacterium bovis***, a member of the *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* complex. *M. caprae* causes a similar disease.

Cattle are infected mainly **by inhalation** of aerosols from infected animals. Calves can also be infected orally, through contaminated milk.

The bacterium persists inside macrophages and triggers a cell-mediated immune response that forms the **tubercle**: a granuloma with a centre of **caseous necrosis**, which over time mineralises and becomes surrounded by a fibrous capsule. From the initial focus and its regional lymph node, the infection can spread to other organs as generalised or miliary tuberculosis.

In some regions **wildlife reservoirs** hinder eradication:

- the badger, deer and wild boar in Europe
- deer and bison in North America

It is a **zoonosis**. People are infected by consuming raw milk or unpasteurised dairy products and through contact with infected animals. Herders and abattoir workers are most at risk.

## Who it affects

Cattle of any age and type. Younger, pregnant, malnourished or immunosuppressed animals are considered more susceptible.

Prevalence varies widely between countries: several are officially free, while in others it is endemic.

Other domestic and wild ruminants, pigs and people can also be infected.

## Anatomy involved

In the lungs, the most common primary focus is a granuloma in the parenchyma together with the lesion in its regional lymph node: the **primary complex**. Tubercles are nodules of variable size, often encapsulated, with caseous, caseocalcareous or calcified contents.

When lesions communicate with the trachea and bronchi, the animal sheds bacilli in respiratory secretions and by coughing. This is "open" tuberculosis, the main route of spread between cattle.

The **tracheobronchial and mediastinal lymph nodes**, next to the tracheal bifurcation and in the mediastinum, are very often affected. When greatly enlarged they can compress the trachea or oesophagus and cause dyspnoea or bloat.

## Clinical signs

The vast majority of infected animals **show no signs**. They are only detected by official testing or at slaughter.

Advanced disease brings:

- **progressive weight loss**
- fluctuating fever
- intermittent, moist **chronic cough**
- dyspnoea
- enlargement of superficial lymph nodes
- recurrent **ruminal bloat**, from compression of the oesophagus

Udder involvement causes mastitis and shedding of bacilli in milk.

## Diagnosis

- The **intradermal tuberculin test** is the basis of official programmes. Tuberculin is injected into the skin of the neck, or the caudal fold in some countries, and the increase in skin thickness is measured at 72 hours.
- The **comparative test**, with bovine and avian tuberculin, helps distinguish sensitisation by environmental mycobacteria or by *M. avium* subsp. *paratuberculosis*.
- The **interferon-gamma** blood test is ancillary. Used together with the tuberculin test, it increases detection of infected animals.
- Suspicious lesions found at meat inspection are confirmed by histopathology, which shows granulomas with acid-fast bacilli on Ziehl-Neelsen staining, by mycobacterial culture and by PCR.

Tests can fail early in infection and in very advanced animals that no longer react to tuberculin because of **anergy**.

## Treatment and control

It is not treated, because of the public health risk.

Control relies on official **test-and-slaughter** programmes for reactor animals, mandatory in many countries. They include:

- repeated testing of affected herds
- pre-movement testing
- epidemiological investigation of outbreaks
- abattoir surveillance
- measures against wildlife reservoirs, where they exist

**Pasteurisation of milk** protects people.

BCG vaccine partially protects cattle but interferes with the tuberculin test, so in many countries it is not permitted.

<summary>Key studies</summary>

- **Nuñez-Garcia et al. (2018).** Bayesian meta-analysis of the sensitivity and specificity of 14 diagnostic tests, with data published between 1934 and 2009 in the United Kingdom and Ireland. The comparative intradermal test with standard interpretation had a median sensitivity of only 0.50 (95% credible interval 0.26–0.78) and a specificity of 1.00. Interferon-gamma had a sensitivity of 0.67 with tuberculins and 0.78 with the ESAT-6/CFP-10 antigens, with specificities of 0.98 and 0.99. The test is very reliable when positive but misses many infected animals.
- **Willgert et al. (2025).** Systematic review of 91 studies on the impact of tuberculosis on health, welfare and production. 87% of positive animals had no clinical signs, which allows silent spread without routine surveillance, and 46% had visible lesions on post-mortem inspection. The data were sparse and sometimes contradictory.
- **Collins et al. (2022).** Systematic review and meta-analysis of 83 studies on *M. bovis* in milk. Prevalence was 5% in milk from individual cows, 8% in tuberculin-positive cows and 5% in bulk-tank milk, with large heterogeneity between studies. It supports the zoonotic risk of raw milk.
- **Müller et al. (2013).** Systematic review of zoonotic tuberculosis in humans in 61 countries. Outside Africa, the median proportion of human tuberculosis cases due to *M. bovis* or *M. caprae* was up to 1.4%; in the African countries studied it was 2.8%, with a crude estimate of 7 cases per 100,000 population per year. Data were missing for many regions.
- **Fromsa et al. (2024).** Natural transmission experiment in Ethiopia with vaccinated and unvaccinated calves exposed for one year. BCG reduced infection of vaccinated calves by 58%, transmission from vaccinated animals that became infected by 74% and overall by 89%. It is a single experiment, carried out in one country.
