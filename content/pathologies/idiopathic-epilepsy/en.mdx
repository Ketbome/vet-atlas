## What it is

Epilepsy is a brain disease characterised by an enduring predisposition to generate epileptic seizures. In practice it is defined as at least **two unprovoked seizures more than 24 hours apart**.

The International Veterinary Epilepsy Task Force (IVETF) classifies it by cause:

- **idiopathic**: genetic, suspected genetic or unknown cause, with no structural lesion
- **structural**: caused by a brain lesion, such as a tumour, inflammation, malformation or vascular event

Seizures caused by extracranial problems, such as hypoglycaemia, hepatic encephalopathy, electrolyte disorders or toxins, are **reactive seizures** and are not epilepsy.

## Who it affects

It affects about 0.5–0.8% of dogs seen in general practice.

Typically, the first seizure appears between about **6 months and 6 years** of age.

A genetic basis has been shown or is suspected in many breeds, including:

- Belgian shepherd, Border collie and Australian shepherd
- Labrador and golden retrievers
- beagle, boxer and Bernese mountain dog
- Lagotto Romagnolo, Irish wolfhound, standard poodle and Dalmatian

## Anatomy involved

The seizures originate in the **cerebral cortex (prosencephalon)** as abnormal, excessive, synchronous neuronal activity.

In idiopathic epilepsy the brain is structurally normal on imaging and gross examination.

Prolonged or clustered seizures can cause secondary damage, for example to the hippocampus and piriform lobe.

## Clinical signs

### Seizure types

- **Generalised** from onset. Most commonly tonic-clonic: loss of consciousness, limb paddling or rigidity, salivation, urination and defecation.
- **Focal**. Motor signs on one part of the body, such as facial twitching, or autonomic or behavioural signs, with or without later generalisation.

### Phases

A prodrome of hours or days of restlessness or anxiety may precede the seizure.

A post-ictal phase follows, with disorientation, pacing, blindness, proprioceptive deficits or aggression. It may be very short or last hours to days.

Between seizures the dog is **neurologically normal**.

### Emergencies

- **cluster seizures**: two or more in 24 hours
- **status epilepticus**: a seizure lasting more than 5 minutes, or repeated seizures without recovery

## Diagnosis

- History and **video** of the events distinguish seizures from syncope, movement disorders or vestibular attacks.
- The physical and inter-ictal neurological examinations are normal. A **neurological deficit between seizures** points to a structural cause.
- Laboratory tests exclude reactive seizures: blood count, biochemistry including glucose and electrolytes, bile acids or ammonia, and urinalysis.

### IVETF confidence levels

- **Tier I**: the above together with age at onset.
- **Tier II**: adds normal brain MRI and cerebrospinal fluid analysis.
- **Tier III**: adds characteristic EEG abnormalities.

## Treatment

Antiseizure medication is usually started when a dog has had:

- two or more seizures within six months
- any cluster or status epilepticus
- severe or worsening post-ictal signs

First-line drugs are **phenobarbital**, **imepitoin** and **potassium bromide**. Levetiracetam and other drugs are added when control is insufficient.

The aim is to reduce the frequency and severity of seizures with acceptable side effects. Complete seizure freedom is reached in only some dogs.

Monitoring includes seizure diaries and serum drug concentrations.

For clusters or status epilepticus, owners can be given **intranasal midazolam** to use at home or, if it is not available, rectal diazepam.

The diagnosis also matters for breeding decisions, because the disease often has a genetic basis.
