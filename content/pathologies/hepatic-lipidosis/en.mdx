---
title: Feline hepatic lipidosis
summary: Massive accumulation of triglycerides in the hepatocytes of a cat that stops eating, especially if obese. It causes liver failure with jaundice; with tube feeding most cats recover.
---

## What it is

Feline hepatic lipidosis is the most common acquired liver disease of the cat. When a cat stops eating for several days, adipose tissue releases large amounts of fatty acids that reach the liver. The cat has metabolic peculiarities that make it vulnerable: fasting quickly depletes nutrients such as taurine, and carnitine and other factors needed to use fat may fall short, so the liver cannot oxidise or redistribute the fat as fast as it arrives. Triglycerides then accumulate in the hepatocytes, which swell, compress the bile canaliculi and stop working: **intrahepatic cholestasis** and **liver failure** follow.

In most cats lipidosis is **secondary** to another disease that caused the anorexia: inflammatory bowel disease or intestinal lymphoma, cholangitis, pancreatitis, lower urinary tract disease, diabetes mellitus, hyperthyroidism, kidney disease, other neoplasia, or oral and dental disease that prevents eating. In other cats the anorexia is triggered by stress: moving house, a diet change, a new animal, boarding.

## Who it affects

Middle-aged adult cats of either sex, with greater risk in **obese** cats that lose weight abruptly. A weight-loss plan in which the cat refuses the new food can also trigger it.

## Anatomy involved

- **Liver**: enlarged and pale. Histologically, more than half of the hepatocytes are filled with lipid vacuoles; there is no necroinflammatory component.
- **Bile ducts**: intrahepatic cholestasis from compression of the canaliculi; ultrasound shows no extrahepatic obstruction unless the primary disease causes it (for example pancreatitis).
- **Adipose tissue and muscle**: the cat loses peripheral fat and muscle mass, but keeps its omental and falciform fat.

## Clinical signs

Prolonged anorexia (usually more than a few days), weight loss, **jaundice**, lethargy, vomiting, dehydration and weakness. **Ventroflexion of the neck** from hypokalaemia, hypophosphataemia or thiamine deficiency may be present, as well as ptyalism and dullness (from nausea or metabolic or **hepatic encephalopathy**) and bleeding from vitamin K deficiency.

## Diagnosis

- **Blood work**: markedly increased alkaline phosphatase with normal or mildly increased gamma-glutamyl transferase (a characteristic pattern; a high GGT points instead to cholangitis, pancreatitis or other bile duct disease), hyperbilirubinaemia, increased ALT; hypokalaemia, hypophosphataemia and hypomagnesaemia are common; non-regenerative anaemia.
- **Ultrasound**: enlarged, diffusely **hyperechoic** liver compared with the falciform fat; it also looks for the primary disease.
- **Fine-needle aspiration cytology**: severe lipid vacuolation in more than 80% of hepatocytes; together with blood work and ultrasound it is usually enough, and biopsy is not necessary.
- **Vitamin K1** before any invasive procedure (aspiration, catheter, feeding tube), because coagulopathy is common and can exist even with normal coagulation tests; and a systematic search for the cause of the anorexia.

## Treatment

The key is **nutrition**. Appetite stimulants will not recover cats with severe lipidosis and forcing food can create food aversion, so a **feeding tube** is placed: nasoesophageal during the first days while the cat is unstable, then an **oesophagostomy tube**, the preferred route, often kept for weeks. A diet with adequate feline protein (protein must not be restricted) is given, starting with a fraction of the energy requirement and increasing over several days, to avoid **refeeding syndrome** (hypophosphataemia, hypokalaemia and haemolysis). Also: fluid therapy, potassium and phosphate correction, vitamin K, thiamine, antiemetics and often vitamin B12, L-carnitine and antioxidant supplements. The primary disease is treated. With early, intensive nutritional support the lipidosis resolves within 2–3 weeks in most cats and recurrence is rare; the prognosis depends more on the severity of the disease at presentation (older age, signs of liver failure, hypoalbuminaemia) than on the type of underlying disease.

## Key studies

- **Center et al. (1993)**, retrospective study of 77 cats with severe hepatic lipidosis. Females and middle-aged cats predominated. Most had hepatomegaly, jaundice and dehydration and had lost 25% or more of their usual weight; ALT, AST and alkaline phosphatase were increased, with little or no increase in GGT. Coagulation tests were abnormal in 45% of the cats tested, although few bled. Of 42 cats given intensive nutritional and supportive care, 55% survived; a low PCV, hypokalaemia and older age were associated with death.
- **Biourge et al. (1994)**, experimental study in 15 obese cats subjected to prolonged voluntary fasting. Obesity alone did not cause fat to accumulate in the liver, but fasting caused lipidosis in all 15, with clinical disease in 12 after 5 to 7 weeks and a 30–35% loss of body weight. Alkaline phosphatase rose up to 3 weeks before hyperbilirubinaemia.
- **Yeager and Mohammed (1992)**, retrospective study of 36 cats with liver disease and a liver biopsy. A liver hyperechoic to falciform fat was the best ultrasound criterion for severe lipidosis, with 91% sensitivity and 100% specificity.
- **Kuzi et al. (2017)**, retrospective study of 71 cats with hepatic lipidosis and 85 controls with other diseases. 66% were female. A primary disease was found in most cats (gastrointestinal disease, pancreatitis or cholangiohepatitis in 44%, a stressful event in 20%) and 28% were idiopathic. Mortality was 38%. Older age, signs of liver failure, hypoalbuminaemia and, during hospitalisation, effusions or hypotension were associated with death; the type of primary disease was not.
- **Wallace et al. (2024)**, retrospective study of 48 cats. Three-month survival did not differ between cats fed within 12 hours of admission (68%) and those fed later (57%), and the latter were hospitalised for less time; older age and ascites were associated with death. A short stabilisation period before starting enteral feeding does not seem to worsen the outcome, although the study is small.
