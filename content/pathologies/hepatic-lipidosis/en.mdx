## What it is

Feline hepatic lipidosis is the most common acquired liver disease of the cat.

When a cat stops eating for several days, adipose tissue releases large amounts of fatty acids that reach the liver. The cat's metabolism makes it especially vulnerable:

- fasting quickly depletes nutrients such as taurine
- carnitine and other factors needed to use fat may fall short

The liver cannot oxidise or redistribute the fat as fast as it arrives. Triglycerides accumulate in the hepatocytes, which swell, compress the bile canaliculi and stop working. The result is **intrahepatic cholestasis** with **liver failure**.

In most cats lipidosis is **secondary** to another disease that caused the anorexia:

- inflammatory bowel disease or intestinal lymphoma
- cholangitis
- pancreatitis
- lower urinary tract disease
- diabetes mellitus
- hyperthyroidism
- kidney disease
- other neoplasia
- oral and dental disease that prevents eating

In other cats the anorexia is triggered by stress: moving house, a diet change, a new animal or boarding.

## Who it affects

Middle-aged adult cats of either sex. The risk is greater in **obese** cats that lose weight abruptly.

A weight-loss plan in which the cat refuses the new food can also trigger it.

## Anatomy involved

### Liver

Enlarged and pale. Histologically, more than half of the hepatocytes are filled with lipid vacuoles, with no necroinflammatory component.

### Bile ducts

There is intrahepatic cholestasis from compression of the canaliculi. Ultrasound shows no extrahepatic obstruction unless the primary disease causes it, for example pancreatitis.

### Adipose tissue and muscle

The cat loses peripheral fat and muscle mass, but keeps its omental and falciform fat.

## Clinical signs

Prolonged anorexia, usually of more than a few days, weight loss, **jaundice**, lethargy, vomiting, dehydration and weakness.

There may also be:

- **ventroflexion of the neck**, from hypokalaemia, hypophosphataemia or thiamine deficiency
- ptyalism and dullness, from nausea or from metabolic or hepatic encephalopathy
- bleeding from vitamin K deficiency

## Diagnosis

- Blood work shows markedly increased **alkaline phosphatase** with normal or mildly increased gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT). This pattern is characteristic: a high GGT points instead to cholangitis, pancreatitis or other bile duct disease.
- There is also hyperbilirubinaemia and increased ALT. Hypokalaemia, hypophosphataemia and hypomagnesaemia are common, and there is non-regenerative anaemia.
- On ultrasound the liver is enlarged and diffusely **hyperechoic** compared with the falciform fat. Ultrasound also looks for the primary disease.
- Fine-needle aspiration cytology shows severe lipid vacuolation in more than 80% of hepatocytes. Together with blood work and ultrasound it is usually enough, and biopsy is not necessary.

Coagulopathy is common and can exist even with normal coagulation tests. **Vitamin K1** is therefore given before any invasive procedure: aspiration, catheter or feeding tube.

The cause of the anorexia is always searched for systematically.

## Treatment

The key is **nutrition**. Appetite stimulants will not recover cats with severe lipidosis, and forcing food can create food aversion.

A **feeding tube** is therefore placed:

- nasoesophageal during the first days, while the cat is unstable
- then an **oesophagostomy tube**, the preferred route, often kept for weeks

The diet must have adequate protein for a cat; protein is not restricted. Feeding starts with a fraction of the energy requirement and increases over several days to avoid **refeeding syndrome**, with hypophosphataemia, hypokalaemia and haemolysis.

Also given:

- fluid therapy
- potassium and phosphate correction
- vitamin K and thiamine
- antiemetics
- often, vitamin B12, L-carnitine and antioxidant supplements

The primary disease is treated.

With early, intensive nutritional support the lipidosis resolves within 2–3 weeks in most cats, and recurrence is rare. The prognosis depends more on the severity at presentation (older age, signs of liver failure, hypoalbuminaemia) than on the type of underlying disease.

<summary>Key studies</summary>

- **Center et al. (1993).** Retrospective study of 77 cats with severe hepatic lipidosis. Females and middle-aged cats predominated. Most had hepatomegaly, jaundice and dehydration, and had lost 25% or more of their usual weight. ALT, AST and alkaline phosphatase were increased, with little or no increase in GGT. Coagulation tests were abnormal in 45% of the cats tested, although few bled. Of 42 cats given intensive nutritional and supportive care, 55% survived; a low PCV, hypokalaemia and older age were associated with death.
- **Biourge et al. (1994).** Experimental study in 15 obese cats subjected to prolonged voluntary fasting. Obesity alone did not cause fat to accumulate in the liver, but fasting caused lipidosis in all 15, with clinical disease in 12 after 5 to 7 weeks and a 30–35% loss of body weight. Alkaline phosphatase rose up to 3 weeks before hyperbilirubinaemia.
- **Yeager and Mohammed (1992).** Retrospective study of 36 cats with liver disease and a liver biopsy. A liver hyperechoic to falciform fat was the best ultrasound criterion for severe lipidosis, with 91% sensitivity and 100% specificity.
- **Kuzi et al. (2017).** Retrospective study of 71 cats with hepatic lipidosis and 85 controls with other diseases. 66% were female. A primary disease was found in most cats (gastrointestinal disease, pancreatitis or cholangiohepatitis in 44%, a stressful event in 20%) and 28% were idiopathic. Mortality was 38%. Older age, signs of liver failure, hypoalbuminaemia and, during hospitalisation, effusions or hypotension were associated with death; the type of primary disease was not.
- **Wallace et al. (2024).** Retrospective study of 48 cats. Three-month survival did not differ between cats fed within 12 hours of admission (68%) and those fed later (57%), and the latter were hospitalised for less time. Older age and ascites were associated with death. A short stabilisation period before starting enteral feeding does not seem to worsen the outcome, although the study is small.
