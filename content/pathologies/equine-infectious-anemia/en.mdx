---
title: Equine infectious anaemia
summary: Lifelong infection by a lentivirus transmitted mechanically by horse flies and other biting insects, and also by needles and blood products. Many infected horses show no signs but remain a source of infection; there is no treatment or widely used vaccine, and control relies on serological testing and culling or isolation of positive horses.
---

## What it is

Equine infectious anaemia (EIA, also called *swamp fever*) is caused by a **lentivirus** of the family *Retroviridae*. The virus multiplies in tissue **macrophages** and, like all retroviruses, integrates into the genome of infected cells: infection lasts **for life**.

It is transmitted by **blood**. **Horse flies** and, to a lesser extent, stable flies (*Stomoxys*) carry blood on their mouthparts when their feeding is interrupted and they move on to bite another nearby horse; the insect is not infected, it only carries the virus (**mechanical transmission**). Shared needles and surgical equipment and transfusions of contaminated blood or plasma also transmit it, and less often the transplacental route.

Infection has three forms that can follow one another in the same horse:

- **Acute**: fever and thrombocytopenia in the weeks after infection; it often goes unnoticed.
- **Chronic**: repeated episodes of fever, anaemia, oedema and weight loss, each with a new viral variant that escapes antibodies.
- **Inapparent carrier**: most infected horses control the virus and have no signs, but remain infectious, especially if stress or corticosteroids lower their immunity.

The anaemia has an **immune-mediated** component, with complement-mediated phagocytosis of red cells, and inflammatory cytokines also suppress red cell production. It is a WOAH-listed, notifiable disease.

## Who it affects

Horses, donkeys and mules. It occurs worldwide and is especially common in hot, humid regions with large biting fly populations, such as the wetlands of South America; it is also present in Europe. Risk is highest in areas with many horse flies, near **wetlands**, in working horses, where horse density is high and where needles are reused or blood products shared.

## Anatomy involved

- **Spleen**: rich in macrophages, it is where many red cells are removed. Acute and chronic episodes cause **splenomegaly**.
- **Liver**: the **liver macrophages** (Kupffer cells) also harbour the virus, phagocytose red cells and fill with **haemosiderin**. There is **hepatomegaly** with non-suppurative hepatitis and lymphocytic infiltrates.

## Clinical signs

- Intermittent **fever** and depression.
- **Anaemia**, with pale mucous membranes, in the chronic form.
- **Petechiae** on the mucous membranes, from thrombocytopenia.
- Ventral and limb **oedema**, progressive **weight loss**.
- Many horses have no signs at all and are only detected by testing.

## Diagnosis

- **Agar gel immunodiffusion** (Coggins test): the WOAH reference test, highly specific.
- **ELISA**, more sensitive, for screening; positives are confirmed by immunodiffusion and, if the results disagree, by **immunoblot**.
- Antibodies take weeks to appear, so a negative result shortly after exposure must be repeated.
- **PCR** in particular cases, such as very recent infections.
- Bloodwork: thrombocytopenia and anaemia.

## Treatment and prevention

- **There is no treatment**. Depending on each country's rules, positive horses are culled or kept **isolated for life**, at a distance from other equids and protected from insects.
- **There is no vaccine** in general use.
- Mandatory **serological testing** before movements, sales, competitions and exports, and active surveillance of the population in risk areas.
- **Prevention of iatrogenic transmission**: single-use needles and syringes, disinfection of equipment and testing of blood and plasma donors.
- **Insect control**: insecticides, repellents and fly control around the stables, and isolation of newly introduced horses until they test negative.

## Key studies

The evidence on EIA comes from surveillance and observational studies: there are no trials of treatments or licensed vaccines to assess.

- **Carvelli et al. (2024)**, analysis of mandatory active surveillance of Italy's entire equid population (about 406,000 animals) from 2007 to 2012: 1,337,899 samples, of which 0.18% were positive. Seroprevalence fell in all species over the period; mules had a much higher infection rate than horses (IRR 48.90) and donkeys a lower one (IRR 0.56), and holding-level prevalence was 1.15%. The authors conclude that passive surveillance alone is not enough to eradicate it. It is an observational study without a comparison group.
- **Scicluna et al. (2013)**, evaluation of Italy's three-tier diagnostic system (ELISA, immunodiffusion and immunoblot) on 74,880 samples. 44 were negative by immunodiffusion but positive by ELISA and immunoblot, most of them from mules. In a follow-up study of 10 mules, viral loads in animals with negative or weak immunodiffusion results were equivalent to those of clearly positive animals. Using immunodiffusion alone can miss infected equids. The follow-up included few animals.
- **de Pádua et al. (2022)**, cross-sectional study of 1,170 equids on 332 randomly selected farms in Goiás (Brazil). 2% of equids and 3.1% of farms were positive by immunodiffusion. Using a shared **vaccination gun** and having bodies of water on the farm were risk factors. Only immunodiffusion was used, which may underestimate prevalence.
- **Borges et al. (2013)**, cross-sectional study of 547 equids in the Brazilian Pantanal. Adjusted seroprevalence was 31.5% (95% CI 17.4–48.8%) and 52.0% of farms had at least one positive animal. Animals in regularly flooded areas were 146 times more likely to be positive, and those with clinical signs 3.74 times more likely. It is a very particular area, with few farms (25).
