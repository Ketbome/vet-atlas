---
title: Gastrointestinal foreign body
summary: Ingested objects that lodge in the oesophagus, stomach or intestine and cause obstruction. Linear foreign bodies plicate the intestine and can perforate it. A common reason for abdominal surgery in young dogs.
---

## What it is

Dogs frequently swallow indigestible objects: bones, toys, balls, stones, corn cobs, fruit stones, socks and other fabrics, and string or rope. Small objects pass in the faeces; larger ones stop where the tract narrows. **Mechanical obstruction** causes fluid and gas to accumulate proximally, with vomiting, dehydration, electrolyte and acid–base disorders. Pressure on the wall causes ischaemia, necrosis and perforation, leading to **septic peritonitis**. **Linear foreign bodies** (string, fabric) anchor at one point — often the pylorus or the base of the tongue — while peristalsis gathers the intestine along them, so that the bowel bunches up (plication) and the object cuts through the mesenteric border.

## Who it affects

Dogs of any age, but most often young dogs and breeds prone to chewing and swallowing objects, such as retrievers. Dogs that scavenge or eat bones are at risk of oesophageal foreign bodies.

## Anatomy involved

- **Oesophagus**: objects (often bones) lodge at the thoracic inlet, over the base of the heart or just cranial to the cardia, where the oesophagus cannot dilate as much.
- **Stomach**: many objects stay there for a long time with intermittent vomiting; the pylorus is a frequent point of obstruction and anchoring.
- **Small intestine**: the most common site of obstruction, usually the jejunum; linear bodies plicate the duodenum and jejunum.

## Clinical signs

Vomiting (acute and repeated with complete proximal obstruction, intermittent with partial or gastric bodies), anorexia, lethargy, abdominal pain and dehydration; sometimes diarrhoea. Oesophageal foreign bodies cause ptyalism, gagging, regurgitation and repeated swallowing. Fever, collapse and severe pain suggest perforation and peritonitis. Linear bodies may be seen under the tongue.

## Diagnosis

- **History** of chewing or a missing object.
- **Abdominal palpation**: occasionally the object or a plicated intestine can be felt.
- **Radiographs**: radiopaque objects; in obstruction, segmental gas-distended small intestinal loops (with a clear difference in diameter between dilated and normal loops); in linear bodies, bunched loops with small, comma-shaped gas bubbles. Free abdominal gas indicates perforation.
- **Ultrasound**: identifies radiolucent objects, plication, wall thickness and peritoneal fluid.
- **Contrast studies or endoscopy** in selected cases.

## Treatment

After fluid and electrolyte stabilisation: **endoscopic retrieval** for oesophageal and many gastric foreign bodies. Objects that cannot be removed endoscopically are removed surgically by **gastrotomy** or **enterotomy**; non-viable intestine is resected with **enterectomy and anastomosis**. Linear bodies require release of the anchor and often several enterotomies. Peritonitis is managed with lavage, drainage and antibiotics. Inducing vomiting is not advised for sharp or large objects. The prognosis is good when the condition is treated before perforation.
