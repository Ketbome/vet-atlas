## What it is

Equine asthma is a **chronic inflammatory disease of the lower airways**. It develops when the horse inhales particles from its environment:

- organic dust from hay and straw
- mould spores
- endotoxins and β-glucans
- forage mites

The bronchi and bronchioles develop **bronchospasm**, **excess mucus** and inflammation. If the inflammation persists, it leads to **remodelling** of the bronchial wall, with smooth muscle hypertrophy and fibrosis.

It is not an infection and is not contagious.

Two forms are recognised, now grouped under the term equine asthma.

### Mild-moderate asthma

It was formerly called inflammatory airway disease (IAD). It causes occasional cough and poor performance, without breathing difficulty at rest.

### Severe asthma

It was formerly called recurrent airway obstruction (RAO), or *heaves*. It causes episodes of **airflow obstruction with increased breathing effort at rest**. They resolve when the cause is removed and return with new exposures.

There is a summer pasture-associated variant in hot, humid climates. There is a hereditary component.

## Who it affects

Mild-moderate asthma affects horses of any age, often young sport horses or racehorses kept in stables.

Severe asthma affects mainly **adult and older horses**, stabled and fed **dry hay**. It worsens in winter, when they spend more time indoors.

Risk factors include:

- stabling
- dry hay, even of good quality
- straw bedding
- poor ventilation
- age

## Anatomy involved

### Lungs

The lesion lies in the **bronchioles** and other peripheral airways. There is smooth muscle contraction, mucus plugs and neutrophils in the lumen. Obstruction of the small airways traps air and forces active expiration.

### Trachea

Mucus coming up from the bronchi pools on the ventral floor of the trachea, especially at the thoracic inlet. There it is seen and scored by **endoscopy**. The tracheal wash is collected through the trachea, and the endoscope or tube for bronchoalveolar lavage is passed along it.

### External abdominal oblique muscle

In severe asthma expiration is no longer passive, and the abdominal muscles push at the end of each breath. Over time the external oblique **hypertrophies** and forms a ridge along the costal arch. This is the **heave line**, a sign of chronic disease.

## Clinical signs

### Mild-moderate asthma

- intermittent **cough**
- **poor performance**
- slow recovery after exercise
- sometimes, scant nasal discharge

Resting respiratory rate and effort are normal, and there is no fever.

### Severe asthma

- **increased breathing effort at rest**, mainly on expiration, with a **prolonged expiratory phase**
- a heave line in chronic cases
- frequent cough and mucoid nasal discharge
- exercise intolerance
- often, poor body condition

Wheezes and crackles are heard on auscultation. There is no fever either, which helps distinguish it from an infection.

## Diagnosis

- The history links the signs to stabling and hay. There is no fever and there are no systemic signs.
- Airway **endoscopy** shows the amount of mucus in the trachea.
- The key test is **bronchoalveolar lavage cytology**. In mild-moderate asthma more than 10% neutrophils, or more than 5% mast cells or eosinophils, is considered abnormal. In severe asthma neutrophils usually exceed 25%.
- **Lung function tests** and the response to a bronchodilator are done at specialist centres.
- Thoracic radiography and ultrasonography mainly serve to rule out other diseases.

## Treatment and prevention

Asthma is not cured. The aim is sustained **remission** while the environment is controlled.

### Environmental control

It is the basis of treatment:

- more time at **pasture**
- replacing dry hay with soaked or steamed hay, haylage or pelleted forage
- **shavings or cardboard** bedding instead of straw
- good ventilation
- not mucking out or handling hay with the horse inside

### Corticosteroids

Systemic corticosteroids (dexamethasone, prednisolone) control exacerbations. **Inhaled** ones (fluticasone, beclomethasone, ciclesonide) are given with an inhaler or a mask designed for horses.

### Bronchodilators

β2-agonists such as oral clenbuterol or inhaled salbutamol (albuterol) relieve obstruction. Intravenous N-butylscopolamine bromide is used in a crisis.

They do not treat inflammation, so they **should not be used alone**.

### Regulations

Drug use in horses is subject to each country's regulations and to **withdrawal periods**. In the EU the horse is a food-producing species. Its identification document (passport) states whether it is excluded from slaughter for human consumption, and that determines which drugs may be given. In competition, anti-doping rules also apply.

<summary>Key studies</summary>

- **ACVIM consensus (Couëtil et al., 2016).** It revises the definition of the mild-moderate form (then IAD): cough, poor performance, excess tracheal mucus, inflammatory bronchoalveolar lavage cytology or pulmonary dysfunction, without fever, systemic signs or increased resting respiratory effort. It is expert opinion based on the literature, not a study.
- **Hotchkiss et al. (2007).** Survey of owners randomly selected through veterinary practices in Great Britain, using a risk-screening questionnaire. The estimated true prevalence of severe asthma was 14.0% (95% CI 10.7–17.4%). Age, an urbanised environment, hay exposure and respiratory infections early in life were associated with the disease. Diagnosis relied on what owners described, not on examination.
- **Couëtil et al. (2005).** Randomised controlled trial in 28 horses with severe asthma kept outdoors on pelleted feed, given inhaled fluticasone, oral prednisone or placebo for 4 weeks. All horses improved, and at 4 weeks those treated with corticosteroids did not differ from controls. In severe cases, fluticasone sped up the improvement in lung function at 2 weeks. The authors conclude that environmental control is the most important factor. The sample is small.
- **Pirie et al. (2021).** Multicentre, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in 224 client-owned horses with severe asthma. After 10 days, 73.4% of horses treated with inhaled ciclesonide (80 of 109) improved by at least 30% in clinical score, versus 43.2% with placebo (48 of 111), with few adverse effects. There was a marked placebo effect, and several authors were employees of the drug's manufacturer.
- **Pistocchini et al. (2025).** Meta-analysis of 4 trials with 252 asthmatic horses comparing inhaled corticosteroids with placebo. About 3 horses need to be treated for one to improve clearly (NNT 3.2; 95% CI 2.3–4.7), and the probability of improvement was 1.73 times that with placebo. GRADE rated the quality of evidence as high. The trials are few and short.
- **Kinnison et al. (2022).** Systematic scoping review of the definition of mild-moderate asthma: 44 studies with 6,092 horses, mostly racing Thoroughbreds. They were mostly observational, with a median of 74 horses per study and a high risk of bias for statistical power and masking in almost half. Heterogeneity precluded meta-analysis. The link between cough and tracheal mucus was more consistent than that between lavage cytology and lung function. The evidence behind the definition of the mild-moderate form is limited and heterogeneous.
