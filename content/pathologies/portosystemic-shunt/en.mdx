---
title: Portosystemic shunt
summary: Abnormal vessel that carries portal blood from the digestive tract directly into the systemic circulation, bypassing the liver. Toxins such as ammonia reach the brain, causing hepatic encephalopathy, and urate stones form in the urinary tract.
---

## What it is

Normally the portal vein carries blood from the stomach, intestines, pancreas and spleen to the liver, which metabolises ammonia and other substances absorbed from the gut. A **portosystemic shunt (PSS)** is a vessel that connects the portal system to the caudal vena cava or the azygos vein, so that portal blood skips the liver. The liver receives less portal flow and remains small (atrophy), and ammonia and other toxins accumulate in the blood.

- **Congenital shunts** are usually single. **Intrahepatic** shunts (often a patent *ductus venosus*) are typical of large breeds; **extrahepatic** shunts (for example portocaval or portoazygos) are typical of small and toy breeds.
- **Acquired shunts** are multiple, small extrahepatic vessels that open secondarily in response to portal hypertension, for example in cirrhosis.

## Who it affects

Congenital shunts usually cause signs in young dogs, often in the first year. Extrahepatic shunts: Yorkshire terrier, Maltese, Havanese, miniature schnauzer, pug, shih tzu and other small breeds. Intrahepatic shunts: Irish wolfhound, Labrador and golden retrievers, Australian cattle dog, Old English sheepdog. Acquired shunts appear in adults with chronic liver disease.

## Anatomy involved

- **Liver**: small, with hypoplasia of the intrahepatic portal branches (microhepatia).
- **Portal vein and caudal vena cava**: the anomalous vessel joins the portal system or one of its tributaries (left gastric, splenic, gastroduodenal veins) to the caudal vena cava or the azygos vein.
- **Brain**: ammonia and other toxins cause **hepatic encephalopathy**.
- **Kidneys and urinary bladder**: hyperammonaemia and hyperuricaemia lead to **ammonium urate crystals and uroliths**, with renomegaly in some dogs.

## Clinical signs

Stunted growth and poor body condition; neurological signs of hepatic encephalopathy that often worsen after eating a protein-rich meal: dullness, disorientation, head pressing, circling, apparent blindness, ataxia and seizures. Prolonged recovery from sedation or anaesthesia. Urinary signs from urate stones: haematuria, pollakiuria, stranguria or obstruction. Vomiting, diarrhoea, polyuria and polydipsia.

## Diagnosis

- **Blood work**: microcytosis, low urea, low albumin, low cholesterol and glucose, raised ammonia.
- **Liver function tests**: increased fasting and postprandial **bile acids** or a raised ammonia (fasting or after an ammonia tolerance test).
- **Urinalysis**: ammonium biurate crystals.
- **Imaging**: abdominal ultrasound with Doppler identifies many shunts; **CT angiography** is the reference method to map the vessel and plan surgery. Portovenography is used intraoperatively.

## Treatment

**Medical management** stabilises the patient and controls encephalopathy: a moderately protein-restricted, highly digestible diet, lactulose (which acidifies the colon and traps ammonia), and antibiotics such as metronidazole or neomycin to reduce ammonia-producing bacteria; seizures are treated with anticonvulsants. Single congenital shunts are ideally **attenuated surgically**, gradually, with an ameroid constrictor or cellophane band (extrahepatic) or by percutaneous transvenous coil embolisation (intrahepatic). Gradual closure prevents acute portal hypertension. Acquired shunts are not closed; the underlying liver disease is managed.
