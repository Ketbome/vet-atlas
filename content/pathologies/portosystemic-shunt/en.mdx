---
title: Portosystemic shunt
summary: Abnormal vessel that carries portal blood from the digestive tract directly into the systemic circulation, bypassing the liver. Toxins such as ammonia reach the brain, causing hepatic encephalopathy, and urate stones form in the urinary tract.
---

## What it is

Normally the portal vein carries blood from the stomach, intestines, pancreas and spleen to the liver, which metabolises ammonia and other substances absorbed from the gut. A **portosystemic shunt (PSS)** is a vessel that connects the portal system to the caudal vena cava or the azygos vein, so that portal blood skips the liver. The liver receives less portal flow and remains small (atrophy), and ammonia and other toxins accumulate in the blood.

- **Congenital shunts** are usually single. **Intrahepatic** shunts (often a patent *ductus venosus*) are typical of large breeds; **extrahepatic** shunts (for example portocaval or portoazygos) are typical of small and toy breeds.
- **Acquired shunts** are nests of multiple, small, tortuous extrahepatic vessels that open secondarily in response to portal hypertension, for example in chronic liver disease with fibrosis.

## Who it affects

Congenital shunts usually cause signs in young animals. Extrahepatic shunts: Yorkshire terrier, Maltese, miniature schnauzer, pug, shih tzu and other small breeds. Intrahepatic shunts: Irish wolfhound, Labrador and golden retrievers, German shepherd, Dobermann and other large breeds. Acquired shunts appear in animals with chronic liver disease or other causes of portal hypertension.

## Anatomy involved

- **Liver**: small, with hypoplasia of the intrahepatic portal branches (microhepatia).
- **Portal vein and caudal vena cava**: the anomalous vessel joins the portal system or one of its tributaries (left gastric, splenic, gastroduodenal veins) to the caudal vena cava or the azygos vein.
- **Brain**: ammonia and other toxins cause **hepatic encephalopathy**.
- **Kidneys and urinary bladder**: hyperammonaemia and hyperuricaemia lead to **ammonium urate crystals and uroliths**, with renomegaly in some dogs.

## Clinical signs

Stunted growth and poor body condition; neurological signs of hepatic encephalopathy, which may wax and wane and worsen after a meal (only in some patients) or with a high-protein diet: dullness, disorientation, head pressing, circling, apparent blindness, ataxia and seizures. Intolerance to drugs metabolised by the liver, often noticed at neutering. Urinary signs from urate stones: haematuria, pollakiuria, stranguria or obstruction. Vomiting, diarrhoea, polyuria and polydipsia.

## Diagnosis

- **Blood work**: microcytosis, low urea, low albumin, low cholesterol and glucose, raised ammonia.
- **Liver function tests**: increased fasting and postprandial **bile acids** or a raised ammonia (fasting or after an ammonia tolerance test).
- **Urinalysis**: ammonium biurate crystals.
- **Imaging**: abdominal ultrasound with Doppler identifies many shunts; **CT angiography** is the reference method to map the vessel and plan surgery. Portovenography is used intraoperatively.

## Treatment

**Medical management** stabilises the patient and controls encephalopathy: a highly digestible diet with appropriate protein (severe protein restriction is avoided), lactulose, and antibiotics such as metronidazole (at a reduced dose) or ampicillin; neomycin is no longer recommended because it is nephrotoxic and ototoxic; seizures are treated with anticonvulsants. Single congenital shunts are ideally **attenuated surgically**, gradually, with an ameroid constrictor or cellophane band (extrahepatic) or by percutaneous transvenous coil embolisation (intrahepatic). Gradual closure is used because most animals do not tolerate acute complete occlusion, which causes portal hypertension. Acquired shunts must not be ligated, because they decompress the portal hypertension; the underlying disease and the encephalopathy are managed medically.

## In the cat

In the cat, congenital portosystemic shunts are usually **single and extrahepatic**, and mainly affect domestic shorthair cats, although the Persian, Himalayan, Siamese and Burmese are represented. Signs appear in kittens and young cats, and some are characteristic of the species:

- **Ptyalism** (excessive salivation), present in the vast majority of affected cats.
- **Copper-coloured irises** in some cats.
- Neurological signs (dullness, ataxia, apparent blindness, behavioural changes, **seizures**), stunted growth and slow recovery from anaesthesia.
- Ammonium urate uroliths, with haematuria or urinary obstruction.

Diagnosis (bile acids, ammonia, Doppler ultrasound, CT angiography) and initial medical management are similar to those in the dog. Gradual **surgical attenuation** or ligation of the anomalous vessel is the treatment of choice, but in the cat **postoperative neurological complications**, including seizures, are more frequent than in the dog and worsen the prognosis; even so, many cats that undergo surgery do well in the long term.

## Key studies in the cat

- **Weisse et al. (2024)**, CT angiography of 231 cats with congenital extrahepatic shunts from 29 institutions. Twenty-five different anatomies were found, five of which accounted for 78% of shunts, and the left gastric vein was involved in the origin of the shunt in 75% of the classified types.
- **Lipscomb et al. (2007)**, 49 cats with surgical ligation of a congenital shunt. Two died perioperatively; of 36 with long-term follow-up, 83% were alive at a median of 47 months, most without signs, medication or a special diet. The main complication was neurological signs, in 37% of cats, which did not depend on shunt type, degree of attenuation, age or preoperative seizures; 56% of these cats recovered normal neurological function.
- **Strickland et al. (2021)**, retrospective cohort of 50 cats with a single congenital shunt. Post-attenuation neurological signs appeared in 62%, including seizures in 11 cats, and 5 of the affected cats died before discharge. Preoperative levetiracetam did not prevent them; the only risk factor found was a lower osmolality 24 hours after surgery.
- **Otero Balda et al. (2025)**, 59 cats with post-attenuation neurological signs from 10 institutions. 78% were alive at 30 days, but only 50% of cats with seizures; seizures and the need for propofol were associated with lower survival.
- **Mohammadi et al. (2024)**, meta-analysis of 12 studies: post-attenuation neurological signs occurred in 38.9% of cats and seizures in 20.2%, and mortality among affected cats was 17% and 37.2%, respectively. The evidence supporting preventive antiepileptics such as levetiracetam is limited.
