---
title: Cervical spondylomyelopathy (wobbler syndrome)
summary: Compression of the caudal cervical spinal cord and nerve roots by disc protrusion, ligament hypertrophy or malformed vertebrae. It causes a progressive ataxia known as wobbler syndrome in large and giant breed dogs and in young horses.
---

## What it is

Cervical spondylomyelopathy (CSM) groups the disorders of the caudal cervical spine that narrow the vertebral canal and compress the spinal cord, often in a **dynamic** way that changes with the position of the neck. Two main forms are recognised:

- **Disc-associated CSM**: protrusion of the dorsal anulus of one or more caudal cervical discs, sometimes with hypertrophy of the ligamentum flavum. Compression may worsen with extension of the neck.
- **Osseous-associated CSM**: malformation and proliferation of the articular processes, pedicles or vertebral arch, which narrow the canal from the sides and from above.

After surgery at one site, the adjacent disc may develop a new compression (the "domino lesion").

## Who it affects

- Disc-associated form: middle-aged to older large-breed dogs, classically the **Dobermann**.
- Osseous-associated form: young adult **giant breeds**, such as the Great Dane, mastiff, Bernese mountain dog and Rottweiler.

## Anatomy involved

The lesions sit mostly between **C5 and C7**, at the C5–C6 and C6–C7 discs in the Dobermann; in giant breeds several sites, usually between C4 and C7, are often affected. In this region the cervical intumescence gives rise to the nerves of the thoracic limb, so compression can affect both the long tracts to the pelvic limbs and the lower motor neurons of the thoracic limbs. The **articular processes** of the caudal cervical vertebrae are frequent sites of osseous proliferation.

## Clinical signs

Acute or slowly progressive **ataxia**, most evident in the pelvic limbs, which take long, protracted strides. In many dogs the thoracic limbs take short, stiff steps while the pelvic limbs are ataxic ("two-engine gait"). Neck pain is variable. Advanced cases are tetraparetic.

## Diagnosis

- **Neurological examination** localising the lesion to C6–T2 or C1–C5.
- **Survey radiographs** show vertebral abnormalities but cannot confirm the diagnosis; they help to rule out discospondylitis or bone tumours.
- **MRI** is the method of choice; **CT myelography** with traction or flexion-extension views shows the dynamic component.

## Treatment

**Medical**: restricted activity, corticosteroids and, if needed, weight loss; signs improve in about half of dogs and stay unchanged in about a quarter. **Surgical**: ventral slot for a single disc protrusion, distraction-stabilisation techniques for dynamic lesions, and dorsal laminectomy for dorsal or multiple compressions. About 80% of dogs do well after surgery.

## In the horse

In the horse the disease is called **cervical vertebral compressive myelopathy** or **cervical vertebral malformation-malarticulation**, and it is one of the most common causes of **ataxia**. Compression is caused by **malformation of the vertebrae** and their articular processes, **stenosis of the vertebral canal** and osteoarthritis of the articular process joints. Two types are described, which often overlap:

- **Type 1, dynamic instability**: young horses, from a few months to about 2 years of age, with malformed, unstable vertebrae that compress the cord when the neck is **flexed**, mainly between C3 and C5. It is linked to **rapid growth**, high-energy diets and a genetic predisposition.
- **Type 2, static stenosis**: somewhat older or adult horses, in which **osteoarthritis of the articular processes**, and hypertrophy of the ligamentum flavum narrow the canal permanently, mainly between C5 and C7.

It affects **males** and **Thoroughbreds**, **warmbloods** and Tennessee Walking Horses more often. Compression can occur anywhere between **C2 and C7**, most often at C3–C4 and C4–C5. C6 has a ventral lamina on its transverse process that may be absent on one or both sides, a common anatomical variation whose link with the disease is unproven.

Signs are **ataxia** with paresis and spasticity, usually worse in the pelvic than in the thoracic limbs, with dragging of the hooves; gait asymmetry is frequent. Ataxia worsens when the horse is walked with the head raised or backed up. Severity is graded on a numerical neurological scale. Neck pain (cervical hyperaesthesia) is frequent. An ataxic horse is **dangerous to ride** and handle.

Diagnosis combines the **neurological examination** with **lateral radiographs of the neck**, on which the ratio between the minimum sagittal diameter of the canal and the height of the vertebral body (intra- and intervertebral) is measured; a low ratio indicates stenosis. **Myelography** and **CT myelography** localise the compression, although their agreement with necropsy is imperfect. Other neurological diseases, such as equine protozoal myeloencephalitis, must be ruled out, with cerebrospinal fluid analysis among other tests.

Conservative treatment of foals with type 1 is based on **restricting dietary energy and protein** and exercise to slow growth, with anti-inflammatory drugs; in adults with osteoarthritis, corticosteroids are injected into the articular process joints. **Ventral interbody fusion** of the affected vertebrae is the most widely used surgical technique, but its evidence comes from retrospective case series. Many affected horses are euthanised, and drug use is subject to **national regulations** and **withdrawal periods**, because the horse is a food-producing animal in many countries.

## Key studies

- **Levine et al. (2008)**, case-control study of 811 horses with cervical vertebral compressive myelopathy and 805 controls from the North American veterinary hospital database (1974–2007). **Geldings** (OR 2.0; 95% CI 1.5–2.6) and **entire males** (OR 2.4; 95% CI 1.8–3.2) were at higher risk than females; Thoroughbreds, Tennessee Walking Horses and warmbloods were overrepresented compared with Quarter Horses, and horses under 7 years had higher odds than those aged 10 or more. It is based on hospital discharge diagnoses without uniform confirmation.
- **Levine et al. (2010)**, multicentre case-control study of 270 horses with cervical vertebral malformation-malarticulation and 608 controls from 6 hospitals (1992–2007). Cases were younger (median 2 versus 7 years) and more often Thoroughbreds, warmbloods or Tennessee Walking Horses. Agreement with necropsy was **65–71% for radiography** and **67–78% for myelography**. Of 263 horses with a known outcome, 65.4% were euthanised. It is retrospective and the population comes from referral hospitals.
- **Hoffman and Clark (2013)**, retrospective study of 103 Thoroughbreds with a presumptive diagnosis of cervical malformation managed conservatively. Of the 70 discharged for treatment, **30% went on to race**; those that raced had a lower neurological grade, and kyphosis or cranial stenosis on radiographs were associated with not racing. Diagnosis was presumptive, without myelographic or necropsy confirmation in every horse.
- **Saville et al. (2017)**, prospective study of 97 horses examined by two board-certified internists, the second unaware of the history. They agreed that 60 were abnormal and 21 normal, and disagreed on 16. Agreement was good or excellent for the presence of ataxia or lameness in 80% of horses, but only in 20% for spasticity, muscle atrophy and **overall severity**. It shows how subjective the neurological examination on which diagnosis rests is; the data were collected in 1997–1998.
- **Strootmann et al. (2026)**, retrospective study of pre-purchase radiographs of 200 sound warmbloods. Absence of the ventral lamina of **C6** was found in 30% and remodelling of the C6–C7 joints in 32.5%, with no association with competition results. It indicates that this variation is common in sound horses and should not be read as disease on its own. It is retrospective, and performance was measured only by competition results.
