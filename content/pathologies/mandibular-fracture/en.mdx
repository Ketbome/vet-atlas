## What it is

Mandibular fractures account for about 90% of maxillofacial injuries in the dog.

Most result from **trauma**: fights with other animals, vehicle accidents and falls.

In small and toy breeds, severe **periodontal disease** destroys the alveolar bone around the roots of the teeth. The mandible becomes so thin that it may break with minimal force, a **pathological fracture**. Oral tumours can also cause pathological fractures.

## Who it affects

Dogs of any age and breed after trauma. Pathological fractures are typical of toy and small breeds with advanced periodontitis.

## Anatomy involved

Each half of the **mandible** has a **body**, which carries the teeth, and a **ramus**, with three processes:

- the coronoid process, where the temporalis inserts
- the condylar process, which forms the temporomandibular joint
- the angular process

The two halves are joined rostrally by the **mandibular symphysis**, a fibrocartilaginous joint.

Inside the body runs the **mandibular canal**, with the inferior alveolar artery, vein and nerve.

The roots of the **mandibular first molar** occupy a larger share of the body the smaller the dog, and the bone over them is thin. This is a weak point: almost half of mandibular fractures occur there.

## Clinical signs

- acute malocclusion, with the midline of the mandible displaced towards the side of the fracture
- a mouth that hangs open, especially in bilateral fractures
- pain
- inability to eat

## Diagnosis

- The trauma patient is evaluated first: thorax, neurological status and other injuries.
- Oral examination **under general anaesthesia**, checking occlusion and the teeth in the fracture line.
- **Intraoral dental radiographs** and, ideally, **CT** of the head. CT avoids the superimposition of skull radiographs and shows fractures of the ramus and temporomandibular joint.

## Treatment

The priority is to **restore normal occlusion**. The jaw must be reduced with the teeth in their normal relationship.

The options are:

- a tape muzzle, for minimally displaced fractures
- interdental wiring with an intraoral acrylic or composite splint
- intraosseous wiring
- miniplates
- external fixators

Implants must avoid the **tooth roots** and the mandibular canal.

Teeth in the fracture line are kept if they are healthy and stabilise the fragments. They are extracted if they are loose or periodontally diseased.

Pathological fractures heal poorly and need treatment of the periodontal disease. In some cases partial mandibulectomy is an option.

A feeding tube may be needed while the fracture heals.

### Causes and injuries

In the cat mandibular fractures are caused mainly by **road traffic accidents** and **fights with other animals**. Falls from height are a less common cause.

The most common injury is **mandibular symphyseal separation**. It is part of high-rise syndrome, together with fracture of the hard palate.

Fractures of the ramus, the condylar process and the temporomandibular joint are also common. Temporomandibular joint injuries are usually unilateral; bilateral ones are more typical of falls from height.

Pathological fractures from periodontal disease or oral tumours also occur in cats.

### Treatment

The feline jaws are small, and the tooth roots and the mandibular canal leave little room for implants. The most used techniques are:

- **circumferential cerclage** wire around the mandible, for symphyseal separation and fractures of the incisive region
- **maxillomandibular fixation** with composite or acrylic bonding the upper and lower canines; it reduces the fragments less precisely than internal fixation and carries a risk of aspiration if the cat vomits
- intraoral splints, miniplates or external fixators, for fractures of the body

An **oesophagostomy tube** is recommended with maxillomandibular fixation. With other techniques it is only occasionally needed.

<summary>Key studies in the cat</summary>

- **Knight and Meeson (2019).** CT of 75 cats with skull fractures. 89% had fractures in several bones, mostly of the mandible, upper jaw and craniofacial region, and 56% had temporomandibular joint injuries. Road traffic accidents caused 89% of cases and were associated with ocular, neurological and thoracic injuries. Half were managed without surgery. Mortality was 8%, complications occurred in 22% and occlusion problems after treatment were uncommon.
- **Mestrinho et al. (2022).** CT of 158 temporomandibular joints in 79 cats from six centres. 70.9% of injuries were unilateral. The mandibular condyle was fractured in 55.7% of joints, mostly intra-articular and through its medial half. 32.9% of cats had a luxation, mostly rostrodorsal. Bilateral injuries were 13.1 times more likely after falls from height.
- **Evenhuis et al. (2025).** 109 cats with mandibular trauma. The sites most often injured were the symphysis (55.0%), the condylar process (49.5%) and the mid ramus (48.6%). Open reduction and internal fixation healed better than maxillomandibular fixation and left less persistent malocclusion (9.1% versus 53.9%). An oesophagostomy tube did not change weight trends, and 94.5% of cats survived. Treatment was not randomised.
