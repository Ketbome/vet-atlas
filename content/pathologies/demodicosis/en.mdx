## What it is

***Demodex canis*** is a normal inhabitant of the canine **hair follicle** in small numbers.

Puppies acquire it from their dam by direct contact during the first days of life. It is not contagious between adult dogs.

Demodicosis develops when the mites multiply uncontrollably because the host's immune control of them fails.

Other forms are recognised:

- *D. injai*, a long mite that lives in hair follicles and sebaceous glands and is associated with greasy skin and hair on the dorsal trunk
- a short form found in the stratum corneum

### Classification

It is classified by age at onset and extent.

- **Juvenile-onset** disease occurs in young dogs, with a hereditary predisposition. Localised forms often resolve without treatment.
- **Adult-onset** disease occurs in dogs with no previous history. It usually reflects an underlying condition such as hyperadrenocorticism, hypothyroidism, neoplasia or immunosuppressive treatment.
- **Localised** disease has a few patches. **Generalised** disease has widespread lesions, often with involvement of the paws.

## Who it affects

Juvenile-onset disease occurs in puppies and young dogs, often in breeds such as:

- shar-pei
- bulldogs
- pug
- Staffordshire bull terrier
- Dobermann

Adult-onset disease affects older dogs with predisposing conditions.

## Anatomy involved

The mites live in the **hair follicles** of the **skin** (*cutis*). Their overgrowth damages the follicle and causes hair loss and folliculitis.

If the follicle ruptures, keratin and mites in the dermis cause **furunculosis** and deep pyoderma.

Early lesions are typical around the eyes, on the lips and on the forelimbs. Pododemodicosis affects the paws.

## Clinical signs

- patchy alopecia
- erythema and scaling
- comedones
- hyperpigmentation

**Pruritus** is absent or mild unless there is secondary infection.

Generalised disease may progress to deep pyoderma with swelling, lymphadenopathy, fever and malaise.

## Diagnosis

- **Deep skin scrapings** after squeezing the skin, until capillary bleeding, to bring mites out of the follicles.
- Trichograms (plucked hairs) and tape impressions after squeezing. They are useful in sensitive areas, such as around the eyes and the paws.
- Biopsy, when scrapings and trichograms are negative but demodicosis is still suspected.
- In adult-onset cases, investigation of underlying disease.

## Treatment

Generalised demodicosis is now usually treated with **isoxazolines** (afoxolaner, fluralaner, sarolaner, lotilaner), which are effective and safe.

Older options include amitraz rinses and macrocyclic lactones. Ivermectin and similar drugs are dangerous at high doses in dogs with the **ABCB1 (MDR1) mutation**, common in collies and related herding breeds.

Treatment continues until repeated skin scrapings are negative. Also:

- secondary pyoderma is treated at the same time
- glucocorticoids are avoided
- in adults the underlying disease is managed

Dogs with juvenile-onset generalised demodicosis should not be used for breeding.
