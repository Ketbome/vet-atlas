title: Elbow dysplasia
summary: Group of inherited developmental disorders of the elbow joint in young dogs of medium and large breeds. Fragmented medial coronoid process, osteochondrosis of the humeral condyle, ununited anconeal process and joint incongruity all end in osteoarthritis.
---

## What it is

Elbow dysplasia is not a single disease but a collective term for several developmental lesions of the elbow (*articulatio cubiti*) that share a common outcome: incongruity of the joint and early **osteoarthritis**. The recognised components are:

- **Medial coronoid disease** (fragmented medial coronoid process of the ulna), one of the most frequent.
- **Osteochondrosis / osteochondritis dissecans (OCD)** of the medial part of the humeral condyle.
- **Ununited anconeal process (UAP)**, a failure of the anconeal process to fuse with the ulna.
- **Elbow incongruity**, a step between the articular surfaces of radius and ulna (short radius or short ulna).

The causes are multifactorial: a strong genetic component, rapid growth, osteochondrosis and unequal length of the radius and ulna.

## Who it affects

Young, fast-growing dogs of medium, large and giant breeds: Labrador and golden retriever, Rottweiler, Bernese mountain dog, German shepherd (typical breed for UAP). Signs usually start between 4 and 10 months of age, although some cases, especially bilateral ones, are not diagnosed until after 1 year of age. It is often bilateral.

## Anatomy involved

The elbow is a composite joint between the **humeral condyle**, the **trochlear notch of the ulna** and the **head of the radius**. The medial coronoid process of the ulna and the medial part of the humeral condyle carry a large share of the load; when the radius and ulna do not form a smooth joint surface, that region is overloaded and the coronoid cartilage and subchondral bone fragment. The anconeal process, which locks into the olecranon fossa of the humerus during extension, has a separate ossification centre in some large breeds that normally fuses with the ulna by 5–6 months; if it does not, it stays loose.

## Clinical signs

Thoracic limb lameness, stiffness after rest, outward rotation of the limb or abduction of the elbow, pain on flexion and extension of the elbow, joint effusion and, in chronic cases, reduced range of motion, crepitus and muscle atrophy.

## Diagnosis

- **Radiographs** of both elbows in several projections. The flexed mediolateral view shows UAP. Medial coronoid disease is often not visible directly; indirect signs include sclerosis of the coronoid process, signs of osteoarthritis and incongruity.
- **CT**: the most useful imaging method to see coronoid fragments and incongruity.
- **Arthroscopy**: confirms and treats cartilage lesions and fragments.
- **Screening**: radiographic grading schemes (IEWG, OFA and kennel club schemes) are used to select breeding animals.

## Treatment

Removal of the coronoid fragments or OCD flap, usually by arthroscopy, followed by weight control, controlled exercise, physiotherapy and NSAIDs. UAP is treated by fixation of the process or by its removal, often combined with a proximal ulnar osteotomy. Corrective osteotomies of the ulna or radius are used for marked incongruity. Surgery does not stop osteoarthritis, so long-term management of the arthritic joint is part of the plan. Prevention relies on breeding from unaffected dogs.
