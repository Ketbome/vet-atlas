## What it is

Elbow dysplasia is not a single disease. It is a collective term for several developmental lesions of the elbow (*articulatio cubiti*) that share a common outcome: incongruity of the joint and early **osteoarthritis**.

The recognised components are:

- **Medial coronoid disease**, or fragmented medial coronoid process of the ulna. It is one of the most frequent.
- **Osteochondrosis or osteochondritis dissecans (OCD)** of the medial part of the humeral condyle.
- **Ununited anconeal process (UAP)**, when the anconeal process fails to fuse with the ulna.
- **Elbow incongruity**, a step between the articular surfaces of the radius and ulna, from a short radius or a short ulna.

The causes are multifactorial:

- a strong genetic component
- rapid growth
- osteochondrosis
- unequal length of the radius and ulna

## Who it affects

Young, fast-growing dogs of medium, large and giant breeds:

- Labrador and golden retriever
- Rottweiler
- Bernese mountain dog
- German shepherd, the typical breed for UAP

Signs usually start between **4 and 10 months** of age. Some cases, especially bilateral ones, are not diagnosed until after 1 year of age.

It is often bilateral.

## Anatomy involved

The elbow is a composite joint between the **humeral condyle**, the **trochlear notch of the ulna** and the **head of the radius**.

The medial coronoid process of the ulna and the medial part of the humeral condyle carry a large share of the load. When the radius and ulna do not form a smooth joint surface, that region is overloaded, and the coronoid cartilage and subchondral bone fragment.

The anconeal process locks into the olecranon fossa of the humerus during extension. In some large breeds it has a separate ossification centre, which normally fuses with the ulna by 5–6 months. If it does not, it stays loose.

## Clinical signs

- thoracic limb lameness
- stiffness after rest
- outward rotation of the limb or abduction of the elbow
- pain on flexion and extension of the elbow
- joint effusion
- in chronic cases, reduced range of motion, crepitus and muscle atrophy

## Diagnosis

- **Radiographs** of both elbows in several projections. The flexed mediolateral view shows UAP. Medial coronoid disease is often not visible directly; its indirect signs are sclerosis of the coronoid process, signs of osteoarthritis and incongruity.
- **CT**. The most useful imaging method to see coronoid fragments and incongruity.
- **Arthroscopy**. Confirms and treats cartilage lesions and fragments.
- **Screening**. Radiographic grading schemes (IEWG, OFA and kennel club schemes) are used to select breeding animals.

## Treatment

The coronoid fragments or the OCD flap are removed, usually by arthroscopy. This is followed by:

- weight control
- controlled exercise
- physiotherapy
- NSAIDs

UAP is treated by fixation of the process or by its removal, often combined with a proximal ulnar osteotomy.

Corrective osteotomies of the ulna or radius are used for marked incongruity.

Surgery does not stop **osteoarthritis**, so long-term management of the arthritic joint is part of the plan.

Prevention relies on breeding from unaffected dogs.
