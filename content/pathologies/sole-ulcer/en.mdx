## What it is

Sole ulcer is a **claw horn disruption lesion**.

The **corium** of the sole is repeatedly compressed between the third phalanx and the ground, bleeds and stops producing healthy horn. A **sole haemorrhage** appears first. If the insult continues, horn growth is interrupted and the corium protrudes through the defect, with an appearance that ranges from fresh red to brown and necrotic.

The typical lesion lies at the **sole-heel junction**, beneath the flexor tuberosity of the distal phalanx. A variant occurs at the tip of the claw, the toe ulcer.

### Factors

Mechanical, hormonal and metabolic changes, for example around calving, weaken the suspensory apparatus of the distal phalanx, which can sink inside the horn capsule.

Compression increases with:

- time spent standing on hard floors
- uncomfortable beds
- poor trimming
- overloading of the lateral claw of the pelvic limb

Negative energy balance and inflammation are also thought to contribute.

### White line disease

It shares these factors. The junction between wall and sole separates, bacteria and foreign material enter and an abscess can form, mostly in the abaxial heel region of the lateral claw of the pelvic limb.

A draining tract can extend up to the coronary band or the bulb of the heel.

## Who it affects

Mainly **high-yielding dairy cows** in free-stall housing with concrete floors. It is more common in **multiparous** cows and from mid-lactation onwards.

It is much rarer in first-calf heifers and in cows housed on deep-bedded packs.

## Anatomy involved

### Claws (*ungulae*)

The ulcer lies in the sole, at the sole-heel junction, mostly of the **lateral claw of the pelvic limb**, which bears more weight than the medial claw. In the thoracic limb it is the medial claw that bears more weight.

White line disease affects the white line, the junction between wall and sole.

Beneath the horn lie the corium and the **digital cushion**, which helps absorb and dissipate the forces of locomotion.

### Phalanges of the pelvic and thoracic limbs

The **distal phalanx** rests on the corium of the sole. Its **flexor tuberosity**, where the deep digital flexor tendon inserts, lies just above the typical ulcer site.

If infection progresses deeply it can cause:

- osteitis of the distal phalanx
- septic arthritis of the **distal interphalangeal joint**
- bursitis of the navicular bone
- tenosynovitis of the deep digital flexor

## Clinical signs

- **lameness** of varying severity, often in one pelvic limb
- changes in weight bearing to unload the painful claw
- on paring the sole, haemorrhage in the horn or a defect with protruding corium
- pain on pressure with hoof testers

In white line disease there is a haemorrhage, separation or abscess in the white line.

With deep complications, asymmetrical swelling of the foot appears, above the dewclaws if the tendon sheath is involved. Lameness is severe and the cow does not bear weight on the limb.

## Diagnosis

- **Inspection of the lifted foot** and **functional trimming**. The lesion is seen when sole horn is removed, and hoof testers help locate the pain.
- **Locomotion scoring** of the herd, to detect lame cows early.
- Differential diagnosis from other claw horn lesions, puncture wounds and infectious foot diseases.

## Treatment and prevention

- **Therapeutic trimming**: loose horn is removed and the injured area unloaded by lowering the affected claw.
- **Block** glued to the sound claw, to raise the affected one and relieve weight bearing.
- **Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs** to control pain and inflammation.
- For deep complications, surgery: **digit amputation** or **arthrodesis** of the distal interphalangeal joint.

Drug use in food-producing animals is subject to national regulations and to **withdrawal periods** for milk and meat.

### Prevention

- regular functional trimming
- early detection by locomotion scoring and prompt treatment
- cow **comfort**: soft beds, enough lying time and less standing time in the holding pen
- floors that are neither abrasive nor slippery
- body condition management around calving

<summary>Key studies</summary>

- **Solano et al. (2016).** Cross-sectional study of 28,607 cows on 156 farms in Alberta (Canada), with lesions recorded by hoof trimmers. On farms where the whole herd was trimmed, sole ulcer was found in 6% of cows and white line disease in 4%, in 92% and 93% of herds. Cows in parity 4 had 5 and 7 times higher odds of these lesions than first-parity cows, and housing in free stalls at least doubled the odds compared with deep-bedded packs. It is observational and trimmers differed in their recording.
- **Bicalho et al. (2009).** Cross-sectional study of 501 Holstein cows. Sole ulcer prevalence was 4.2% in first-parity and 27.8% in older cows, and white line disease prevalence 1.0% and 6.5%. Both were associated with a thinner **digital cushion**, measured by ultrasonography: cows in the thickest quartile had a lameness prevalence 15 percentage points lower than those in the thinnest. Cushion thickness was associated with body condition and fell from calving to a nadir at 120 days. A cross-sectional design cannot prove causation.
- **Newsome et al. (2017).** Prospective cohort of 179 cows (158 completed the study) followed from before calving. Thin sole soft tissue and thin or decreasing back fat increased the likelihood of lesions, but thinning of the soft tissue between assessments did not predict new lesions. This **challenges the widespread idea** that loss of body fat thins the digital cushion and causes the lesions.
- **Thomas et al. (2015).** Randomised controlled trial on 5 farms in newly lame cows with claw horn lesions, all given a therapeutic trim. At 35 days, 11 of 45 (24.4%) were sound with trimming alone, 14 of 39 (35.9%) with a block, 12 of 42 (28.6%) with ketoprofen and 23 of 41 (56.1%) with a **block and ketoprofen**; only this last combination was better than trimming alone. The cows were newly and mildly lame.
- **Wilson et al. (2022).** 34-month randomised controlled trial with 132 heifers per group in a single herd (438 analysed). Those given ketoprofen for 3 days after each calving, plus trimming, a block and ketoprofen whenever lame, were less likely to become lame (OR 0.66) and severely lame (OR 0.28) than those treated with trimming and a block alone. A single herd limits generalisation.
- **Sadiq et al. (2024).** Systematic review of 10 studies on the treatment of claw horn lesions. Trimming with a block, with or without an anti-inflammatory, was associated with more complete and faster resolution than trimming alone, but the effect on locomotion and pain was unclear and the studies were highly heterogeneous.
