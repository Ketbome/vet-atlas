## What it is

**Colic** is not a diagnosis but the name for **abdominal pain** in the horse. It is the most common medical emergency in the species.

The horse's digestive anatomy explains much of it:

- a small stomach that can hardly vomit
- a long small intestine on a very mobile mesentery
- a huge large intestine, the caecum and large colon, only partly fixed to the abdominal wall and changing calibre at several points

Causes are grouped by the damage to the intestinal wall.

### Simple obstructions

In non-strangulating obstructions transit stops but blood supply is preserved. They are:

- **impactions**, of the pelvic flexure of the large colon, the caecum, the small colon or the ileum
- **large colon displacements**: right dorsal displacement and nephrosplenic entrapment or left dorsal displacement
- spasmodic or gas colic

### Strangulating lesions

Transit and blood supply are obstructed together, and the wall soon becomes necrotic. The most typical are:

- **large colon volvulus**
- the **pedunculated lipoma**, hanging from the mesentery, whose pedicle wraps around and strangulates a loop of small intestine
- **epiploic foramen entrapment**, in which a loop of small intestine passes through the epiploic foramen and becomes trapped
- small intestinal volvulus
- inguinal hernias in stallions

### Inflammatory causes

Enteritis and other inflammatory causes, such as proximal enteritis or colitis.

The key decision is to recognise early the colic that resolves with medical treatment and the one that needs surgery.

## Who it affects

Horses of any age and breed.

The best-documented risk factors are **management changes**: in feeding, in the type or amount of forage, in housing and in exercise. More hours stabled, lack of deworming and a previous colic also count.

Some risks are specific to each lesion:

- **pedunculated lipoma**, in older horses, most often over 10 years of age
- **epiploic foramen entrapment**, very strongly associated with **crib-biting or windsucking**
- **large colon volvulus**, in broodmares, especially in the months after foaling, and in taller horses
- **pelvic flexure impaction**, with forage that is too coarse, insufficient water intake and dental problems

## Anatomy involved

### Small intestine

The **duodenum** is fixed and short. The **jejunum**, by contrast, hangs from a long mesentery that makes it very mobile. This is why it easily wraps around the pedicle of a lipoma or slips into the epiploic foramen.

The **epiploic foramen** is the opening of the omental bursa, between the caudate lobe of the liver, the caudal vena cava and the portal vein.

The **ileum** opens into the caecum through the ileal orifice. It can become impacted or intussuscept.

### Caecum

A large blind-ended sac. Its **base** lies in the right flank, under the paralumbar fossa, and its **apex** on the abdominal floor, towards the xiphoid cartilage. It communicates with the large colon through the caecocolic orifice.

Its impactions can end in **rupture** with little preceding pain, so they are monitored closely.

### Large colon

The large colon (*colon ascendens*) forms a double horseshoe with four parts: right ventral, left ventral, left dorsal and right dorsal colon. The sternal, **pelvic** and diaphragmatic flexures join them.

Only the right dorsal colon and the base of the caecum are fixed to the dorsal wall. The left parts are free, which allows displacements and volvulus, which usually twists near the base of the caecum.

At the **pelvic flexure** the lumen narrows abruptly. It is the usual site of impactions and is palpable per rectum.

### Spleen

It lies on the left side of the abdomen. Its dorsal part is joined to the left kidney by the **nephrosplenic ligament**.

In nephrosplenic entrapment, the left colon becomes displaced over that ligament, into the space between the spleen and the left kidney, and hangs there. It is recognised by rectal palpation or ultrasound of the left flank.

## Clinical signs

Pain shows as looking at the flank, pawing, kicking at the abdomen, lying down and getting up, rolling or stretching postures.

The intensity of pain and its response to analgesics are among the most useful findings. **Severe pain that does not respond to analgesia** points to a surgical lesion.

Heart rate rises with pain and hypovolaemia. **Persistent tachycardia** is a warning sign.

These indicate shock and endotoxaemia:

- congested or muddy mucous membranes
- prolonged capillary refill time
- weak pulse

There may also be:

- reduced or absent gut sounds
- abdominal distension
- no faeces
- **gastric reflux** through the nasogastric tube, in small intestinal obstructions

## Diagnosis

- The general examination is repeated: heart rate, mucous membrane colour, capillary refill time, auscultation of the four quadrants and response to analgesia.
- **Nasogastric intubation** is both diagnostic and therapeutic. The horse can hardly vomit because the cardia acts as a one-way valve. Fluid accumulated in the stomach must be drained through the tube to prevent gastric rupture.
- **Rectal palpation** can detect an impacted pelvic flexure, distended small intestinal loops, tight bands of the colon or caecum, or the colon in the nephrosplenic space.
- Abdominal ultrasound shows distended, amotile small intestinal loops, thickening of the colon wall, colon in the nephrosplenic space or free fluid.
- On abdominocentesis, fluid appearance, cells, protein and **peritoneal lactate** help recognise intestinal ischaemia.
- Packed cell volume and blood lactate indicate severity.

Many lesions are confirmed at **exploratory laparotomy**.

## Treatment and prevention

### Medical treatment

- analgesia with non-steroidal anti-inflammatories, α2 agonists or butorphanol
- gastric decompression
- **fluid therapy**, oral by tube or intravenous, to rehydrate the contents of impactions
- laxatives
- withholding feed
- frequent reassessment

Nephrosplenic entrapment can resolve without surgery, with exercise and phenylephrine or by rolling the horse under anaesthesia. A meta-analysis of 19 retrospective studies found no difference in resolution between medical and surgical treatment, or with phenylephrine, although there are no randomised trials.

### Surgical treatment

A ventral midline laparotomy is performed with the horse in dorsal recumbency. The displacement or volvulus is corrected, the colon is emptied through an enterotomy at the pelvic flexure and non-viable intestine is **resected** with an anastomosis.

Strangulating lesions carry a worse prognosis than simple obstructions. Small intestinal lesions carry a worse one than large colon lesions.

Recurrent colic and **ileus** are among the most frequent postoperative complications.

The decision to operate and its cost should be discussed early with the owner, because delay worsens the prognosis of strangulating lesions.

### Prevention

- gradual changes in feeding and management
- good-quality forage available for many hours
- water always available
- regular exercise or turnout
- parasite control
- dental checks

### Medicines and the food chain

The horse is a food-producing species in many countries. In the EU, its identification document (passport) states whether it is excluded from the food chain. Only then can it receive medicines not authorised for food-producing animals, and some substances considered essential for equids can be used with a six-month withdrawal period.

Otherwise national regulations and their **withdrawal periods** apply.

<summary>Key studies</summary>

- **Curtis et al. (2015).** Prospective study of 1016 first examinations of horses with colic by 167 practitioners in the United Kingdom. Of the 822 classifiable cases, 23.6% were critical: they needed intensive treatment or surgery, or died. In the final model, pain score (OR 1.19 per point), heart rate (OR 1.06 per beat/min), capillary refill time over 2.5 s (OR 3.21), weak pulse (OR 2.90) and absent gut sounds in one or more quadrants (OR 3.65) were associated with a critical case. Cases were classified as critical retrospectively, by outcome.
- **Curtis et al. (2019).** Scoping review of 58 studies on risk factors for colic and systematic review of management changes. Only 14 studies (1 cohort, 8 case-control and 5 cross-sectional, all from 1990–2008) met the criteria. Changes in feed (5 of 14) and housing (3 of 14) were the factors with most evidence. Eight of the 14 did not control confounding well and methods were very heterogeneous.
- **Archer et al. (2008).** Matched case-control study in hospitals in the UK, Ireland and the USA with 109 cases of epiploic foramen entrapment and 310 controls. Crib-biting or windsucking was associated with a much higher risk (OR 67.3; 95% CI 15.3–296.5), as were colic in the previous 12 months (OR 4.4) and greater height (OR 1.05 per cm). The very wide confidence interval reflects few exposed cases, and the data come from telephone questionnaires.
- **Suthers et al. (2013), risk factors.** Prospective case-control study in 4 UK hospitals with 69 horses with large colon volvulus and 204 controls. Risk increased with greater height, several colic episodes in the last year, being a mare (more so if she had foaled), more hours stabled in the previous 2 weeks, being fed hay or sugar beet, and recent changes of pasture or of the amount of forage. Data were obtained by telephone and may carry recall bias.
- **Suthers et al. (2013), survival.** Cohort of 116 horses operated on for strangulating large colon volvulus (360° or more). 76.7% survived anaesthesia; of these, 70.7% reached discharge, 48.3% one year and 33.7% two years. Higher preoperative packed cell volume (HR 1.08 per point) and abnormal serosal colour at surgery (HR 3.61) were associated with shorter survival.
- **Proudman et al. (2002).** Cohort of 341 horses that recovered from colic surgery, followed long term. The probability of survival was 0.87 at 10 days, 0.82 at 100 days and 0.75 at 600 days, with the highest mortality in the first 10 days. Epiploic foramen entrapment had worse survival (RR 2.1), and 29% of horses had at least one episode of postoperative colic. Techniques and care have changed since.
