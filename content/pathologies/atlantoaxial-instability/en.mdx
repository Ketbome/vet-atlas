## What it is

Atlantoaxial instability, also called atlantoaxial subluxation, is excessive flexion between the first two cervical vertebrae.

When the joint is unstable, the cranial part of the axis rotates dorsally into the vertebral canal of the atlas and compresses the spinal cord.

In most dogs it is **congenital**. The dens of the axis is hypoplastic, absent or not fused, or the ligaments that hold it are missing. A minor trauma, such as a jump or rough play, often triggers the first signs.

In dogs of any breed it can also follow trauma that fractures the dens or tears the ligaments.

## Who it affects

Young dogs of **toy and small breeds**, usually showing signs before 1 year of age: Yorkshire terrier, Chihuahua, toy poodle, Pomeranian and Pekingese.

## Anatomy involved

The **atlas** (C1) has no conventional vertebral body. Its dorsal arch lies over the cord.

The **axis** (C2) carries a prominent spinous process and, cranially, the **dens** (*dens axis*), which projects into the ventral part of the vertebral foramen of the atlas.

The **transverse ligament of the atlas** holds the dens against the floor of the atlas, and the apical and alar ligaments anchor it further. A dorsal atlantoaxial ligament also joins the two vertebrae.

Without a normal dens or these ligaments, flexion of the head lets the axis tilt up. The dens or the body of the axis then presses on the cord.

## Clinical signs

Neck pain and neck stiffness, with signs of a cranial cervical (C1–C5) lesion. There is ataxia and paresis of all four limbs, from mild to tetraplegia.

Signs may be acute, or progressive and episodic.

Severe compression can cause respiratory paralysis and **sudden death**.

## Diagnosis

- A **lateral radiograph** shows an increased space between the dorsal arch of the atlas and the spinous process of the axis. The ventrodorsal or open-mouth view may show an abnormal dens.
- **Flexed views** are dangerous and should be avoided or taken with great care.
- CT shows the dens and the bony anatomy. MRI shows the cord compression and concurrent malformations of the craniocervical junction.

The neck is handled carefully during examination and anaesthesia.

## Treatment

### Conservative

A rigid neck splint for several weeks, with strict rest and analgesia. It is mainly used for mild signs, especially in skeletally immature dogs, but relapses occur.

### Surgical

**Surgical stabilisation** is preferred in most cases. It can be ventral fixation, with transarticular screws or pins, often with bone cement, plus arthrodesis of the atlantoaxial joints. Dorsal techniques are also used.

The surgery is technically demanding in very small dogs and carries a real perioperative risk. Results are best in young dogs with a short history and mild deficits.
