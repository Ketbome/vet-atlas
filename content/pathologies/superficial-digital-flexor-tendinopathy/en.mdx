## What it is

Superficial digital flexor tendinopathy (**tendonitis**, "bowed tendon") is a partial rupture of the fibres of the **superficial digital flexor tendon**.

This tendon works as a **spring**: it stores and returns elastic energy. At the gallop it undergoes strains at which microdamage has been recorded.

With age and exercise it accumulates **microdamage** and degenerative changes in the matrix, without clinical signs. Clinical injury appears when a load exceeds the strength of an already weakened tendon. It is therefore considered an overstrain injury on top of prior degeneration rather than an isolated trauma.

The typical lesion is a **core lesion** in the mid-metacarpal region.

The repaired tissue is a scar, with less organised collagen, and is weaker than normal tendon. **Reinjuries** are frequent, in the same tendon or the contralateral one.

## Who it affects

- Mainly **racehorses**, flat and jump.
- Also sport horses, such as **event horses** and showjumpers.

Risk increases with **age** and years of training, and is higher in entire males.

The **thoracic limbs** are almost always affected. In the pelvic limb the injury is rare.

## Anatomy involved

### Superficial digital flexor muscle of the thoracic limb

Its muscle belly lies on the caudal aspect of the forearm. Past the carpus it continues as a thick tendon that runs down the palmar aspect of the metacarpus, the most superficial one in the region. At the fetlock it divides into branches that attach on each side of the pastern (**first and second phalanges**).

The **accessory ligament** (superior check ligament) joins it to the distal radius. It is part of the **stay apparatus**, which helps the horse bear weight standing with very little muscular effort.

The lesion lies mainly in the middle third of the metacarpus, although it can extend into the fetlock and pastern.

### Superficial digital flexor muscle of the pelvic limb

In the horse it is almost entirely tendinous. It attaches partly to the point of the hock (**calcaneal tuberosity**) and runs down the metatarsus to insert as in the thoracic limb. Its injuries are much less common than those of the thoracic limb.

### Metacarpus

The tendon lies on the palmar aspect of the **third metacarpal bone**, superficial to the deep digital flexor tendon, its accessory ligament and the suspensory ligament, which arises from the proximal palmar aspect of the bone.

Ultrasonography is described by zones along the metacarpus.

## Clinical signs

- **Heat, pain on palpation and swelling** of the palmar aspect of the metacarpus.
- In severe injuries, a curved ("bowed") profile seen from the side.
- **Lameness**, of a degree that depends on the severity of the injury.

With time, some horses become less lame at the walk and trot while the tendon is still thickened. The lameness can recur under hard work.

## Diagnosis

- Careful **palpation** of the tendon, comparing both limbs.
- **Ultrasonography**, which confirms the lesion and measures its extent: cross-sectional area, percentage of the tendon affected, echogenicity and fibre alignment.

Ultrasonography is repeated at intervals to guide the return to work. The extent of the lesion helps predict whether a racehorse will race again.

The differential diagnosis includes:

- lesions of the suspensory ligament
- lesions of the deep digital flexor tendon and its accessory ligament
- digital sheath tenosynovitis

## Treatment and prevention

### Acute phase

Cold, compression bandaging, box rest and **non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs** for the first days.

### Controlled exercise

This is the basis of treatment. It consists of walking in hand and a very gradual return to work over many months, guided by ultrasonographic checks.

### Intralesional treatments

- Bone marrow-derived **mesenchymal stem cells**.
- **Platelet-rich plasma**.
- Other biological products.

There are few controlled trials and the quality of the evidence is low (see "Key studies").

### Surgery

Desmotomy of the accessory ligament (superior check ligament) of the superficial digital flexor tendon has been used as an adjunct to reduce recurrence. Tendon splitting of core lesions is no longer common practice.

**Intratendinous corticosteroids** are contraindicated because they inhibit repair.

### Prevention

- Progressive training.
- Avoiding **firm ground**.
- Monitoring the tendons of horses in training by palpation and ultrasonography.
- Withdrawing from intense work those that show changes.

### Regulations

The horse is a food-producing animal in many countries, and drug use is subject to **national regulations** and **withdrawal periods**. In the EU, the equine identification document (passport) states whether the horse is excluded from slaughter for human consumption, and this decides which medicines may be given.

<summary>Key studies</summary>

- **Kasashima et al. (2004).** Retrospective study of 10,262 flat-racing Thoroughbreds registered with the Japan Racing Association in 1999. Forelimb superficial digital flexor tendonitis affected **11.1%** (1,130 cases) and suspensory desmitis 3.61%; in the hindlimb there were only 6 and 14 cases. The risk of tendonitis was higher from 3 years of age and in entire males. It is based on the veterinary records of a single organisation.
- **Reardon et al. (2012).** Retrospective cohort of hurdle race starts in the UK between 2001 and 2009, with 1,031 starts with injury and 168,637 without. **Firmer going**, older age at first race, a **previous tendon injury** and racing in summer were associated with higher risk. Racing more often in the previous 9–12 months and a more successful trainer were associated with lower risk. It only includes injuries during races, not in training.
- **Dyson (2004).** Comparative study of 219 horses with superficial digital flexor tendonitis followed for at least 2 years after returning to work. The **reinjury** rate was 42.5–44.4% with controlled exercise alone and with hyaluronan or polysulphated glycosaminoglycans. With beta-aminopropionitrile fumarate it was 16% in the treated limb, but the overall injury rate, including the contralateral limb, did not change. Reinjury risk increased from showjumpers to event horses to racehorses. Groups were not randomised.
- **Godwin et al. (2012).** Cohort of 141 racehorses treated with bone marrow-derived **mesenchymal stem cells** and followed for at least 2 years. There were no adverse effects, and reinjury was 27.4% in the 113 with follow-up, lower than published for jump racehorses with other treatments. The comparison was with **historical controls** from other studies, which limits the conclusion.
- **Geburek et al. (2016).** Randomised, placebo-controlled clinical trial in 20 horses: a single intralesional injection of **platelet-rich plasma** (n = 10) or saline (n = 10). Lameness decreased significantly sooner with plasma (week 8 versus 12) and the repair tissue was better organised, with no difference in tendon cross-sectional area. At 12 months, 80% of treated horses versus 50% of controls had regained their level of performance, and at 24 months, 60% versus 50%. It is a small trial.
- **M'Cloud et al. (2024).** Systematic review and meta-analysis of 21 studies with a comparison group on stem cells and platelet-rich plasma for tendon and ligament injuries (17 in the meta-analysis). No biological treatment increased the likelihood of returning to performance, but stem cells, alone or with platelet-rich plasma, were associated with **fewer reinjuries**. Most studies were of low quality, heterogeneous and at high risk of bias.
