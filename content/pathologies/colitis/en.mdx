## What it is

Colitis is inflammation of the colonic mucosa. It reduces the colon's capacity to absorb water and store faeces, and increases mucus secretion.

It is a clinical and histological description, not a single disease.

### Acute colitis

Its causes are:

- dietary indiscretion or a sudden diet change
- stress
- parasites, such as *Trichuris vulpis* or *Giardia*
- bacterial causes; *Clostridium perfringens* enterotoxin is suspected in some cases
- drugs

It is usually **self-limiting**.

### Chronic colitis

It lasts more than about three weeks. Its causes are:

- food-responsive enteropathy
- antibiotic-responsive enteropathy
- immunosuppressant-responsive enteropathy (inflammatory bowel disease, usually lymphoplasmacytic)
- fibre-responsive large-bowel diarrhoea
- chronic parasitism
- **granulomatous colitis** (histiocytic ulcerative colitis) in boxers and French bulldogs, associated with invasive *Escherichia coli*

Neoplasia, such as adenocarcinoma or lymphoma, and colorectal polyps must be ruled out.

## Who it affects

Dogs of any age. Most dogs with chronic colitis are middle-aged.

Acute large-bowel diarrhoea is common after dietary changes and in dogs housed in shelters.

Granulomatous colitis affects young boxers and French bulldogs.

## Anatomy involved

The large intestine comprises the caecum, colon and rectum. The colon absorbs water and electrolytes and stores faeces.

The inflamed mucosa loses absorptive capacity, produces more mucus and becomes irritable. This is why the rectum signals urgency even when it holds little faeces.

In granulomatous colitis the mucosa is thickened, irregular and ulcerated. The lamina propria holds macrophages laden with PAS-positive material.

## Clinical signs

**Large-bowel diarrhoea** is characterised by:

- increased frequency, with small volumes each time
- urgency
- **mucus**
- fresh blood (**haematochezia**)
- **tenesmus**

Weight loss is uncommon; it is more typical of small-bowel disease. Some dogs with colitis also vomit.

Chronic cases may be intermittent.

## Diagnosis

- Faecal examination, with flotation and testing for *Giardia*, and empirical deworming, for example with fenbendazole.
- Rectal examination detects mucosal irregularity, polyps or masses.
- In chronic cases, blood work and abdominal imaging to exclude systemic disease and neoplasia. Ultrasound assesses wall thickness and lymph nodes.
- Before more invasive tests, **treatment trials** with diet are run: hydrolysed or novel protein, or added fibre.
- **Colonoscopy with biopsies** is kept for chronic cases that do not respond. Histopathology characterises the inflammation and excludes neoplasia. Culture and fluorescence in situ hybridisation are used for granulomatous colitis.

## Treatment

### Acute colitis

A short period of a highly digestible diet, soluble fibre such as psyllium, and treatment of parasites. Antibiotics are **generally not needed**.

### Chronic colitis

It is treated stepwise:

1. **dietary trial**, with hydrolysed or novel protein or a high-fibre diet
2. then probiotics
3. immunosuppressants (prednisolone, budesonide, ciclosporin) when diet fails and biopsies support inflammatory bowel disease

### Granulomatous colitis

It responds to antibiotics chosen by susceptibility testing, classically **enrofloxacin**.

The prognosis is good for acute and diet-responsive forms.

<summary>In the cat</summary>

Colitis is also common in the cat. Parasites such as *Giardia* are ruled out first.

### *Tritrichomonas blagburni*

A cause specific to the cat. This flagellated protozoan was formerly called *T. foetus*.

It causes **chronic large-bowel diarrhoea**, malodorous, sometimes with fresh blood and mucus, and even faecal incontinence. It affects **young cats** from catteries and shelters, often pedigree cats.

It is diagnosed by a direct smear of fresh faeces, specific culture or, more sensitively, **PCR**.

The treatment is **ronidazole**, which can cause neurotoxic effects and must be handled carefully. In many cats the diarrhoea resolves on its own within months, even though the infection persists.

### Chronic enteropathy

Feline chronic enteropathy often affects the small and large intestine together. Many cats respond to a **hydrolysed or novel protein diet**, and soluble fibre helps in large-bowel diarrhoea.

In older cats intestinal neoplasia must be ruled out: lymphoma, adenocarcinoma and mast cell tumour.

<summary>Key studies in the cat</summary>

### *Tritrichomonas*

- **Walden et al. (2013).** Described the feline parasite as a new species, *Tritrichomonas blagburni*. They based this on cross-infection experiments between cats and cattle, its different pathogenicity in each host and genetic differences from the bovine *T. foetus*.
- **Gookin et al. (2006).** In vitro testing plus treatment of 1 naturally and 10 experimentally infected cats. After ronidazole at 10 mg/kg, infection relapsed in all 5 experimental cats. At 30–50 mg/kg every 12 hours for 14 days, all 10 cats stayed PCR-negative for 21–30 weeks. Experimental infection in few cats.
- **Foster et al. (2004).** Prospective follow-up of 26 cats with diarrhoea caused by *T. foetus*. Diarrhoea resolved in 23 cats a median of 9 months after onset, but 12 of the 22 cats retested were still infected a median of 39 months after the diarrhoea ended.
- **Xenoulis et al. (2013).** Retrospective study of 104 PCR-positive pet cats. 98% had diarrhoea, with a median duration of 135 days. Of the 45 cats that completed ronidazole treatment, 64% responded well and the rest improved only partly, did not improve or relapsed soon after.

### Diet in chronic enteropathy

- **Guilford et al. (2001).** 55 cats with chronic idiopathic gastrointestinal signs fed a selected-protein elimination diet. 29% were confirmed as food-sensitive on dietary challenge, and another 20% improved without relapsing on challenge. In the food-sensitive cats, large-bowel diarrhoea was more common than small-bowel diarrhoea.
- **Kathrani et al. (2020).** Primary-care records of 977 cats given a hydrolysed diet for chronic vomiting or diarrhoea. Response was poor in 34% of cats started on the diet alone and in 61% of those also given antibiotics or corticosteroids. Cats over 6 years old were more likely to respond poorly. Observational data without a confirmed diagnosis.
