title: Colitis
summary: Inflammation of the large intestine. It causes large-bowel diarrhoea, with frequent small stools, mucus, fresh blood and tenesmus. It may be acute and self-limiting or chronic, with many possible causes.
---

## What it is

Colitis is inflammation of the colonic mucosa, which reduces the colon's capacity to absorb water and store faeces and increases mucus secretion. It is a clinical and histological description, not a single disease:

- **Acute colitis**: dietary indiscretion or sudden diet change, stress, parasites (*Trichuris vulpis*, *Giardia*), bacterial causes (*Clostridium perfringens* enterotoxin is suspected in some cases) and drugs. It is usually self-limiting.
- **Chronic colitis** (more than about three weeks): food-responsive enteropathy, antibiotic-responsive enteropathy, immunosuppressant-responsive enteropathy (inflammatory bowel disease, usually lymphoplasmacytic), fibre-responsive large-bowel diarrhoea, chronic parasitism and, in boxers and French bulldogs, **granulomatous (histiocytic ulcerative) colitis** associated with invasive *Escherichia coli*. Neoplasia (adenocarcinoma, lymphoma) and colorectal polyps must be ruled out.

## Who it affects

Dogs of any age. Acute colitis is very common in young dogs and after dietary changes or kennel stays. Granulomatous colitis affects young boxers and French bulldogs.

## Anatomy involved

- **Large intestine**: caecum, colon and rectum. The colon absorbs water and electrolytes and stores faeces; its inflamed mucosa loses absorptive capacity, produces more mucus and becomes irritable, so the rectum signals urgency even when it holds little faeces.
- In granulomatous colitis the mucosa is thickened, irregular and ulcerated, with macrophages laden with PAS-positive material in the lamina propria.

## Clinical signs

**Large-bowel diarrhoea**: increased frequency with small volumes each time, urgency, **mucus**, **fresh blood (haematochezia)** and **tenesmus**. Weight loss is uncommon (it is more typical of small-bowel disease), although some dogs with colitis also vomit. Chronic cases may be intermittent.

## Diagnosis

- **Faecal examination** (flotation, *Giardia* antigen) and empirical deworming, for example with fenbendazole.
- **Rectal examination**: mucosal irregularity, masses, pain, and assessment of the prostate and anal sacs.
- **Blood work and abdominal imaging** in chronic cases to exclude systemic disease and neoplasia; ultrasound assesses wall thickness and lymph nodes.
- **Treatment trials**: diet (hydrolysed or novel protein, or added fibre) before more invasive tests.
- **Colonoscopy with biopsies**: in chronic cases that do not respond; histopathology characterises the inflammation and excludes neoplasia; culture and fluorescence in situ hybridisation for granulomatous colitis.

## Treatment

Acute colitis: a short period of a highly digestible diet, soluble fibre (such as psyllium) and treatment of parasites; antibiotics are generally not needed. Chronic colitis is treated stepwise: **dietary trial** (hydrolysed or novel protein, high-fibre diet), then probiotics, and immunosuppressants (prednisolone, budesonide, ciclosporin) when diet fails and biopsies support inflammatory bowel disease. Granulomatous colitis responds to antibiotics chosen by susceptibility testing, classically enrofloxacin. The prognosis is good for acute and diet-responsive forms.
