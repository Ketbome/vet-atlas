## What it is

Bovine respiratory disease (BRD) is not a single disease but a **multifactorial syndrome**. Host, environmental and management factors combine with several infectious agents to produce **bronchopneumonia**.

Respiratory viruses and the stress of transport, weaning or commingling damage the respiratory epithelium and mucociliary clearance. The viruses involved are:

- **bovine respiratory syncytial virus**
- **bovine herpesvirus 1**
- **parainfluenza 3**
- **bovine viral diarrhoea virus**
- bovine coronavirus

Bacteria that normally live in the nasopharynx then descend to the lung:

- *Mannheimia haemolytica*, the most important
- *Pasteurella multocida*
- *Histophilus somni*
- *Mycoplasmopsis* (formerly *Mycoplasma*) *bovis*

*M. haemolytica* produces a **leukotoxin** that contributes to tissue damage. It causes severe fibrinonecrotic bronchopneumonia with fibrinous pleuritis, known as **fibrinous pleuropneumonia**.

The classic term "shipping fever" refers to the onset of disease after transport and arrival at a feedlot.

## Who it affects

- **Rearing calves**, especially of dairy breeds, before and around weaning. This is so-called enzootic calf pneumonia.
- **Beef calves newly arrived at feedlots**, with the highest risk in the first weeks after arrival.

Risk factors include:

- **transport** and passage through auction markets
- **commingling of cattle from different sources**
- abrupt weaning, castration and dehorning
- poor **transfer of passive immunity**
- poorly ventilated housing
- sudden temperature changes

## Anatomy involved

### Lungs

Lesions concentrate in the **cranioventral** parts, where exudate gravitates: the cranial lobes, the middle lobe and the ventral part of the caudal lobes.

The marked **lobulation** of the bovine lung, with thick interlobular septa, makes the lobule the main unit of injury. In fibrinous pneumonia the septa are thickened by fibrin and oedema.

Cattle also have relatively small lungs with a low tidal volume, which reduces their respiratory reserve.

### Trachea

The **tracheal bronchus** arises from the right wall of the trachea before its bifurcation and ventilates the right cranial lobe. That lobe must therefore be included when auscultating cattle with pneumonia.

Bovine herpesvirus 1 causes necrotic tracheitis that damages the mucociliary apparatus.

The trachea is also the route for sampling the lower airways by tracheal wash.

## Clinical signs

**Fever**, depression, reduced feed intake, nasal and ocular discharge, **cough** and increased respiratory rate.

Severe cases show **dyspnoea**, with open-mouth breathing and an expiratory grunt.

Auscultation reveals abnormal sounds over the cranioventral thorax.

*M. bovis* can also cause otitis media and arthritis in calves.

Many animals with lung lesions never show obvious signs, and some are only detected at slaughter.

## Diagnosis

- In dairy calves, standardised **clinical respiratory scores** are used, such as the Wisconsin score. In feedlots, pen riders detect sick animals visually.
- **Lung ultrasonography** shows lung consolidation on the farm, helps assess severity and can be used to screen groups of calves.
- To identify the agent, culture with susceptibility testing and PCR are run on bronchoalveolar lavage, tracheal wash or deep nasopharyngeal swab samples. They are taken before treatment from several animals in the group.
- **Necropsy** of dead animals identifies the agents and lesions in the group.

## Treatment and prevention

Treatment relies on early **antimicrobials**. The choice depends on local susceptibility, previous response on the farm, suspicion of *M. bovis* and the authorisation of each product.

The animal must be **reassessed** after treatment.

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs can be used case by case to reduce fever and discomfort.

In high-risk groups arriving at the feedlot, **metaphylaxis** is used, treating the whole group.

Drug use in food animals is subject to national regulations and to meat and milk withdrawal periods.

Prevention depends above all on management:

- adequate **colostrum feeding**
- good **ventilation**
- avoiding commingling of cattle from different sources
- reducing the stress of transport and weaning
- vaccination and early weaning before shipment, known as preconditioning

Vaccines against the viruses and bacteria of the complex are widely used, but the evidence that vaccination on arrival at the feedlot reduces disease is scarce.

<summary>Key studies</summary>

- **ENOVAT guideline (Scahill et al., 2026).** European guideline developed with the GRADE method from systematic reviews of randomised trials. No antimicrobial class showed a clinically meaningful advantage in the need for re-treatment. It suggests penicillin G, oxytetracycline or florfenicol as first choice, keeps macrolides as second line and recommends against fluoroquinolones except in exceptional, justified circumstances. It recommends against adding a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug solely to avoid re-treatment. Some panel members declared past links with industry.
- **Scahill et al. (2026).** Systematic review and meta-analysis of 17 randomised trials with 4909 animals. Adding a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug to the antimicrobial did not reduce the need for re-treatment (RR 0.94; 95% CI 0.84–1.05; moderate certainty). The comparison of the anti-inflammatory alone against the antimicrobial rested on two trials and its certainty is very low. The outcome measured does not capture effects on pain or fever.
- **Timsit et al. (2016).** Systematic review and Bayesian meta-analysis of 7 feedlot studies comparing disease detected in the pens with lung lesions at slaughter. Clinical detection had a sensitivity of only 0.27 and a specificity of 0.92: most animals with lesions go undetected. Heterogeneity between studies was large.
- **Buczinski et al. (2026).** Systematic review and meta-analysis of lung ultrasonography in calves (26 studies), corrected for the imperfect reference test. With a consolidation threshold of 1 cm or more, sensitivity was 68.4% and specificity 91.2%; with 3 cm or more, 58.2% and 95.8%. The included studies were few and heterogeneous.
- **Buczinski et al. (2021).** Systematic review and meta-analysis of 27 articles in dairy heifers. Heifers with calfhood BRD had 2.85 times higher odds of dying and 2.30 times higher odds of leaving the herd before first calving, gained 0.067 kg/day less and produced 121.2 kg less milk in their first lactation. Two of the authors worked for a pharmaceutical company.
- **O'Connor et al. (2019).** Systematic review and network meta-analysis of bacterial and viral vaccines given on arrival at the feedlot (53 studies; 14 in the analysed network). It found little compelling evidence that these vaccines reduce the incidence of BRD, contrary to a very widespread practice.
