## What it is

Equine recurrent uveitis (ERU), also called "moon blindness" or periodic ophthalmia, is an **immune-mediated** inflammation of the uvea. It runs with repeated episodes or with persistent low-grade inflammation.

Each episode leaves damage. Over time, cataracts, synechiae, glaucoma, retinal detachment and blindness develop.

### Origin

Two main factors are involved in its origin.

Many eyes with ERU have a **persistent intraocular infection** with *Leptospira*. Antibodies produced inside the eye, bacterial DNA and, in some cases, live leptospires are found in the vitreous. Uveitis can appear months or years after a systemic infection that went unnoticed.

In the **Appaloosa** and other breeds with the *leopard complex* spotting pattern, ERU has a hereditary basis and usually takes the insidious form.

### Clinical forms

- **Classic**, with acute episodes of active inflammation separated by quiet periods.
- **Insidious**, with continuous low-grade inflammation, without obvious pain, which goes unnoticed until the damage is severe. It is typical of the Appaloosa.
- **Posterior**, which mainly affects the vitreous, choroid and retina. It is less common and, in horses without the leopard pattern, strongly associated with intraocular leptospiral infection.

## Who it affects

Horses of any breed, usually adults. Overrepresented are the Appaloosa and other breeds carrying the *LP* gene, such as the Knabstrupper, and, in Europe, warmbloods.

Risk rises with exposure to leptospires: stagnant water, wet pastures and rodents.

## Anatomy involved

In the horse's eyeball (*bulbus oculi*), the iris has pigmented thickenings on its edge, the **corpora nigra** (*granula iridica*).

The anterior uvea, made up of the iris and ciliary body, produces aqueous humour. The posterior uvea, the choroid, nourishes the outer retina. The fibrous *tapetum lucidum* lies in the dorsal fundus.

In ERU, protein, fibrin and inflammatory cells pass into the aqueous humour and vitreous. Repeated inflammation produces:

- synechiae
- atrophy of the corpora nigra
- cataracts and lens luxation
- vitreous opacities
- retinal detachment
- glaucoma
- finally, small shrunken eyes (*phthisis bulbi*)

## Clinical signs

### Acute episode

Blepharospasm and photophobia, corneal oedema, **miosis**, aqueous flare, fibrin in the anterior chamber and low intraocular pressure. One or both eyes may be affected.

### Chronic forms

Dark, dull iris, atrophied corpora nigra, synechiae, an irregular pupil or one that does not dilate, cataracts, cloudy vitreous, progressive loss of vision and sometimes glaucoma.

In the insidious form the horse may show no pain, and the owner notices the problem when there are already cataracts or blindness.

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis is clinical. It rests on a history of repeated episodes and a full ophthalmic examination with slit lamp and ophthalmoscope.

A **corneal ulcer** must be ruled out with fluorescein before corticosteroids are used.

When opacities prevent examination of the fundus, ocular ultrasound shows vitreous opacities and retinal detachment.

### Leptospires

Serum serology, with the microscopic agglutination test, is of little value on its own.

Most reliable is testing **aqueous humour or vitreous**. Antibodies are sought, compared with those in blood through the Goldmann-Witmer coefficient, along with DNA by PCR. The result guides the choice of surgical treatment.

## Treatment and prevention

### Acute episode

- topical corticosteroids, if there is no corneal ulcer
- topical **atropine**, to dilate the pupil, relieve pain and prevent synechiae
- systemic non-steroidal anti-inflammatories

### Long-term control

- Pars plana **vitrectomy** removes the vitreous with its antigens and leptospires. It is the preferred option in eyes with proven intraocular leptospiral infection, with few recurrences, although with a risk of cataract and retinal detachment.
- Suprachoroidal cyclosporine implants release the immunosuppressant inside the eye for about 2 years or more. They are used mainly in non-infectious uveitis and are less effective than vitrectomy in leptospiral ERU.
- Intravitreal low-dose gentamicin injection is a more recent alternative, with few data.
- Blind, painful eyes are enucleated.

### Prevention

Reduce exposure to leptospires: rodent control, dry pastures and keeping horses from stagnant water.

<summary>Key studies</summary>

There are no randomised trials of ERU treatment. The evidence comes from observational studies, some with many cases.

- **Gerding and Gilger (2016).** Retrospective study of 224 horses (338 eyes) with ERU at a US hospital. 24.1% were Appaloosas. At the first visit 28.4% of eyes were already blind. Leptospiral titres were positive in 40 of 88 horses tested (45.5%). The globe was lost in 12.1% of eyes. According to owners, 29.4% of horses did not return to their previous use and 14.9% were euthanised because of the disease. These are referral cases, probably more severe than average.
- **Dwyer et al. (1995).** Study of 372 horses in the USA. 56% of horses with uveitis were seropositive to *Leptospira* Pomona, versus 9% of horses without uveitis (OR 13.2). Seropositive horses with uveitis were more likely to go blind (59% versus 24%; OR 4.4), and Appaloosas were overrepresented (OR 8.3). Serology only shows exposure, not that leptospires are the cause.
- **Wollanke et al. (2001).** Cross-sectional study of 242 horses (270 eyes) undergoing vitrectomy in Germany and 39 controls. Leptospires were cultured from the vitreous in 120 of 229 horses with ERU (52%) and from no control eye. Only 76% of culture-positive horses had a vitreous titre at least 4 times higher than the serum titre. These were eyes selected for surgery at a single clinic.
- **Geiger et al. (2022).** Retrospective study of intraocular samples from 1387 eyes with ERU, 237 with other types of uveitis and 216 healthy eyes from the same clinic in Munich. In eyes with ERU, antibodies against leptospires were found in 83% (by MAT and by ELISA) and DNA by PCR in 72%. There were no antibodies in eyes with other uveitis or in healthy eyes. The eyes come from a referral centre for vitrectomy and may not represent ERU in other regions or breeds.
- **Sandmeyer et al. (2020).** Study of 145 Appaloosas in western Canada. 14% had ERU. Age (OR 1.15 per year) and the *LP/LP* genotype (OR 19.4 versus *lp/lp*) increased the risk. The sample is small and from a single region.
- **Gilger et al. (2010).** Cohort of 151 eyes of 133 horses from the USA and Europe with a suprachoroidal cyclosporine implant, followed for 13–85 months. At last follow-up 78.8% of eyes were visual, with a mean of 0.09 uveitis episodes per month. There was no control group.
