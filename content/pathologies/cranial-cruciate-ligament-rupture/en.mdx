## What it is

The cranial cruciate ligament (*ligamentum cruciatum craniale*) is one of the main stabilisers of the stifle. It prevents the tibia from moving cranially relative to the femur and limits internal rotation and hyperextension.

In the dog its rupture is rarely an isolated accident. The usual picture is a **degenerative disease**: the ligament progressively loses fibres and strength, suffers partial tears and finally ruptures completely during normal activity. Hence the term "cranial cruciate ligament disease".

Once ruptured, the tibia subluxates with every step, the medial meniscus becomes trapped and osteoarthritis develops.

## Who it affects

Middle-aged dogs of large breeds, although it can occur in any breed and size. Predisposed breeds include:

- Rottweiler
- Labrador
- Newfoundland
- American Staffordshire terrier
- boxer
- golden retriever

Obesity, neutering and a steeply sloped tibial plateau increase the risk.

A high proportion of dogs go on to rupture the ligament of the **other stifle** in the following years.

## Anatomy involved

### Cranial cruciate ligament

It arises from the axial face of the lateral femoral condyle and inserts on the cranial intercondylar area of the tibia. It has two bands:

- the craniomedial, taut in flexion and extension
- the caudolateral, taut only in extension

A partial rupture may involve only one of them.

### Tibial plateau

It slopes caudally. When weight is borne, compression of the femur on a sloped surface produces a shear force, **cranial tibial thrust**, which the ligament neutralises in the healthy stifle.

The **tibial plateau angle** is measured on radiographs. Values above the usual range are associated with higher risk.

### Medial meniscus

Its caudal horn is firmly attached to the tibia and is crushed by the femoral condyle when the tibia moves. "Bucket-handle" tears are typical.

## Clinical signs

Acute lameness after exercise, or intermittent and progressive lameness in partial ruptures. Also:

- difficulty rising
- sitting with the stifle turned outwards (positive "sit test")
- joint effusion
- fibrous thickening on the medial side of the stifle (*medial buttress*)
- thigh atrophy

A "click" on stifle flexion suggests a meniscal tear.

## Diagnosis

- The **cranial drawer test** shows cranial displacement of the tibia relative to the femur. It is tested in flexion and extension. In partial ruptures it may be subtle or absent.
- In the **tibial compression test** (tibial thrust), flexing the tarsus moves the tibia cranially.
- Radiographs show effusion (displacement of the infrapatellar fat pad), osteophytes on the trochlear ridges, patella and plateau, and tibial subluxation. They are used to measure the plateau angle and plan surgery.
- Arthroscopy or arthrotomy confirm the rupture and allow the meniscus to be treated.

## Treatment

In small dogs conservative management can be tried, but in most dogs surgery gives a better outcome.

- **Extracapsular techniques** (lateral fabellotibial suture) replace the function of the ligament with material outside the joint.
- **Osteotomies** change the biomechanics to cancel tibial thrust. The **TPLO** rotates the plateau until it is almost horizontal. The TTA advances the tibial tuberosity so that the patellar ligament lies perpendicular to the plateau.

With any technique the meniscus is inspected and the damaged portion resected.

Osteoarthritis progresses in every case, so weight and exercise control remain important after surgery.
