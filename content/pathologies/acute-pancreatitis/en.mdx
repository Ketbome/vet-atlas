---
title: Acute pancreatitis
summary: Inflammation of the exocrine pancreas caused by premature activation of digestive enzymes within the gland. It ranges from a mild, self-limiting illness to severe necrotising disease with systemic inflammation.
---

## What it is

In acute pancreatitis, trypsinogen is activated inside the acinar cells instead of in the intestinal lumen, and the activated enzymes digest the pancreas and the surrounding fat (autodigestion). Local inflammation, oedema and necrosis can spread to the peritoneum and trigger a systemic inflammatory response, with disseminated intravascular coagulation, acute kidney injury or respiratory complications in severe cases. Acute pancreatitis is potentially reversible, unlike chronic pancreatitis, which causes permanent fibrosis and loss of tissue. In most dogs no cause is found; risk factors include eating a fatty meal or dietary indiscretion, obesity, hypertriglyceridaemia, endocrine diseases and certain drugs.

## Who it affects

Mainly middle-aged and older dogs. Breeds with a reported higher risk include the miniature schnauzer (associated with hypertriglyceridaemia), Yorkshire terrier and other terriers, and cocker spaniel. Dogs with diabetes mellitus, hyperadrenocorticism or hypothyroidism are at increased risk.

## Anatomy involved

- **Pancreas**: the canine pancreas is V-shaped, with a right lobe in the mesoduodenum along the descending duodenum and a left lobe in the deep leaf of the greater omentum along the greater curvature of the stomach; the body lies at the pylorus. The right lobe is often the most affected.
- **Duodenum**: the pancreatic duct opens at the major duodenal papilla together with the bile duct, and the accessory pancreatic duct at the minor papilla; duodenal wall thickening and ileus are common.
- **Liver and extrahepatic bile ducts**: inflammation or fibrosis around the common bile duct can cause **extrahepatic biliary obstruction** and jaundice.

## Clinical signs

Anorexia, vomiting, lethargy and **cranial abdominal pain**, sometimes shown as the "praying position" (forelimbs down, hindquarters up). Diarrhoea, fever or hypothermia and dehydration. Severe cases show shock, jaundice, bleeding tendencies and dyspnoea. Mild cases may have only nonspecific inappetence.

## Diagnosis

- **Pancreas-specific lipase (canine pancreatic lipase immunoreactivity, cPLI, or validated lipase assays)**: the most sensitive and specific blood test; a normal result makes pancreatitis unlikely.
- **Abdominal ultrasound**: enlarged, hypoechoic pancreas surrounded by hyperechoic peripancreatic fat, local fluid and duodenal changes; it also excludes other causes of acute abdomen.
- **Blood work**: inflammatory leucogram, raised liver enzymes and bilirubin, azotaemia, electrolyte disorders, hypertriglyceridaemia; coagulation profile in severe cases.
- The diagnosis combines clinical signs, lipase results and imaging; histopathology is rarely performed.

## Treatment

Treatment is supportive: intravenous fluids, analgesia (often opioids), antiemetics (maropitant) and **early enteral nutrition**, which is preferred over prolonged fasting; a feeding tube is used if the dog does not eat. Severe cases may need plasma, management of coagulation and complications, and intensive care. Long-term, a low-fat diet is advised for dogs with recurrent pancreatitis or hypertriglyceridaemia. The prognosis is good in mild cases and guarded in severe necrotising forms.
