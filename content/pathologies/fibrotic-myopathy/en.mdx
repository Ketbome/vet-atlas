## What it is

Fibrotic myopathy is a chronic disorder of the caudal thigh muscles. Their fibres are replaced by dense collagenous tissue, most often in the **gracilis** and less often in the **semitendinosus**.

The fibrotic muscle cannot stretch. It restricts the movement of the hip, stifle and hock during the swing phase of the stride.

The cause is unknown. Proposed causes are:

- repetitive strain or trauma in active dogs
- immune-mediated mechanisms
- neurogenic causes

## Who it affects

Mainly **German shepherds**, usually active or working young adult males.

It has been reported in other breeds such as the Belgian Malinois, Rottweiler and Dobermann.

## Anatomy involved

The gracilis (*m. gracilis*) and the semitendinosus (*m. semitendinosus*) belong to the caudal thigh, or hamstring, muscles. Through the crural fascia they contribute to the common calcaneal tendon.

They are stretched when the stifle is extended with the hip flexed, so a fibrotic muscle reduces that range of movement.

When they become fibrous, a **firm band** can be palpated running from the proximal inner thigh to the caudomedial stifle.

## Clinical signs

The **gait is characteristic**. The stride of the pelvic limb is shortened and, at the end of the swing phase, there is:

- a rapid, elastic medial rotation of the paw
- external rotation of the hock
- internal rotation of the stifle

The condition is usually **not painful** and progresses slowly. It can be unilateral or bilateral; about half of cases are bilateral.

## Diagnosis

- The gait is observed and a taut, non-painful band is **palpated** in the gracilis or semitendinosus.
- Ultrasound or MRI shows the fibrotic segment.
- **Biopsy** confirms the replacement of muscle by connective tissue.
- Other orthopaedic and neurological causes of pelvic limb lameness must be ruled out.

## Treatment

Surgical excision or release of the fibrotic band improves the gait at first. But the gait abnormality **usually recurs within a few months**, so surgery alone is generally not recommended.

Rehabilitation, with stretching, massage and shockwave therapy, may help working dogs keep working. The evidence comes only from small case series.

Because the condition is not painful, many dogs live with it and continue moderate activity. The long-term outlook for normal gait is poor.
