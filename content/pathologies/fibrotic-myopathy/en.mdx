---
title: Fibrotic myopathy of the gracilis and semitendinosus
summary: Progressive replacement of the gracilis or semitendinosus muscle by fibrous tissue, which forms a taut band on the caudomedial thigh. It produces a characteristic non-painful gait abnormality, typically in German shepherds.
---

## What it is

Fibrotic myopathy is a chronic disorder in which muscle fibres of the caudal thigh muscles, most often the **gracilis** and less often the **semitendinosus**, are replaced by dense collagenous tissue. The fibrotic muscle cannot stretch, so it restricts the movement of the hip, stifle and hock during the swing phase of the stride. The cause is unknown; repetitive strain or trauma in active dogs, immune-mediated mechanisms and neurogenic causes have been proposed.

## Who it affects

Mainly **German shepherds**, usually active or working young adult males; it has been reported in other breeds such as the Belgian Malinois, Rottweiler and Dobermann.

## Anatomy involved

The **gracilis** (*m. gracilis*) and the **semitendinosus** (*m. semitendinosus*) belong to the caudal thigh ("hamstring") muscles and contribute, through the crural fascia, to the common calcaneal tendon. They are stretched when the stifle is extended with the hip flexed, so a fibrotic muscle reduces that range of movement. When they become fibrous, a firm band can be palpated running from the proximal inner thigh to the caudomedial stifle.

## Clinical signs

A **characteristic gait**: a shortened stride of the pelvic limb with, at the end of the swing phase, a rapid, elastic medial rotation of the paw, external rotation of the hock and internal rotation of the stifle. The condition is usually **not painful** and progresses slowly. It can be unilateral or bilateral (about half of cases).

## Diagnosis

- Gait observation and **palpation** of a taut, non-painful band in the gracilis or semitendinosus.
- **Ultrasound** or **MRI** to show the fibrotic segment; **biopsy** confirms the replacement of muscle by connective tissue.
- Rule out other orthopaedic and neurological causes of pelvic limb lameness.

## Treatment

Surgical excision or release of the fibrotic band improves the gait at first, but the gait abnormality **usually recurs within a few months**, so surgery alone is generally not recommended. Rehabilitation (stretching, massage, shockwave therapy) may help working dogs keep working, although the evidence comes only from small case series. Because the condition is not painful, many dogs live with it and continue moderate activity; the long-term outlook for normal gait is poor.
