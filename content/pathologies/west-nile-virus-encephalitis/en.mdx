## What it is

**West Nile virus** is a *Flavivirus* (family *Flaviviridae*) that circulates between wild **birds** and **mosquitoes**, mainly of the genus *Culex*.

Horses and people are infected through mosquito bites and are **dead-end hosts**. Viraemia is low and brief and not enough to infect new mosquitoes, so the horse does not transmit the virus to other horses or to people.

After the bite, the virus multiplies. In a minority of infected horses, 10–39% in the United States, it reaches the central nervous system and causes **encephalomyelitis**.

### Distribution

In Europe the virus circulates, and lineage 2 has spread there in recent years. Seroprevalence in horses is somewhat higher in the Mediterranean basin. In the Americas it spread across the continent from 1999.

It is notifiable in many countries. A case in a horse is a **sentinel** that infected mosquitoes are active in the area, which matters because the virus also infects people.

## Who it affects

**Unvaccinated** horses in areas where the virus circulates, in late summer and autumn, when mosquitoes are abundant.

Older horses are at greater risk of dying.

## Anatomy involved

The virus affects the brain, especially the **brainstem**, and the spinal cord.

There are usually no gross lesions. The brainstem is the preferred sample for PCR.

Brain involvement explains the cranial nerve deficits and behavioural changes. Spinal cord involvement explains the weakness, muscle tremors and paralysis.

## Clinical signs

- fever, not always present, depression and inappetence
- **ataxia** and weakness, often more marked in the pelvic limbs, which can progress to recumbency
- **muscle fasciculations** and tremors, among the most common signs
- behavioural changes, such as altered mental state or hyperexcitability
- hypersensitivity to touch, light or sound
- cranial nerve deficits, such as lip droop, difficulty swallowing and impaired vision
- teeth grinding

Most survivors recover completely, although convalescence can last weeks. Some keep neurological deficits for six months or more.

## Diagnosis

- Serum **IgM-capture ELISA** indicates recent infection and is the most widely used test in the live horse.
- Other antibody tests cannot distinguish vaccinated from naturally infected horses. Vaccination records are needed to interpret them.
- At necropsy, RT-PCR and immunohistochemistry are done on brain and spinal cord.

Other causes of acute neurological signs must be ruled out:

- **rabies**, whose signs can be indistinguishable
- equine herpesvirus 1 myeloencephalopathy
- other viral encephalitides
- equine protozoal myeloencephalitis

## Treatment and prevention

**There is no antiviral treatment.** Supportive care is given, with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and good nursing care, especially for recumbent horses.

### Vaccination

Vaccines are available, such as inactivated whole-virus vaccines. Two initial doses are given 3–4 weeks apart, then annual or semi-annual boosters depending on the area and the vaccine.

### Mosquito control

- removing standing water
- repellents
- fans and screens in stables

### Regulations

Drug use in horses is subject to each country's regulations and to **withdrawal periods**. In the EU the horse is a food-producing species. Its identification document (passport) states whether it is excluded from slaughter for human consumption, which determines which drugs may be given.

<summary>Key studies</summary>

- **Salazar et al. (2004).** Cross-sectional study of 484 equids with confirmed infection in Nebraska and Colorado, with data obtained from owners by telephone. 28.4% died or were euthanised (137 of 482). Unvaccinated animals were twice as likely to die as those vaccinated at least once, those recumbent and unable to rise 78 times more likely, and females 2.9 times more likely. 79.9% of survivors (271 of 339) recovered fully, in a mean of 22.3 days. The data depend on owners' recall.
- **Schuler et al. (2004).** Retrospective study of 569 affected horses in the 2002 North Dakota outbreak. 22% died. The odds of death in unvaccinated horses were 3 times those of horses given 1 or 2 doses and 16 times those of horses vaccinated according to the manufacturer's instructions. Recumbency, hind limb paresis and age over 5 years were associated with death. Vaccination status was unknown in 19% of cases.
- **Epp et al. (2007).** Case-control study of 300 equids on 46 premises during the 2003 Saskatchewan outbreak. 64% had antibodies from natural infection. Unvaccinated animals were 23 times more likely to become ill (95% CI 3.0–168.5), and estimated field vaccine efficacy was 96% (95% CI 67–99%). The confidence interval is very wide.
- **Bowen et al. (2014).** Randomised, blinded trial in 20 horses, given inactivated lineage 1 vaccine or no vaccine, followed by intrathecal challenge with a lineage 2 strain from Greece. 60% of controls had to be euthanised and none of the vaccinated horses. 80% of controls became viraemic versus a single vaccinated horse, and 8 of 10 vaccinated horses had no lesions. It is an experimental infection that does not reproduce the natural route.
- **Metz et al. (2021).** Systematic review and meta-analysis of 39 seroprevalence studies in 28,089 equids from 16 European countries (2001–2018). Pooled seroprevalence was 8% (95% CI 5–12%), slightly higher in Mediterranean countries. Heterogeneity between studies was very high (I² 99.3%), partly because many were carried out after outbreaks.
