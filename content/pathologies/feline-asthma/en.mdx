---
title: Feline asthma
summary: Chronic eosinophilic inflammation of the lower airways with reversible bronchoconstriction, of allergic origin. It causes cough and attacks of expiratory dyspnoea; it is controlled with inhaled or oral corticosteroids and bronchodilators.
---

## What it is

Feline asthma is an inflammatory disease of the bronchi caused by **type I hypersensitivity** to inhaled allergens (such as pollens, moulds and dust). The allergic immune response recruits **eosinophils** into the airway wall and causes:

- **Bronchoconstriction** from contraction of bronchial smooth muscle, reversible with bronchodilators.
- **Mucus hypersecretion** and thickening of the airway wall.
- Over time, airway **remodelling**: smooth muscle hyperplasia and fibrosis, which alter the airways persistently.

It is distinguished from feline **chronic bronchitis**, with neutrophilic inflammation, although both share signs and are often grouped as feline lower airway disease. Tobacco smoke, aerosol air fresheners and dust from some litters can worsen signs.

## Who it affects

Mainly young and middle-aged cats; sudden onset in older cats is very uncommon. The **Siamese** appears predisposed.

## Anatomy involved

- **Bronchi and bronchioles**: thickened walls infiltrated by eosinophils, with the lumen narrowed by smooth muscle spasm and mucus plugs. Air is trapped in the lungs during expiration.
- **Lungs**: hyperinflation; sometimes collapse of the **right middle lobe** when a mucus plug obstructs its bronchus.
- **Trachea**: inflammation of the tracheal and bronchial mucosa triggers coughing.

## Clinical signs

Chronic or intermittent **cough** (which owners may mistake for retching or vomiting), wheezing and tachypnoea. In **asthmatic attacks**: dyspnoea with **expiratory effort** (marked abdominal push on expiration) and, in severe cases, cyanosis. Between attacks many cats are normal.

## Diagnosis

- **Exclusion of other causes**: lungworms (*Aelurostrongylus abstrusus*, heartworm *Dirofilaria immitis* in endemic areas), infection, heart disease, neoplasia.
- **Thoracic radiographs**: **bronchial pattern** ("doughnuts" and "tram lines"), hyperinflation and sometimes right middle lobe collapse. They may be normal.
- **Bronchoalveolar or tracheal lavage**: inflammation with **eosinophil predominance**; culture, including for *Mycoplasma*.
- **Faecal examination** (Baermann technique) for lungworm larvae and heartworm tests depending on the region.
- **Computed tomography** in unclear cases. Blood tests are usually normal; peripheral eosinophilia is inconsistent.

## Treatment

In an **acute attack**: oxygen, a **bronchodilator** (injectable terbutaline or inhaled salbutamol) and a fast-acting **corticosteroid**. Long term, **corticosteroids** are the mainstay: oral prednisolone or **inhaled fluticasone** through a spacer chamber and mask designed for cats. Inhaled salbutamol is reserved for attacks; it should not be used alone long term, because it does not treat the inflammation. Environmental allergens and irritants are reduced. Allergen immunotherapy and other options are under study, with limited evidence. With treatment most cats do well, although severe attacks can be fatal.

## Key studies

### Diagnosis

- **Grotheer et al. (2020)**, retrospective study of 73 cats with asthma and 24 with chronic bronchitis, classified by bronchoalveolar lavage cytology. Cough (95% and 96%), abnormal lung sounds and dyspnoea were equally common, and radiographic lung patterns were abnormal in 94% and 91%. Only blood eosinophilia differed (40% versus 27%), so airway cytology is needed to tell the two apart.
- **Gadbois et al. (2009)**, radiographs of 40 cats with bronchial disease and 40 controls. A bronchial pattern was present in 37 cats and hyperinflation in 31, but five examiners reached a sensitivity of 71–89% and a specificity of 43–74%, with poor to good agreement between them. Radiographs support the diagnosis but cannot confirm or rule it out.
- **Chalifoux et al. (2021)**, case-control study of cats presented in respiratory distress, 54 with inflammatory lower airway disease and 121 with other causes. A history of cough (odds ratio 7.9) and an abdominal component to breathing (odds ratio 5.8) predicted airway disease, pleural effusion made it less likely, and venous blood gases did not help. Survival was 94% compared with 61% in the other cats.

### Treatment

- **Reinero et al. (2005)**, randomised crossover study in 6 cats with experimentally induced asthma. Oral prednisone and inhaled flunisolide reduced eosinophils in lavage fluid (5% and 2.5%, compared with 34% on the control treatment); no treatment changed airway hyperresponsiveness. An experimental model with very few cats.
- **Reinero et al. (2009)**, randomised crossover study in 6 healthy and 5 experimentally asthmatic cats. Racemic albuterol (salbutamol) inhaled twice daily for 2 weeks increased airway inflammation, including lavage eosinophils in the asthmatic cats, whereas the pure R-enantiomer did not. This supports the advice not to use salbutamol alone as maintenance treatment.
