## What it is

Feline asthma is an inflammatory disease of the bronchi caused by **type I hypersensitivity** to inhaled allergens, such as pollens, moulds and dust.

The allergic immune response recruits **eosinophils** into the airway wall and causes:

- bronchoconstriction from contraction of bronchial smooth muscle, reversible with bronchodilators
- mucus hypersecretion and thickening of the airway wall
- over time, airway remodelling, with smooth muscle hyperplasia and fibrosis that alter the airways persistently

It is distinguished from feline **chronic bronchitis**, with neutrophilic inflammation. Both share signs and are often grouped as feline lower airway disease.

Tobacco smoke, aerosol air fresheners and dust from some litters can worsen signs.

## Who it affects

Mainly young and middle-aged cats. Sudden onset in older cats is very uncommon.

The **Siamese** appears predisposed.

## Anatomy involved

The walls of the bronchi and bronchioles are thickened and infiltrated by eosinophils. The lumen is narrowed by smooth muscle spasm and mucus plugs, and air is trapped in the lungs during expiration.

The lungs are hyperinflated. Sometimes the **right middle lobe** collapses when a mucus plug obstructs its bronchus.

Inflammation of the tracheal and bronchial mucosa triggers coughing.

## Clinical signs

Chronic or intermittent **cough**, which owners may mistake for retching or vomiting. There is also wheezing and tachypnoea.

In asthmatic attacks there is dyspnoea with **expiratory effort**, with a marked abdominal push on expiration. Severe cases become cyanotic.

Between attacks many cats are normal.

## Diagnosis

Other causes must be excluded:

- lungworms: *Aelurostrongylus abstrusus* and, in endemic areas, heartworm (*Dirofilaria immitis*)
- infection
- heart disease
- neoplasia

The tests are:

- Thoracic radiographs, with a **bronchial pattern** ("doughnuts" and "tram lines"), hyperinflation and sometimes right middle lobe collapse. They may be normal.
- Bronchoalveolar or tracheal lavage, which shows inflammation with **eosinophil predominance**. Samples are cultured, including for *Mycoplasma*.
- Faecal examination with the Baermann technique for lungworm larvae, and heartworm tests depending on the region.
- Computed tomography in unclear cases.

Blood tests are usually normal, and peripheral eosinophilia is inconsistent.

## Treatment

In an acute attack, give:

- oxygen
- a **bronchodilator**: injectable terbutaline or inhaled salbutamol
- a fast-acting corticosteroid

Long term, **corticosteroids** are the mainstay: oral prednisolone or inhaled fluticasone through a spacer chamber and mask designed for cats.

Inhaled salbutamol is reserved for attacks. It should not be used alone long term, because it does not treat the inflammation.

Environmental allergens and irritants are reduced. Allergen immunotherapy and other options are under study, with limited evidence.

With treatment most cats do well, although severe attacks can be fatal.

<summary>Key studies</summary>

### Diagnosis

- **Grotheer et al. (2020).** Retrospective study of 73 cats with asthma and 24 with chronic bronchitis, classified by bronchoalveolar lavage cytology. Cough (95% and 96%), abnormal lung sounds and dyspnoea were equally common, and radiographic lung patterns were abnormal in 94% and 91%. Only blood eosinophilia differed (40% versus 27%), so airway cytology is needed to tell the two apart.
- **Gadbois et al. (2009).** Radiographs of 40 cats with bronchial disease and 40 controls. A bronchial pattern was present in 37 cats and hyperinflation in 31, but five examiners reached a sensitivity of 71–89% and a specificity of 43–74%, with poor to good agreement between them. Radiographs support the diagnosis but cannot confirm or rule it out.
- **Chalifoux et al. (2021).** Case-control study of cats presented in respiratory distress, 54 with inflammatory lower airway disease and 121 with other causes. A history of cough (odds ratio 7.9) and an abdominal component to breathing (odds ratio 5.8) predicted airway disease, pleural effusion made it less likely, and venous blood gases did not help. Survival was 94% compared with 61% in the other cats.

### Treatment

- **Reinero et al. (2005).** Randomised crossover study in 6 cats with experimentally induced asthma. Oral prednisone and inhaled flunisolide reduced eosinophils in lavage fluid (5% and 2.5%, compared with 34% on the control treatment); no treatment changed airway hyperresponsiveness. It is an experimental model with very few cats.
- **Reinero et al. (2009).** Randomised crossover study in 6 healthy and 5 experimentally asthmatic cats. Racemic albuterol (salbutamol) inhaled twice daily for 2 weeks increased airway inflammation, including lavage eosinophils in the asthmatic cats, whereas the pure R-enantiomer did not. This supports the advice not to use salbutamol alone as maintenance treatment.
