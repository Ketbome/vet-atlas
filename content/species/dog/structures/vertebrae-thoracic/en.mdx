## Overview

The dog has thirteen thoracic vertebrae (T1–T13), one for each pair of ribs.

Their bodies are short and their spinous processes are the tallest of the column. They form the skeleton of the withers.

They are the least mobile segment of the vertebral column because the ribs brace them.

## Key features

- **Body**. Short, with **costal foveae** (*foveae costales*) at its cranial and caudal ends. The head of each rib articulates with the caudal fovea of the vertebra in front and the cranial fovea of its own vertebra. The last ribs articulate with a single vertebra.
- **Transverse processes**. Short, each with a transverse costal fovea for the tubercle of the rib of the same number.
- **Mammillary and accessory processes**. They appear on the caudal thoracic vertebrae and continue into the lumbar series.
- **Intercapital ligaments**. They join the heads of each rib pair across the dorsal surface of the discs, from T2 to T10.

### Spinous processes

They are longest in the first thoracic vertebrae, then progressively shorter.

- Up to T10 they slope caudally.
- The **anticlinal vertebra**, usually T11, has an upright spinous process.
- Caudal to it the processes slope cranially, as in the lumbar region.

## Clinical relevance

- **Intervertebral disc herniation**. Rare between T1 and T10, where the intercapital ligaments reinforce the dorsal annulus. Frequent at the thoracolumbar junction (T11–L2), especially in chondrodystrophic breeds such as the dachshund.
- **Hemivertebrae**. Wedge-shaped malformed vertebrae. They occur mostly in the mid-thoracic region of screw-tailed breeds, such as the French bulldog, the pug and the English bulldog. They can cause kyphosis and spinal cord compression.
- **Anticlinal vertebra**. A radiographic landmark for counting the vertebrae.
