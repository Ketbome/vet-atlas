## Overview

The dog has seven cervical vertebrae. The atlas (C1), the axis (C2) and the seventh (C7) are distinctive. The third to sixth share a similar plan and are described together here.

They are the longest vertebrae of the column after the axis. Their length decreases slightly from C3 to C6.

## Key features

- **Body** (*corpus vertebrae*). Long, with a convex cranial end, the head, and a concave caudal end, the fossa. A crest runs along its ventral surface.
- **Vertebral arch and canal**. Wide. In the caudal part of the neck they house the cervical intumescence of the spinal cord.
- **Spinous processes**. Low in C3 and C4, becoming progressively taller towards C7.
- **Articular processes**. Large, with nearly horizontal articular surfaces that allow wide lateral and dorsoventral movement of the neck.

### Transverse processes

They are large and divided into dorsal and ventral parts.

- At their base lies the **transverse foramen** (*foramen transversarium*). The series of foramina forms the transverse canal for the vertebral artery and vein.
- In C6 the ventral part forms a broad plate, the **ventral lamina** (*lamina ventralis*), a landmark on radiographs.

## Clinical relevance

- **Cervical intervertebral disc disease**. Most frequent at C2–C3 and C3–C4 in small chondrodystrophic breeds, such as the dachshund and the beagle. It causes intense neck pain, often with few neurological deficits.
- **Cervical spondylomyelopathy**, or wobbler syndrome. Compression of the caudal cervical spinal cord (C5–C7) by malformed vertebrae, hypertrophied ligaments or disc protrusion. It affects large and giant breeds, such as the Dobermann and the Great Dane. It causes ataxia that is most obvious in the pelvic limbs.
