## Overview

The cat has seven cervical vertebrae.

The atlas (C1) and the axis (C2) are very distinctive. The third to the seventh share a similar plan and are described together here.

They form a short but very mobile neck, which the cat flexes and turns widely when grooming.

## Features

- **Body** (*corpus vertebrae*). It has a convex cranial end, the head, and a concave caudal end, the fossa. The intervertebral discs lie between the bodies.
- **Vertebral arch and canal**. Both are wide. In the caudal neck they house the cervical intumescence of the spinal cord, which gives rise to the nerves of the brachial plexus.
- **Spinous processes**. Low in C3 and C4, becoming progressively taller towards C7.
- **Transverse processes**. From C3 to C6 their base is pierced by the **transverse foramen**. The series of foramina forms the canal for the vertebral artery and vein. In C6 the ventral part of the process forms a broad plate, the **ventral lamina**, a landmark on lateral radiographs.
- **Seventh cervical (C7)**. The transition to the thorax. It has no transverse foramen, its spinous process is taller and the caudal edge of its body carries a costal fovea for the head of the first rib.
- **Articular processes**. Large, with nearly horizontal surfaces that allow wide lateral and dorsoventral movement.

## Clinical relevance

- **Hypervitaminosis A**. Cats fed liver-rich diets for months develop exostoses that fuse the cervical and cranial thoracic vertebrae. This is deforming cervical spondylosis, with neck stiffness and pain.
- **Intervertebral disc disease**. Much less common than in the dog. Cervical disc herniation is rare in the cat.
- **Ventroflexion of the neck**. In the cat it is usually caused by neuromuscular weakness, not by vertebral disease. Causes include hypokalaemia, thiamine deficiency and myasthenia.
- **Trauma**. Fractures and luxations after road accidents and falls.
