## Topography

The body and apex extend cranially along the abdominal floor depending on how full it is.

Unlike the dog's, the feline bladder is **abdominal even when empty**, and its neck is relatively long.

The ureters arrive on the dorsal surface and open at the trigone.

## Urethra

### Male

- after a long preprostatic urethra, it passes through the small prostate
- it runs along the pelvic floor
- beyond the bulbourethral glands, it continues as the **penile urethra**, the narrowest part

Urethral plugs and stones lodge in the penile urethra.

### Female

It is shorter and wider. It opens on the floor of the vestibule.

## Clinical relevance

### Feline idiopathic cystitis

The most common cause of lower urinary tract disease in young and middle-aged cats. It is linked to stress and indoor life.

It causes:

- pollakiuria
- stranguria
- haematuria
- urination outside the litter box

### Urethral obstruction

A typical emergency of male cats. It is caused by matrix-crystalline plugs, stones or urethral spasm.

It produces **hyperkalaemia**, bradycardia and azotaemia.

It is relieved by catheterisation and, if it recurs, by perineal urethrostomy.

### Urolithiasis

**Struvite** and **calcium oxalate** stones are the most common.

Feline struvite is usually sterile and can be dissolved by diet. Oxalate cannot.

### Cystocentesis

The feline bladder is easy to palpate and immobilise, which makes ultrasound-guided or blind puncture straightforward.

### Sacrocaudal luxation

Tail-pull injuries damage the pelvic and pudendal nerves and cause bladder atony.

The prognosis worsens if perineal sensation and anal tone are absent.
