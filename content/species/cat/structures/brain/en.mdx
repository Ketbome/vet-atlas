## Topography

The brain fills the cranial cavity. In the adult cat it weighs about 25–30 g.

- **Olfactory bulbs**. They sit in the ethmoidal fossa, rostral to the orbits. They are well developed, although proportionally smaller than in the dog.
- **Cerebral hemispheres**. They fill most of the vault.
- **Cerebellum**. It lies caudal to the hemispheres, in the caudal cranial fossa. The tentorium cerebelli separates it from the cerebrum, and its bony part (*tentorium cerebelli osseum*) is especially well developed in the cat.
- **Brainstem**. It rests on the floor of the skull and leaves through the *foramen magnum* towards the atlas.

## Parts

- **Cerebrum**. Two gyrencephalic hemispheres joined by the corpus callosum. It includes the olfactory bulbs and tracts. The cruciate sulcus (*sulcus cruciatus*) marks the motor cortex dorsally. Further caudally, the lateral gyrus holds the visual cortex, one of the most studied in neurophysiology.
- **Cerebellum**. Median vermis and lateral hemispheres. Coordinates posture and movement.
- **Brainstem** (*truncus encephali*). Midbrain, pons and medulla oblongata. Most cranial nerves arise from it.

## Clinical relevance

- **Feline infectious peritonitis** (FIP). Its neurological form is one of the most common causes of central nervous system disease in young cats. It causes pyogranulomatous meningoencephalitis, ependymitis and obstructive hydrocephalus.
- **Hepatic encephalopathy**. In cats with a portosystemic shunt it causes ptyalism, behavioural changes and seizures.
- **Meningioma**. The most common primary intracranial tumour of the cat, typical of older animals. It is usually well demarcated and can often be removed surgically.
- **Cerebellar hypoplasia**. A sequel of feline panleukopenia virus infection during gestation or the neonatal period. It causes non-progressive intention tremor and hypermetria.
- **Thiamine deficiency**. Seen with raw-fish or poorly formulated diets. It causes vestibular signs and ventroflexion of the neck.
