## Overview

The cat has **thirteen thoracic vertebrae** (T1–T13), one for each pair of ribs.

Together with the ribs and the sternum they form the rib cage.

The thoracic spine of the cat is very flexible. With the lumbar spine, it takes part in flexing and extending the back during galloping and jumping.

## Features

- **Bodies**. Short, with cranial and caudal **costal foveae**. The head of each rib articulates between two consecutive vertebrae, except in the last ones, where it rests on a single vertebra.
- **Transverse processes**. Short, each with a fovea for the tubercle of the rib.
- **Spinous processes**. Tall and sloping caudally in the cranial region; they get shorter caudally. In the caudal thoracic region, around T10–T11, lies the **anticlinal vertebra**, with a nearly vertical spinous process. Caudal to it the processes slope cranially, as in the lumbar vertebrae.
- **Mammillary and accessory processes**. They appear on the last thoracic vertebrae, in transition to the lumbar pattern. The epaxial muscles attach to them.
- **Vertebral canal**. Relatively narrow. It houses the thoracic spinal cord.

## Clinical relevance

- **Spinal lymphoma**. The most common tumour of the vertebral canal in the cat. It is usually extradural and causes paresis of the pelvic limbs.
- **Hypervitaminosis A**. The exostoses of deforming spondylosis extend from the neck to the first thoracic vertebrae.
- **Trauma**. Fractures and luxations after road accidents. The anticlinal vertebra and the rib count help to locate the lesion on radiographs.
- **Disc herniation**. Less common than in the dog, although it is described in older cats, mainly in the thoracolumbar region.
