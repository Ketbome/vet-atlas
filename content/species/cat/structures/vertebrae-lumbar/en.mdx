## Overview

The cat has **seven lumbar vertebrae** (L1–L7).

They are the largest vertebrae of the column. Their bodies lengthen caudally up to the mid-lumbar region.

The region is very flexible. Flexion and extension of the lumbar spine lengthen the stride at the gallop and power the jump.

## Features

- **Bodies**. Long and slightly flattened dorsoventrally.
- **Transverse processes**. Long, flat and directed cranioventrally. They give attachment to the hypaxial muscles, such as the psoas and quadratus lumborum, and to the aponeurosis of the abdominal muscles.
- **Spinous processes**. Lower and wider than the thoracic ones, sloping cranially.
- **Mammillary and accessory processes**. The mammillary processes sit on the cranial articular processes. The accessory processes project caudally from the pedicle and overlap the next vertebra. Both anchor bundles of the epaxial muscles.
- **L7**. Shorter, with a wide caudal end that articulates with the sacrum at the lumbosacral joint.
- **Spinal cord**. In the cat it ends further caudally than in the dog, at about L7. The dural sac can continue into the sacrum.

## Clinical relevance

- **Epidural anaesthesia**. The needle is placed in the lumbosacral space. Because the spinal cord and dural sac reach further back than in the dog, **accidental intrathecal injection** is more likely in the cat.
- **Disc disease and lumbosacral stenosis**. Less common than in the dog but described in older cats. They cause lumbar pain, reluctance to jump and pelvic limb deficits.
- **Spinal lymphoma and trauma**. Other causes of lumbar spinal cord injury. Trauma is usually a road accident.
