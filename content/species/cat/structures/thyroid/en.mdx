## Topography

Each lobe rests on the lateral surface of the trachea, caudal to the larynx and medial to the common carotid artery.

In the cat the two lobes are normally **not joined by an isthmus**.

Each lobe has two parathyroid glands:

- the **external** one, at the cranial pole
- the **internal** one, embedded in the thyroid tissue

Accessory or ectopic thyroid tissue can occur anywhere from the base of the tongue to the heart base.

## Clinical relevance

### Hyperthyroidism

It is the most common endocrinopathy of older cats. It is almost always due to adenomatous hyperplasia or a functional adenoma.

In most cases (≈70%) **both lobes** are affected. Carcinoma accounts for less than 3%.

It causes:

- weight loss with increased appetite
- tachycardia
- hypertension
- secondary cardiac hypertrophy

By raising the glomerular filtration rate, it can **mask chronic kidney disease**.

### Palpation

In a healthy cat the lobes are not palpable.

A thyroid nodule is sought by sliding the fingers along both sides of the trachea, from the larynx to the thoracic inlet. Enlarged, loosely attached lobes can descend towards the thoracic aperture.

### Ectopic tissue

Hyperfunctional accessory thyroid tissue, sometimes intrathoracic, explains some failures of thyroidectomy.

**Technetium-99m pertechnetate scintigraphy** locates all active tissue.

### Treatment

- methimazole or carbimazole
- an iodine-restricted diet
- **radioiodine** (I-131), the curative treatment of choice
- thyroidectomy, which must preserve at least one external parathyroid gland to avoid hypocalcaemia
