## Topography

### Aorta

- It leaves the left ventricle and forms the aortic arch, which gives the brachiocephalic trunk and the left subclavian artery.
- It descends ventral to the vertebral bodies, slightly left of the median plane.
- It crosses the diaphragm through the aortic hiatus, between the crura.
- In the abdomen it gives the coeliac, cranial mesenteric, renal, gonadal and caudal mesenteric arteries.
- At the last lumbar vertebrae it gives off the two external iliac arteries.
- It ends by dividing into the internal iliac arteries and the median sacral artery. This is the so-called **aortic trifurcation**.

### Caudal vena cava (*vena cava caudalis*)

- It is formed by the common iliac veins in the caudal lumbar region.
- It runs right of the aorta and receives the renal veins.
- It passes through the dorsal border of the liver and the caval foramen of the diaphragm.
- It opens into the right atrium.

## Clinical relevance

### Feline aortic thromboembolism

A thrombus formed in an enlarged left atrium, mostly from hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, lodges most often at the aortic trifurcation. This is the "saddle thrombus".

It causes acute, painful paralysis of the pelvic limbs, which are:

- cold
- without a femoral pulse
- with firm gastrocnemius muscles

### Femoral pulse

It is palpated on the medial thigh. Its **absence or asymmetry** is a key sign of thromboembolism.

### Systemic hypertension

It is common in older cats with chronic kidney disease or hyperthyroidism. It damages the retina, heart, kidney and brain.

Blood pressure is measured indirectly, with Doppler or oscillometry.

### Portosystemic shunts

In the cat they are usually **single and extrahepatic**, often arising from the left gastric vein. They carry portal blood into the caudal vena cava, bypassing the liver.

Ptyalism and, in cats without blue eyes, copper-coloured irises are typical.
