## Overview

The equine spleen (*lien*) is large and falciform: wide at the dorsal end and narrow at the ventral end.

Its capsule and trabeculae contain abundant **smooth muscle**, so it can contract strongly.

Its functions:

- it filters blood and removes aged red cells
- it takes part in the immune response
- it stores a large reserve of red cells, released during exercise or excitement

## Topography

- **Position**. In the left cranial abdomen, with its parietal surface against the diaphragm and the left costal wall.
- **Dorsal end or base**. Under the last left ribs, next to the left kidney and the blind sac of the stomach.
- **Ventral end or apex**. Narrow. It runs cranially and towards the median plane along the ventral abdominal wall. Its size varies greatly with the degree of contraction.
- **Ligaments**. The gastrosplenic ligament joins it to the greater curvature of the stomach and the **nephrosplenic ligament** to the left kidney.

The caudal border of the spleen can be palpated per rectum on the left side.

## Clinical relevance

- **Nephrosplenic entrapment**. The left large colon hangs over the nephrosplenic ligament, between the spleen and the kidney. On ultrasound, the gas-filled colon hides the left kidney.
- **Splenic contraction and PCV**. Adrenaline releases stored red cells and raises the packed cell volume. In an excited horse or after exercise the value does not reflect the resting state.
- **Splenomegaly**. Some sedatives and anaesthetics, such as acepromazine and barbiturates, relax and enlarge the spleen. It is also enlarged in equine infectious anaemia, lymphoma and other diseases.
- **Rupture and haematoma**. From trauma, such as kicks, or tumour infiltration. They cause abdominal haemorrhage.
- **Examination**. Ultrasound on the left side, through the last intercostal spaces and the flank, shows it clearly next to the left kidney.
