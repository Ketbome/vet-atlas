---
title: Biceps femoris
summary: Broad muscle on the lateral thigh; in the horse it is separate from the superficial gluteal and has a vertebral head that shapes the outline of the croup.
---

## Overview

It covers much of the lateral thigh, caudal to the femur. Unlike in ruminants, in the horse it is **not fused with the superficial gluteal**. It has two heads of origin: a **vertebral** head, which climbs to the sacrum and tail base and gives the croup its rounded outline, and a **pelvic** head from the ischial tuberosity. Distally it splits into several branches that end in a broad aponeurosis.

## Origin

- Vertebral head: spinous processes of the sacrum, broad sacrotuberous ligament, and gluteal and caudal fasciae.
- Pelvic head: ischial tuberosity (*tuber ischiadicum*).

## Insertion

Through an aponeurosis continuous with the fascia lata and crural fascia:

- Cranial branch: patella and lateral patellar ligament.
- Middle branch: tibial crest.
- Caudal branch: crural fascia and, through a tarsal tendon, the calcaneal tuber, as part of the common calcanean tendon.

## Action

Extends the hip and abducts the limb. The cranial branch extends the stifle and the caudal branches flex it; the tarsal tendon helps extend the hock. It is one of the main drivers of propulsion.

## Innervation

Caudal gluteal nerve (*n. gluteus caudalis*) for the vertebral head and branches of the sciatic nerve (*n. ischiadicus*).

## Clinical relevance

- The caudal thigh muscles are a **common intramuscular injection site** in the horse, especially in foals. The **sciatic nerve** runs deep to this muscle, so the needle is aimed into the muscle mass and not towards the femur.
- In **exertional rhabdomyolysis** ("tying-up") the large muscles of the croup and thigh become hard and painful; the horse is reluctant to move and creatine kinase activity rises.
